Eighty Years of Cases in Which the Prosecution Itself Became the Argument

Objective

The Persecution Ledger: Prosecutions Called Political or Religious in the Western Democracies, 1946–2026 — Special Report

Between 1946 and 2026, prosecutors in the Western democracies brought cases that someone with a name and a standing to be heard—a defense lawyer, a parliament, a United Nations body, a foreign government, the defendant’s own movement—publicly called political or religious persecution. This report is the ledger of those cases. It prints, for each one, the state’s case as the record holds it, the persecution claim with its makers named and its grounds stated, the answer the prosecutors and courts gave on the record, and the baseline against which the claim of exception can be weighed. The desk applies the words “political prisoner” and “persecution” to no one in its own voice. It records who applied them, to whom, and what the file shows. Forty entries follow, in six groups, preceded by the desk’s analysis of the shape the whole ledger takes: the statuses the labeled defendants actually hold, the legal instruments that recur, the bodies that do the labeling, and the two doors—appeal and clemency—by which the labeled cases have ended.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1400 ET, Monday, November 16, 2026.

The Ledger in Brief

The phenomenon is a claim about a prosecution, and the claim is the reportable fact. Every entry in this ledger exists because the characterization was made in public by an identifiable source: a UN Working Group that declared a detention arbitrary, a European Parliament resolution, an Amnesty International report on a trial’s fairness, a foreign head of government, a defense organization, a member of Congress, a defendant at his own sentencing. The desk’s rule for inclusion was that the claim be attributable and the case be a matter of court record. The desk’s rule for treatment was symmetry: each entry carries the same four parts, in the same order, at the same depth, whatever the politics of the accused.

The ledger runs the full width of the spectrum, and the instruments do not change with the defendant. The Espionage Act of 1917 supplies the whistleblower entries. Conspiracy statutes supply entries on the left in 1970 and on the right in 2023. Contempt of Congress imprisoned two presidential advisers in 2024; contempt of court imprisoned a British activist in 2018, 2019, and 2024. A Reconstruction-era civil-rights statute, 18 U.S.C. § 241, was used in 2023 against a man who posted voting memes. A Finnish hate-speech statute was used against a member of parliament who quoted Romans. The state’s toolkit, across eighty years and six groups, is short: process crimes, conspiracy, contempt, novel readings of old statutes, pretrial detention, and, at the edge of the criminal law, civil and administrative proceedings that carry the same practical weight.

The claims recur in the same shapes as the instruments. Five grounds appear again and again in the persecution claims filed below: a statute read more broadly than it had ever been read; a comparator who did the same thing and was not charged; a detention before trial longer or harder than the offense seemed to warrant; a sentence out of line with the statute’s ordinary run; and state misconduct documented in the state’s own records. The fifth ground is the only one that has ever been proved from inside the file itself—the Church Committee’s documentation of COINTELPRO, the wiretaps and break-in that ended the Ellsberg prosecution, the concealed informant who ended Geronimo Pratt’s. The other four are arguments, and the ledger prints the answers to them with the same care, because the answers are also record: jury verdicts, guilty pleas with admitted facts, appellate rulings, and judges of both appointing parties saying on the record that the prisoner before them was a prisoner for conduct.

The Status Axis: Who Is Actually in Prison

The label “political prisoner” is applied across four different legal conditions, and the conditions matter more than the label. The desk sorted every criminal entry in this ledger onto a status axis: (1) serving a sentence now; (2) imprisoned and released, by completion, commutation, or pardon; (3) convicted and never imprisoned; (4) charged and never convicted—acquitted, dismissed, reversed, fled, or still pending. Of the thirty-eight entries that carry a criminal status, two stand in the first condition, fourteen in the second, eight in the third, and twelve in the fourth. One entry, the January 6 prosecutions, is a class of more than fifteen hundred defendants that passed through the second and third conditions and was closed by clemency. One entry fits no box and is printed as the exception it is.

Condition one holds two men, and they could hardly be filed further apart. Mumia Abu-Jamal is serving life without parole in Pennsylvania for the 1981 murder of a Philadelphia police officer, a conviction Amnesty International criticized on fair-trial grounds in 2000 without taking a position on his guilt. Russell Brand is serving ten years and six months in England on a jury’s verdicts of November 5, 2026, for rape and sexual assault committed between 2004 and 2009. Brand’s supporters apply the political-prisoner label to a man a jury convicted on testimony, in a trial whose internal checks—acquittals on three of seven counts, a judge who refused the prosecution’s higher sentencing category—are also in the record. The label and the cell are both facts. The connection between them is the supporters’ claim, and it is printed as theirs in his entry, which cross-refers to the desk’s Special Report “Russell Brand, One Week After Sentence,” filed November 16, 2026.

Condition three holds the ledger’s most argued case. Donald Trump stands convicted of thirty-four felonies in New York. The sentence imposed on January 10, 2025, was an unconditional discharge: no imprisonment, no fine, no probation, no conditions. His supporters state, and the desk attributes the statement to them, that imprisonment was the design of the prosecutions and remains the intent held in reserve for the end of his presidency. The imposed sentence and the attributed claim are printed side by side in his entry. The desk adopts neither the word the supporters use for the prosecutions nor its denial; it prints the judgment, the sentence, the pending appeal argued on November 5, 2026, and the claim, each in its own name.

Condition four is the axis’s quiet rebuke to the label’s arithmetic. Twelve entries—Ellsberg, Snowden, Flynn, Edwards, Swartz, Davis, the Chicago Seven, Houck, Vaughan-Spruce, Mackey, the Tate brothers, Puigdemont—ended, or stand, without a conviction at all: dismissed for the government’s own misconduct, acquitted by juries, reversed by appellate courts, or never brought to trial. Several of those defendants were imprisoned before they were cleared—Angela Davis spent some sixteen months in jail awaiting the trial that acquitted her—and the axis records final status, not the road to it; the road is in the entries. The exception that fits no box is Assata Shakur: convicted in 1977, imprisoned, escaped in 1979, and never returned to an American cell; she died in Havana on September 25, 2025, still under a two-million-dollar reward.

The Instruments

Process crimes are the ledger’s most frequent charge. False statements, obstruction, and perjury—offenses committed inside an investigation rather than in the events investigated—carry the cases of Libby, Stewart, Stone, Flynn, and Papadopoulos, and appear inside the larger cases of Manafort and Manning. The claim made of them is constant across parties: that the state, unable or unwilling to charge the underlying conduct, charged the interview instead. The answer is also constant: the statements were material, recorded, and false, and juries or the defendants’ own pleas said so. The baseline is in the entries; process-crime sentences in this ledger run from fourteen days (Papadopoulos) to forty months imposed and never served (Stone).

Conspiracy is the second instrument, because it converts association into agreement. It charged the Chicago Seven under the Anti-Riot Act in 1969, the January 6 leadership under the seditious-conspiracy statute in 2022, and Douglass Mackey under § 241 in 2023. It is also the instrument appellate courts have most often cut back: the Seventh Circuit reversed the Chicago convictions in 1972, and the Second Circuit reversed Mackey’s in 2025 on the ground that the government had not proved an agreement existed at all.

Novel readings are the third instrument, and the courts themselves have policed them. The Supreme Court narrowed 18 U.S.C. § 1512(c)(2) in Fischer v. United States in June 2024, holding that a post-Enron evidence statute reaches evidence impairment and not every obstruction of a proceeding. A New York appellate court vacated the monetary penalty in the § 63(12) civil fraud case in August 2025 as an excessive fine while leaving the liability finding standing. The felony-elevation theory in the New York bookkeeping case—a misdemeanor raised to a felony by an uncharged federal election crime—awaits an appellate ruling. In each instance the check on the reading came from inside the system the reading was said to disgrace, and the ledger prints that sequence whole.

Contempt is the fourth instrument, and it is the bluntest. It requires no jury finding on an underlying offense. It imprisoned Bannon and Navarro for four months each in 2024 after the Justice Department had declined, across decades, to prosecute other officials referred for the same offense; it imprisoned Tommy Robinson in England for filming outside trials under reporting restrictions and for breaching an injunction; it jailed Chelsea Manning again in 2019 for refusing a grand jury’s questions after her sentence had been commuted. The persecution claim made of contempt is structural—the power is the punishment—and the answer is textual: the statutes and the court orders say what they say, and each defendant was told the consequence in advance.

The Designations, and the Exits

The labels come from a short list of labelers, and their authority varies by kind. The strongest designations in this ledger come from bodies with a method: the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention, which found Leonard Peltier’s detention arbitrary in 2022 under two of its categories; the UN Special Rapporteur on Torture, who reported on Julian Assange’s treatment; Amnesty International, which documented fair-trial failures in the Abu-Jamal proceedings; the European Parliament, which passed resolutions on Peltier in 1994 and 1999; the German and Belgian courts that refused Spain’s extradition requests for the Catalan defendants. The next tier is governments speaking for their own: foreign leaders who called Marine Le Pen’s conviction lawfare in April 2025, American officials who took up the British buffer-zone cases, a president who called the January 6 defendants hostages. The third tier is the movements themselves, whose claims the ledger prints with their names attached and their grounds stated, weighed at the weight a movement’s claim about itself can carry.

The exits from this ledger are two, and both are inside the constitutions that built the cells. The first exit is appeal, and it has worked for defendants of every politics: Davis acquitted, the Chicago Seven reversed, Pratt vacated after twenty-seven years, Mackey reversed with an acquittal ordered, Drake’s felony case collapsing on the eve of trial, Ellsberg dismissed for the government’s misconduct. The second exit is clemency, and it is the larger door: Carter’s pardon of the draft evaders in 1977 stands behind this ledger’s period as its template; Obama commuted Manning; Biden commuted Peltier to home confinement on his last full day in office; Trump pardoned or commuted Stone, Manafort, Flynn, and Papadopoulos, pardoned twenty-three FACE Act defendants including Bevelyn Beatty Williams in January 2025, and closed the January 6 docket the same week with fourteen commutations, a blanket pardon for the remaining 1,256 convicted defendants, and dismissal of the pending indictments. A pardon is a political act that ends a case its recipient calls political; the ledger records the dates and the instruments, and notes that clemency, unlike acquittal, decides nothing about the claim—it retires it.

What the ledger shows, in sum, is a recurring transaction. A state prosecutes a person whose prosecution is itself newsworthy; the person’s side names the prosecution persecution; outside bodies with methods sometimes agree in part and sometimes do not; the courts answer in verdicts, reversals, and sentences that frequently fall below what prosecutors asked; and the political branches, in the end, close by clemency many of the cases that politics opened. The desk’s cocked hat is the ledger itself. It follows, in six groups, each entry in the same four parts: the state’s case, the persecution claim, the answer, and the baseline.

I. Whistleblowers, Leakers, and Publishers

Daniel Ellsberg — United States, 1971–1973

Status axis (4): charged, never convicted. Ellsberg, a former Pentagon analyst, was indicted in 1971 under the Espionage Act, theft, and conspiracy statutes for copying the Pentagon Papers, a classified history of the Vietnam War, and giving them to newspapers. The indictment carried twelve counts and a stated exposure of 115 years.

The persecution claim. Ellsberg and his defenders described the prosecution as the government punishing the disclosure of its own documented deception of Congress and the public; the claim acquired its force from what the government did next, which is in the state’s own record.

The answer. There is no answer that survives the file. During the trial the court learned that White House operatives had burgled the office of Ellsberg’s psychiatrist seeking material to discredit him, and that the government had illegally wiretapped him and failed to disclose the recordings. On May 11, 1973, Judge Matthew Byrne dismissed all charges with prejudice, citing governmental misconduct. The dismissal is the state’s misconduct, established by the state’s own court.

The baseline. Ellsberg remains the benchmark Espionage Act leak case that never reached a verdict. The Act’s later use against leakers, below, proceeded without any court holding that the First Amendment barred it.

Chelsea Manning — United States, 2010–2020

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released. Manning, an Army intelligence analyst, was court-martialed at Fort Meade for transmitting some 700,000 classified documents and cables to WikiLeaks. On July 30, 2013, she was acquitted of aiding the enemy and convicted of twenty offenses, including six Espionage Act violations. She was sentenced on August 21, 2013, to thirty-five years.

The persecution claim. Her supporters and civil-liberties organizations called the sentence a punishment for exposing war crimes, pointing to the pretrial confinement at Quantico that the trial judge credited with 112 days of sentence reduction for its conditions, and to the UN Special Rapporteur on Torture’s reported conclusion that her treatment had been cruel and inhuman.

The answer. The government’s position was that Manning had indiscriminately dumped classified material without regard to the harm, that the court-martial afforded full process, and that the gravest charge, aiding the enemy, failed before the same tribunal that convicted her on the rest.

The baseline and the exit. Thirty-five years was the longest sentence ever imposed in an American leak case at that time. President Obama commuted the sentence on January 17, 2017; Manning was released on May 17, 2017, after about seven years. She was jailed again in 2019 for refusing to testify before a grand jury investigating WikiLeaks, and released in March 2020. The convictions stand.

Julian Assange — United States and United Kingdom, 2010–2024

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released. Assange, the founder of WikiLeaks, was indicted in the Eastern District of Virginia; a superseding indictment of May 23, 2019, charged eighteen counts, seventeen under the Espionage Act, carrying a stated exposure of 175 years, for obtaining and publishing classified military and diplomatic material in 2010. He spent seven years in Ecuador’s London embassy and, from April 2019, more than five years in Belmarsh Prison contesting extradition.

The persecution claim. The claim here carries outside designations: the UN Special Rapporteur on Torture, Nils Melzer, reported in May 2019 that Assange showed symptoms consistent with psychological torture from prolonged public persecution; press-freedom organizations and the governments of Mexico, Brazil, and Australia characterized the extradition effort as an attack on journalism. His defenders’ ground was that the indictment criminalized the ordinary acts of a publisher.

The answer. The Justice Department’s position was that Assange was charged as a hacker and a solicitor of classified material, not as a journalist, and that he had conspired with Manning to crack a password and to publish names of informants. British courts ruled on the extradition in stages, barring it on health grounds in January 2021 and later permitting the appeal process that ended in negotiation.

The baseline and the exit. No American publisher had previously been convicted under the Espionage Act for publishing. On June 26, 2024, in a federal courtroom on Saipan, Assange pleaded guilty to one count, conspiracy to obtain and willfully communicate national-defense documents under 18 U.S.C. § 793(g), and was sentenced to sixty-two months, credited entirely against his Belmarsh time. He flew home to Australia the same day. The felony conviction stands; the seventeen remaining counts were dismissed under the agreement.

Edward Snowden — United States, 2013–present

Status axis (4): charged, never tried. Snowden, a National Security Agency contractor, was charged by criminal complaint filed June 14, 2013, in the Eastern District of Virginia with theft of government property and two Espionage Act counts, under § 793(d) and § 798(a)(3), each carrying ten years. He has never been tried, pleaded, or sentenced.

The persecution claim. Amnesty International and the American Civil Liberties Union describe Snowden as a whistleblower prosecuted for revealing unlawful mass surveillance, and maintain that the Espionage Act affords him no public-interest defense at trial; a global pardon campaign ran in 2016. No pardon has issued.

The answer. The government’s position, stated by officials of both parties across three administrations, is that Snowden committed espionage, fled to adversaries, and caused grave damage to national security; the charges remain outstanding and are renewed as the official position whenever his name is raised.

The baseline. Snowden left the United States in June 2013 and has lived in Russia since, receiving asylum on August 1, 2013, and Russian citizenship by presidential decree on September 26, 2022. His is the ledger’s purest condition-four case: the prosecution exists entirely as an unexecuted charge, and the punishment his supporters describe is exile, imposed by geography rather than by a court.

Reality Winner — United States, 2017–2021

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released. Winner, an Air Force veteran and NSA contractor, was charged in the Southern District of Georgia with one count under 18 U.S.C. § 793(e) for mailing a classified report on Russian election interference to a news outlet. She pleaded guilty on June 26, 2018, and was sentenced on August 23, 2018, to sixty-three months in prison and three years of supervised release.

The persecution claim. Her family and supporters argued that a young veteran who disclosed evidence of an attack on an American election was punished out of proportion, and that her pretrial detention without bond—on the strength of her own recorded statements—was itself the sentence’s first installment.

The answer. Prosecutors stated that Winner had betrayed a clearance, that the plea was her own agreement, and that the sentence was negotiated. The Justice Department itself described the sixty-three months as the longest sentence ever imposed in federal court for a media leak—a characterization the department offered as a deterrent and her supporters quote as a confession.

The baseline. The comparison case is Manning’s thirty-five years, imposed by a court-martial rather than a federal court. Winner was released from prison in June 2021 on good-behavior credit. No pardon has been reported.

Thomas Drake — United States, 2010–2011

Status axis (3): convicted of a misdemeanor, never imprisoned; the felony case collapsed. Drake, a senior NSA official who had complained internally and to Congress about waste and surveillance, was indicted in the District of Maryland in 2010 on ten felony counts, five of them willful retention of national-defense information under § 793(e), with obstruction and false-statement counts, carrying a stated exposure of thirty-five years.

The persecution claim. Drake and the Government Accountability Project maintained that he was a whistleblower prosecuted for going to the Baltimore Sun after every internal channel failed; the claim rested on the thinness of the classified material, much of it marked unclassified or later declassified.

The answer. The government’s answer ended the case: on June 9, 2011, on the eve of trial, prosecutors dropped all ten felony counts. Drake pleaded guilty to one misdemeanor count of exceeding authorized use of a government computer. On July 15, 2011, Judge Richard Bennett sentenced him to one year of probation and 240 hours of community service, and said from the bench that the prosecution had been “unconscionable.”

The baseline. Drake is the Espionage Act’s modern collapse case: a ten-count felony indictment resolved as a computer-misuse misdemeanor, with the trial judge’s rebuke on the record.

Jeffrey Sterling — United States, 2010–2018

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released. Sterling, a former CIA officer, was indicted on December 22, 2010, in the Eastern District of Virginia for disclosing Operation Merlin, a program to feed flawed nuclear designs to Iran, to the journalist James Risen. A jury convicted him on January 26, 2015, on all nine counts, including Espionage Act violations. He was sentenced on May 11, 2015, to forty-two months.

The persecution claim. Sterling, who is Black and had sued the CIA for racial discrimination before his firing, argued through counsel and supporters that he was a whistleblower selected for prosecution while senior officials who leaked for advantage went uncharged; press-freedom groups filed on the case’s subpoena war over Risen’s sources.

The answer. The government’s case was circumstantial and metadata-based: call and email records placed Sterling in contact with Risen at the times of the disclosures. The Fourth Circuit upheld all but one count in June 2017. Sterling was released to a halfway house in January 2018.

The baseline. Forty-two months sits in the middle of the modern leak range, between Winner’s sixty-three and Kiriakou’s thirty, and far below the espionage-for-an-adversary sentences the same Act produces.

John Kiriakou — United States, 2012–2015

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released. Kiriakou, a former CIA officer who in 2007 publicly confirmed that the agency had waterboarded detainees, was indicted on April 5, 2012, on one count under the Intelligence Identities Protection Act, three Espionage Act counts, and one false-statement count, for disclosing a covert officer’s name to a reporter.

The persecution claim. His supporters’ ground was sequence: the only CIA officer imprisoned in connection with the torture program was the one who talked about it. The claim was taken up by whistleblower advocates and by the officers of the anti-torture movement generally.

The answer. In October 2012 Kiriakou pleaded guilty to the single IIPA count; the Espionage Act counts were dropped. He was sentenced on January 25, 2013, to thirty months and three years of supervised release. The government’s stated position was that identifying a covert officer is a crime whatever the identifier’s views on waterboarding.

The baseline. The plea structure—Espionage Act counts traded away for a single lesser count—recurs across this group (Drake, Assange) and is itself a datum: the Act’s maximum exposure is most often present at indictment and negotiated down by plea.

II. Elected Officials and Their Circles

Donald Trump — United States, 2023–present

Status axis (3): convicted, never imprisoned. Two New York cases anchor this entry. In the criminal case, People v. Trump, Manhattan, Trump was indicted on March 30, 2023, on thirty-four counts of falsifying business records in the first degree, N.Y. Penal Law § 175.10, for recording reimbursements to his lawyer Michael Cohen—who had paid $130,000 to Stephanie Clifford before the 2016 election—as legal expenses. A jury convicted on all thirty-four counts on May 30, 2024. On January 10, 2025, Justice Juan Merchan imposed an unconditional discharge under Penal Law § 65.20: no imprisonment, no fine, no probation, no conditions. The convictions stand as a final judgment. A state appeal was argued on November 5, 2026, and awaits decision; a federal-court removal effort was denied again on August 28, 2026. In the civil case, Attorney General Letitia James sued under Executive Law § 63(12); Justice Arthur Engoron’s February 2024 judgment ordered disgorgement of $354.9 million plus interest. On August 21, 2025, the Appellate Division vacated the monetary penalty as an excessive fine under the Eighth Amendment while leaving the liability finding and injunctive relief in place; both sides have sought review in the Court of Appeals.

The persecution claim. Trump and his supporters call the prosecutions lawfare and a witch hunt. The claim’s legal core is the felony-elevation theory: § 175.10 raises a misdemeanor to a felony when records are falsified to conceal another crime, and the jury was instructed on a state election-conspiracy predicate, § 17-152, whose unlawful means could rest on a federal campaign-finance violation with which Trump was never charged, without requiring jurors to agree unanimously on which means. Critics of the theory are not confined to his camp: Jed Shugerman of Boston University called the case a historic mistake in The New York Times; CNN analyst Elie Honig and Vox’s Ian Millhiser raised cognate objections. Supporters state further that imprisonment was the prosecutions’ design and remains the intent reserved for after his presidency; that statement is theirs, and the desk prints it as theirs.

The answer. District Attorney Alvin Bragg’s office answers that § 175.10 is a routine New York statute, that the records were falsified to keep voters from learning of the payment, and that the instructions followed state law on means and elements. Judge Merchan stated at sentencing that the unconditional discharge was the only lawful sentence that preserved the verdict without interfering with the presidency. The Appellate Division has not ruled; the preemption and unanimity questions are open, not resolved, in either direction.

The baseline. Section 175.10 is prosecuted commonly in New York, ordinarily on embezzlement, tax, and insurance predicates. Its use on an uncharged federal predicate, with § 17-152 as the sole other crime, was described even by analysts sympathetic to the prosecution as without direct precedent. The common statute and the uncommon predicate are both the baseline.

Roger Stone — United States, 2019–2020

Status axis (3): convicted, never imprisoned. Stone was convicted on November 15, 2019, in the District of Columbia on seven counts—obstruction of an official proceeding, five counts of false statements to Congress, and witness tampering—arising from his testimony about WikiLeaks contacts and his pressure on an associate not to contradict him. On February 20, 2020, Judge Amy Berman Jackson sentenced him to forty months.

The persecution claim. Stone and the President called the case a Mueller witch hunt, citing the foreperson’s social-media history and the pre-dawn FBI arrest at his Florida home as theater.

The answer. The jury heard the messages and the associate’s testimony. The sentencing record then cut both ways: after a presidential tweet attacked the original 87-to-108-month recommendation, Justice Department leadership filed a softer memorandum, all four trial prosecutors withdrew, and one resigned. The department’s Inspector General documented the intervention in a July 2024 report, without finding that Attorney General Barr acted at the President’s direct order. Judge Jackson imposed forty months, below the original recommendation.

The baseline and the exit. Stone served no time. President Trump commuted the sentence on July 10, 2020, before his surrender date, and pardoned him fully on December 23, 2020.

Paul Manafort — United States, 2018–2020

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released. Manafort, Trump’s 2016 campaign chairman, was convicted in the Eastern District of Virginia on August 21, 2018, on eight counts of tax fraud, bank fraud, and failure to file foreign-account reports; the jury hung on ten counts. He then pleaded guilty in the District of Columbia to conspiracy against the United States—unregistered lobbying for Ukraine, money laundering, tax offenses—and conspiracy to obstruct justice. His combined effective sentence was ninety months.

The persecution claim. His allies argued that a lobbyist’s decade-old finances were prosecuted because of the campaign he ran, as leverage against the President.

The answer. The trial record was documentary: offshore accounts, false loan applications, and some $60 million in Ukrainian payments. Judge Amy Berman Jackson later found that Manafort had lied to investigators after pleading guilty, breaching his cooperation agreement.

The baseline and the exit. Manafort served about two years, including jail from June 2018 after his release was revoked for witness tampering, and was moved to home confinement in May 2020 on COVID grounds. President Trump pardoned him fully on December 23, 2020. A parallel New York state case was dismissed on double-jeopardy grounds.

Michael Flynn — United States, 2017–2020

Status axis (4): pleaded guilty; no judgment of conviction entered. Flynn, the incoming National Security Advisor, pleaded guilty on December 1, 2017, to one false-statement count under 18 U.S.C. § 1001, for denying to FBI agents that he had discussed sanctions with the Russian ambassador. He was never sentenced.

The persecution claim. Flynn, having withdrawn his plea effort in 2020, and his supporters argued the interview was a perjury trap laid without a legitimate investigative basis, citing internal FBI documents released in litigation.

The answer. The Justice Department under Attorney General Barr moved on May 7, 2020, to dismiss the case, stating the interview was not material to a legitimate investigation. Judge Emmet Sullivan appointed an outside adviser and had not ruled when, on November 25, 2020, President Trump pardoned Flynn fully. Judge Sullivan dismissed the case as moot on December 8, 2020, noting that a pardon is not a finding of innocence.

The baseline. Flynn’s is the ledger’s clearest instance of the executive branch answering the persecution claim from inside the prosecution itself: the department disowned its own case before the pardon retired it.

Steve Bannon — United States, 2022–2026

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released. Bannon was convicted on July 22, 2022, of two counts of contempt of Congress under 2 U.S.C. § 192 for defying a subpoena from the House January 6 Committee, and sentenced to four months and a $6,500 fine. He served the term at FCI Danbury from July 1 to October 29, 2024. Separately, in New York state court, he pleaded guilty on February 11, 2025, to scheme to defraud in the “We Build the Wall” matter and received a three-year conditional discharge.

The persecution claim. Bannon and his counsel argued that he had relied on executive-privilege assertions and on advice of counsel, and that the Justice Department departed from its own history by prosecuting a former official for contempt.

The answer. The department’s stated distinction, accepted by the courts, was that Bannon was a private citizen at the time of the subpoena, produced nothing, and appeared nowhere; the trial judge excluded the advice-of-counsel defense for want of evidence that counsel had told him the law permitted total defiance.

The baseline. For decades the department declined to prosecute contempt referrals against sitting executive officials asserting privilege—the Holder and Lerner referrals among them. Bannon and Navarro, below, are the modern exceptions, and each received four months. In April 2026 the Supreme Court vacated and remanded Bannon’s case for reconsideration in light of an intervening decision; the final disposition of the contempt conviction was not verified in the record before the desk at filing.

Peter Navarro — United States, 2023–2026

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released. Navarro was convicted on September 7, 2023, of two counts of contempt of Congress for defying the January 6 Committee’s subpoena, and sentenced on January 25, 2024, to four months and a $9,500 fine. He served the term from March to July 2024, the first former White House official imprisoned for criminal contempt of Congress.

The persecution claim. Navarro’s ground was executive privilege: he maintained the former President had invoked it and that a subordinate could not waive it.

The answer. The courts found no evidence that a privilege invocation covered his subpoena; the D.C. Circuit affirmed his conviction on July 21, 2026, and denied rehearing en banc on August 28, 2026. No clemency has been found in the record.

The baseline. Navarro’s case is the control case for Bannon’s: same statute, same committee, same sentence, opposite party of the appointing president across the two prosecutions’ critics—and, unlike Bannon’s, his conviction has so far survived every appeal.

George Papadopoulos — United States, 2017–2020

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released. Papadopoulos, a 2016 campaign foreign-policy adviser, pleaded guilty on October 5, 2017, to one false-statement count for lying to FBI agents about the timing of his contacts with a professor who claimed Russian connections. He was sentenced on September 7, 2018, to fourteen days, and served twelve.

The persecution claim. He and his supporters described the case as the manufactured predicate of the Russia investigation—a young adviser’s misdated conversation converted into the investigation’s first conviction.

The answer. Prosecutors stated that the lies impeded the investigation at its most time-sensitive stage; the sentencing judge imposed a term far below the guidelines after Papadopoulos expressed remorse.

The baseline and the exit. Fourteen days is the shortest custodial sentence in this ledger. President Trump pardoned Papadopoulos fully on December 22, 2020.

Dinesh D’Souza — United States, 2014–2018

Status axis (3): convicted, never in prison. D’Souza pleaded guilty on May 20, 2014, to making $20,000 in campaign contributions through straw donors, a felony under the Federal Election Campaign Act. He was sentenced to five years of probation, with eight months in a community confinement center, and a $30,000 fine.

The persecution claim. D’Souza argued that felony prosecution of a $20,000 straw-donor offense—of a kind commonly resolved in civil enforcement—was selective retaliation for his anti-Obama films. The comparison dataset behind the word “commonly” was not verified to the desk’s standard, and the claim is printed as his ground, not as an established pattern.

The answer. Prosecutors in the Southern District of New York answered that the amount, the use of two straw donors, and the effort to conceal made the case a standard criminal referral; the sentencing judge rejected the selective-prosecution motion on the record.

The baseline and the exit. President Trump pardoned D’Souza on May 31, 2018; the White House statement said he had been “a victim of selective prosecution.” The pardon statement is the rare instance of a clemency instrument adopting the persecution claim in its own words.

John Edwards — United States, 2011–2012

Status axis (4): charged, never convicted. Edwards, the former senator and 2004 Democratic vice-presidential nominee, was indicted on June 3, 2011, on six counts alleging that some $1 million from two donors, used to conceal his mistress during the 2008 campaign, constituted illegal campaign contributions.

The persecution claim. Legal analysts across the spectrum criticized the theory before any verdict: the payments were not reported as contributions by anyone, the Federal Election Commission had not treated such payments as contributions, and former FEC commissioners and Justice Department veterans called the reading of the statute a stretch. Edwards’s own claim was that the prosecution criminalized a private failing.

The answer. The system’s answer is the verdict. On May 31, 2012, a jury acquitted Edwards on one count and hung on the other five; on June 13, 2012, the Justice Department dismissed the remaining counts. Assistant Attorney General Lanny Breuer’s statement closed the file. The theory was tested before a jury and did not survive it.

The baseline. Edwards is the ledger’s standing exhibit that a novel campaign-finance theory can be brought against a figure of the governing party’s own establishment—and that the jury box is where such theories go to be answered.

I. Lewis “Scooter” Libby — United States, 2007–2018

Status axis (3): convicted, never imprisoned. Libby, chief of staff to Vice President Cheney, was convicted on March 6, 2007, on four of five counts—perjury, obstruction of justice, and false statements—in the investigation of the leak of CIA officer Valerie Plame’s identity. He was never charged with the leak itself; the source of the original disclosure was Deputy Secretary of State Richard Armitage. On June 5, 2007, he was sentenced to thirty months and a $250,000 fine.

The persecution claim. Libby’s defenders argued that a special prosecutor who knew the leaker’s identity within days prosecuted the process instead—the canonical form of the process-crime claim, made on behalf of a Republican by Republicans.

The answer. Prosecutor Patrick Fitzgerald’s position was that Libby’s lies obstructed the investigation of a serious breach, whoever committed it; the jury convicted on the testimony of journalists to whom Libby had spoken.

The baseline and the exits. Libby served no time. President George W. Bush commuted the prison term on July 2, 2007, leaving the conviction and fine; President Trump pardoned him fully on April 13, 2018. Two presidents of his own party used two different clemency instruments on the same conviction, eleven years apart.

Martha Stewart — United States, 2004–2005

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released. Stewart was convicted on March 5, 2004, of conspiracy, obstruction of an agency proceeding, and two counts of false statements to investigators examining her December 2001 sale of ImClone shares. She was not charged with insider trading; the government’s insider-trading theory was never put to her jury, a distinction the entry prints because the popular account of her case routinely omits it. She was sentenced on July 16, 2004, to five months in prison and five months of home confinement, and served the prison term at Alderson, West Virginia, from October 2004 to March 2005. A civil SEC settlement followed in 2006.

The persecution claim. Stewart argued selective prosecution in her own filings—that she was charged for her statements because the underlying offense could not be proved. The desk located her own argument and the proportionality criticism it attracted; it did not locate a second named-analyst persecution claim of the kind the other entries carry, and prints the entry on her claim and the record.

The answer. Prosecutors answered that the false statements were made to federal investigators in a formal interview and that lying to investigators is a crime with its own victim, the investigation itself.

The baseline. Five months sits at the low end of this ledger’s process-crime sentences, and Stewart received no clemency. Her case is the group’s reminder that the process-crime instrument predates the current era’s arguments about it.

Aaron Swartz — United States, 2011–2013

Status axis (4): charged, never tried. Swartz, a programmer and activist, was indicted in 2011 for downloading millions of academic articles from the JSTOR archive through an MIT network closet. A superseding indictment in September 2012 brought the counts to thirteen under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and wire-fraud statutes. The government’s stated exposure for the original charges was thirty-five years and a $1 million fine; scholarly calculations of the superseding indictment’s stacked maximum run higher, and the desk prints no single figure for it.

The persecution claim. The overreach critique came from named legal scholars: Lawrence Lessig, Orin Kerr, and former federal judge Nancy Gertner, among others, argued that the statute’s breadth let prosecutors convert a terms-of-service violation into decades of exposure, and that the exposure was the coercion.

The answer. United States Attorney Carmen Ortiz defended the prosecution on January 16, 2013, stating that her office had sought a sentence in the range of six months and had never pressed the statutory maximum; the charges, she said, fit the conduct of a man who had taken millions of documents he had no right to take.

The record’s end. Swartz died by suicide on January 11, 2013, before trial. The charges were dismissed. The desk prints the sequence plainly because both the claim and the answer in this case are arguments about a prosecution that no verdict ever tested.

III. Mass-Event Defendants

The January 6 Prosecutions — United States, 2021–2025

Status axis: a class entry. The class passed through conditions (2) and (3) and was closed by clemency on January 20, 2025; no defendant remains in prison on a January 6 conviction under that instrument. The Justice Department’s final pre-clemency count, as of January 6, 2025, was approximately 1,583 defendants charged and 1,270 convicted—1,009 by guilty plea, 221 at contested trials, and 40 on stipulated facts. NPR’s independent database counted 1,575 charged and the same 1,270 convicted; the desk prints both tallies because they differ in how they file the stipulated-facts cases. About 608 defendants were charged with assaulting or impeding police, 174 of them with a weapon or serious injury. Eighteen were charged with seditious conspiracy and fourteen convicted. No defendant was charged under the insurrection statute, 18 U.S.C. § 2383.

The named instances. Stewart Rhodes, founder of the Oath Keepers, was convicted of seditious conspiracy at trial and sentenced on May 25, 2023, by Judge Amit Mehta to eighteen years; at sentencing Rhodes called himself a political prisoner. Enrique Tarrio, the Proud Boys’ chairman, who was not in Washington that day, was convicted of seditious conspiracy and sentenced on September 5, 2023, by Judge Timothy Kelly—an appointee of President Trump—to twenty-two years, the longest sentence of the class. Jacob Chansley pleaded guilty to obstruction of an official proceeding and received forty-one months from Judge Royce Lamberth; he completed the custodial term in May 2023.

The persecution claim. President Trump called the jailed defendants “hostages” through 2024 and at the clemency signing itself. Representatives Greene, Gaetz, Gohmert, and Gosar carried the “political prisoners” framing from 2021; the journalist Julie Kelly stated the claim in her own words: “I call it persecution.” The stated grounds are three. First, pretrial detention: most defendants were released pending trial, but about forty were held in the District’s Correctional Treatment Facility in late 2021, and a December 2021 report by members of Congress and an October 2022 letter signed by thirty-four detainees described restrictive housing, filthy cells, and denial of legal access. Second, disparate treatment: the claim compares the prosecutions with the summer 2020 unrest cases. Third, charging reach: the use of 18 U.S.C. § 1512(c)(2), a post-Enron evidence statute carrying twenty years, against some 355 defendants.

The answer. United States Attorney Matthew Graves stated the prosecution’s position in September 2024: “No one is being prosecuted for their views. They’re being prosecuted for their acts.” The Marshals Service’s October 2021 inspection found the facility housing the January 6 detainees “largely appropriate and consistent with federal prisoner detention standards,” while finding the District’s main jail, which did not house them, failed federal standards and emptying it of federal detainees; the same month, Judge Lamberth held the jail’s warden in civil contempt for failing one January 6 detainee’s medical care. The Associated Press’s August 2021 review of more than 300 federal cases from the 2020 unrest found more than 120 convictions and an average sentence of about twenty-seven months among the first seventy sentenced, with about sixty of one hundred Portland cases dismissed; no controlled like-for-like sentencing study of the two full dockets exists in the record before the desk, and the disparity claim is printed as unproved in either direction. Judges appointed by presidents of both parties imposed the sentences and, in the Washington Post’s analysis of the first 357 sentencings, went below prosecutors’ recommendations in roughly three-quarters of cases. Three judges answered the label on the record: Judge Mehta told a defendant, “You are not a political prisoner. You’re not Alexei Navalny”; Judge Lamberth wrote in February 2024 that in thirty-seven years on the bench he could not recall “such meritless justifications of criminal activity” going mainstream; Judge Chutkan said of the jailed defendants, “They’re being kept there because they are dangerous people.”

The baseline. The Supreme Court supplied the baseline correction on the charging-reach ground: in Fischer v. United States, decided June 28, 2024, by 6–3, the Court held that § 1512(c)(2) requires proof that the defendant impaired the availability or integrity of records, documents, or objects for use in an official proceeding. The Justice Department’s own fact sheet that day counted 249 potentially affected cases, noted that no defendant had been charged only under § 1512, and identified fifty-two defendants for whom it was the sole felony of conviction, twenty-seven of them then in prison. By November 2024 the department had dropped the count for about ninety-six pending defendants and did not oppose vacatur in about fifty-four decided cases.

The exit. Proclamation 10887, signed January 20, 2025, did three distinct things that wire copy often blends. It commuted the sentences of fourteen named defendants—Rhodes and nine other Oath Keepers and Proud Boys figures among them—to time served, leaving those convictions standing at the time. It granted “a full, complete and unconditional pardon” to all other convicted defendants, 1,256 people on the department’s convicted total; Tarrio and Chansley were pardoned under this clause, a distinction the record draws and many summaries miss. And it directed dismissal with prejudice of the roughly three hundred pending indictments—dismissal, not pardon, in the instrument’s own terms. Its stated rationale, in full, is one sentence: “This proclamation ends a grave national injustice that has been perpetrated upon the American people over the last four years and begins a process of national reconciliation.” Postscript from the later record: in 2026, at the Justice Department’s own request, the D.C. Circuit vacated Rhodes’s conviction and the district court dismissed his indictment in August 2026; a complete accounting of the fourteen commutations’ present status was not verified to the desk’s standard and is not stated.

IV. Religious and Conscience Cases

Päivi Räsänen — Finland, 2019–2026

Status axis (3): convicted, never imprisoned. Räsänen, a member of Finland’s parliament and a former interior minister, was charged with ethnic agitation under chapter 11, § 10 of the Finnish Criminal Code for three items: a 2019 tweet quoting Romans 1:24–27 in criticism of her church’s sponsorship of a Pride event, a 2019 radio interview, and a 2004 church booklet on sexuality that remained available online. She was acquitted by the Helsinki District Court in 2022 and by the Court of Appeal in 2023. The prosecution appealed to the Supreme Court, which ruled on March 26, 2026 (KKO:2026:27): acquittal on the tweet, and, by 3–2, conviction on the booklet count for keeping it available—fines of €1,800 for Räsänen, €1,100 for Bishop Juhana Pohjola, who was tried with her, and €5,000 for the Luther Foundation, with a removal order. Applications to the European Court of Human Rights were filed in July 2026 and await ruling.

The persecution claim. ADF International, United States lawmakers, and religious-freedom organizations call the case the prosecution of a Christian for quoting the Bible—a seven-year proceeding over a tweet and a booklet, ending in a conviction for a text written in 2004. The stronger version of the claim, that she was convicted under a law that did not exist in 2004, is advocacy framing: the court’s holding rests on the booklet’s continued online availability after 2019, and the desk prints the distinction.

The answer. The Finnish prosecution’s position throughout was that the case concerned agitation against a protected group under a statute applied to speakers of every politics, not the quotation of scripture; the Supreme Court’s split judgment—acquitting on the tweet, convicting on the booklet—is the system’s own record of where it drew the line.

The baseline. Ethnic-agitation prosecutions in Finland are ordinarily brought against online speech and ordinarily end in day-fines. Räsänen’s fines sit inside that range; the proceeding’s length and its defendant’s office are what stand outside it.

Isabel Vaughan-Spruce — United Kingdom, 2022–present

Status axis (4): charged, never convicted; a second case is pending. Vaughan-Spruce, a charity director, was arrested in Birmingham in December 2022 for silently praying inside an abortion-clinic buffer zone established by a local Public Spaces Protection Order. On February 16, 2023, the prosecution offered no evidence and she was acquitted. She was arrested again in March 2023 and not charged. In August 2024 West Midlands Police settled with her for £13,000. The phases must not be merged: the first proceeding ended in acquittal and a settlement, not a conviction.

The new case. On December 17, 2025, she was charged under § 9 of the Public Order Act 2023—the first charge under the national buffer-zone statute—with five substituted counts filed on October 7, 2026. She has pleaded not guilty. Trial is set for January 25–29, 2027. The occasions underlying the counts are described inconsistently in the sources before the desk and are not stated here.

The persecution claim. ADF International, members of the United States Congress, and religious-freedom bodies cite her arrests as the policing of silent prayer—of thought itself. The answer, from the Crown Prosecution Service and the councils, is that the zones regulate conduct within a defined area around clinics, that the statute reaches influence and obstruction rather than belief, and that the first case’s collapse is itself evidence the system corrects. The 2027 trial will supply the next entry in that argument.

The baseline. Her case and the next one are routinely filed together. They are opposites on the axis, and the desk prints them apart for that reason.

Adam Smith-Connor — United Kingdom, 2024

Status axis (3): convicted, never imprisoned. Smith-Connor, an Army veteran, was convicted on October 16, 2024, at Poole Magistrates’ Court of failing to comply with the Bournemouth council’s Public Spaces Protection Order, for silently praying inside the clinic buffer zone and refusing to leave it. He received a two-year conditional discharge and £9,000 in costs.

The persecution claim. His case drew the same designation as Vaughan-Spruce’s—persecution for silent prayer—from ADF International and from American officials who cited it in 2025 statements on European speech.

The answer. The distinction the court drew is the record: unlike Vaughan-Spruce, Smith-Connor was tried, the court made findings—including his refusal to move when directed—and convicted. The prosecution’s position is that the offense is the breach of the order within the zone, whatever the content of the thought. An appeal was reported; its outcome is not established in the record before the desk, and the conviction is printed as it stands.

The baseline. A conditional discharge is the lowest disposal available to the court short of an absolute discharge. The sentence’s weight in this case is the costs order and the conviction itself, which is what the claim is made of.

Mark Houck — United States, 2022–2023

Status axis (4): charged, never convicted. Houck, a Catholic street counselor, was indicted on September 20, 2022, in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania on two counts under the FACE Act, for an October 13, 2021 altercation outside a Philadelphia clinic in which he twice shoved Bruce Love, a clinic escort, who fell. The arrest, at Houck’s home in September 2022, is described in two accounts the desk does not merge: his family’s, of a large armed entry that terrified his children, and the FBI’s, of a standard arrest by agents in ordinary numbers. The figures each side gives for the agents present do not reconcile and neither is printed as fact.

The persecution claim. Houck’s counsel and members of Congress called the case the selective deployment of a federal felony against a pro-life father for a pushing incident that local authorities had declined to prosecute as a felony.

The answer. A jury acquitted Houck on January 30, 2023. The Justice Department’s position had been that the FACE Act reaches force used to injure or intimidate a person obtaining or providing clinic access, whatever the user’s cause. Houck’s later civil suit against the department was dismissed in March 2025 and settled in April 2026 for a seven-figure sum; the sources before the desk give the figure as both $1 million and $1.1 million, and the desk prints the range as unresolved.

The baseline. See the FACE baseline after the Williams entry: the statute’s enforcement runs in both directions, and the disparity claim is about proportions, which the available figures do not settle.

Bevelyn Beatty Williams — United States, 2022–2025

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released, by pardon. Williams was convicted on February 22, 2024, in the Southern District of New York on one count under the FACE Act, arising from her conduct at clinic protests in New York and Florida, and sentenced on July 24, 2024, to forty-one months in prison and two years of supervised release.

The persecution claim. Williams and her supporters described the sentence—more than three years for clinic protest—as the criminalization of pro-life witness, and her case became the named instance in the argument that the FACE Act was enforced in one direction.

The answer. The prosecution’s record included findings of force and threats against clinic staff and patients across multiple incidents and states, and a prior injunction she was found to have defied. The sentence was imposed by the district court on that record.

The baseline and the exit. On January 23, 2025, President Trump pardoned Williams as one of twenty-three FACE Act and pro-life defendants pardoned that day. The FACE baseline, stated with the caution the figures require: the department has prosecuted attacks on abortion clinics and attacks on pregnancy centers and churches under the same statute, and a 2026 Justice Department report on its own prior enforcement gives internal proportions that secondary sources report inconsistently; no independently replicated dataset was before the desk. The verified facts are the two directions of prosecution and the pardon that closed this case.

Jack Phillips and Barronelle Stutzman — United States, 2012–2021

Status: civil. The axis does not reach this entry, and the distinction is the entry. Neither Phillips, a Colorado baker, nor Stutzman, a Washington florist, was ever criminally charged. Both were proceeded against civilly and administratively for declining to create work for same-sex weddings. Phillips’s case reached the Supreme Court, which ruled for him on June 4, 2018, by 7–2, on the narrow ground that the Colorado Civil Rights Commission had shown hostility to religion in his case; the broader question was expressly left undecided, and Colorado proceedings over a later refusal ended on procedural grounds in 2024. Stutzman lost unanimously in the Washington Supreme Court in 2017 and again in 2019, was denied review by the United States Supreme Court on July 2, 2021, and settled in November 2021 for $5,000, retiring rather than selling her shop.

The persecution claim. Religious-freedom organizations file both cases as persecution by process: years of proceedings, ruinous exposure, and livelihoods ended, without any crime.

The answer. The state commissions’ position was that public-accommodations law applies to businesses open to the public and that the proceedings were the ordinary machinery of that law. The Supreme Court’s one ruling in the pair turned on the commission’s own words about religion, which is the record’s judgment on how the machinery was run in Phillips’s case.

The baseline. The desk includes this entry, labeled civil throughout, because the persecution claim is made of these cases in the same public argument as the criminal ones—and because the difference between a prosecution and a proceeding is precisely the kind of distinction this ledger exists to keep.

Douglass Mackey — United States, 2021–2025

Status axis (4): convicted, then reversed with an acquittal ordered; never imprisoned. Mackey, who posted under the name Ricky Vaughn, was convicted in the Eastern District of New York in March 2023 under 18 U.S.C. § 241—the Reconstruction-era statute against conspiracies to injure citizens in the exercise of constitutional rights—for 2016 memes addressed to Hillary Clinton supporters stating that they could vote by text message. He was sentenced in October 2023 to seven months. The Second Circuit stayed his surrender pending appeal.

The persecution claim. FIRE, his counsel, and commentators argued novelty and selection: that § 241, written against the Ku Klux Klan, had never before been used to prosecute online political speech; that the memes were satire; and that a comparable 2016 post by a comedian telling Trump supporters to vote by text drew no charge. The novelty claim is verified as the critics’ characterization; the department made no such concession, and no exhaustive survey of § 241 was before the desk.

The answer. The government’s theory was voter deception, not satire: that Mackey conspired in private groups to suppress votes by fraud. The Second Circuit answered the case on July 9, 2025, unanimously: the government had proved the memes but had not proved that Mackey was party to the alleged agreement—he was not shown even to have seen the group exchanges in which the conspiracy was supposedly formed—and § 241 requires an agreement. The court reversed and ordered a judgment of acquittal. It did not reach the First Amendment defense, and it did not hold § 241 inapplicable to election deception. No government petition for rehearing or certiorari was found in the record; the acquittal was won in court, over the government’s opposition, and not conceded by it.

The baseline. Mackey is the ledger’s cleanest modern instance of the appellate exit: a novel use of an old statute, a conviction, and a reversal on the statute’s own elements.

V. Historical Left and Civil-Rights Cases

COINTELPRO and the Church Committee — United States, 1956–1976

Status: a program, not a defendant; the ledger’s documented baseline of state misconduct. COINTELPRO was the FBI’s counterintelligence program against domestic political movements, run from 1956 to 1971 and exposed by the 1971 burglary of the FBI’s Media, Pennsylvania office and by the Senate committee chaired by Frank Church, established January 27, 1975, whose final report issued on April 29, 1976. The committee concluded that intelligence agencies had “undermined the constitutional rights of citizens.” Of the program’s 295 authorized actions against Black nationalist groups, 233 targeted the Black Panther Party. Whether the American Indian Movement was a formal COINTELPRO category is not established by two reputable sources before the desk; related post-1971 surveillance of AIM is documented, and the desk prints the distinction because the entries below turn on it.

Why the program stands in this ledger. Every persecution claim in this group was made against a background the Senate itself documented: informants inside the movements, forged letters, and efforts to “neutralize” leaders. The committee’s findings are not a claim. They are the state’s own record, and they are the reason the claims in this group are weighed against a proved pattern rather than against an assumption of regularity—and also the reason the desk distinguishes, entry by entry, between what the pattern suggests and what each case’s record shows.

Angela Davis — United States, 1970–1972

Status axis (4): charged, never convicted; imprisoned before trial. Davis, a UCLA philosopher and Communist Party member, was charged in California with murder, kidnapping, and conspiracy—capital charges—after guns registered in her name were used in the August 7, 1970 Marin County courthouse shootout in which a judge and three others died. She fled, was placed on the FBI’s Ten Most Wanted list, and was captured in New York. She spent some sixteen months in custody before and during her trial.

The persecution claim. The claim was global and named: an international campaign, with committees in dozens of countries, argued that a Black communist intellectual was being tried for her politics on a theory of guilt by gun registration. The Soviet Union and its allies made her a state cause; her American defenders made the same argument from the evidence’s thinness.

The answer. The prosecution’s theory was that Davis had supplied the weapons out of love for one of the hostage-takers, an imprisoned activist. The answer that closed the case was a jury’s: on June 4, 1972, an all-white jury in San Jose acquitted Davis on all three counts after thirteen hours of deliberation.

The baseline. Davis is the ledger’s early proof that the pretrial condition and the final status can point in opposite directions: sixteen months in jail under a capital charge, and an acquittal that no appeal was needed to win.

The Chicago Seven — United States, 1968–1973

Status axis (4): convicted at trial; all substantive convictions reversed. Eight defendants—seven after Bobby Seale’s case was severed, Seale having been bound and gagged in the courtroom on the judge’s order—were tried in Chicago for conspiracy and crossing state lines to incite riot at the 1968 Democratic National Convention, under the Anti-Riot Act, the first prosecution of its kind. On February 18, 1970, the jury acquitted all defendants of conspiracy and convicted five—Abbie Hoffman, Jerry Rubin, David Dellinger, Tom Hayden, and Rennie Davis—of incitement; each received five years and a $5,000 fine. Judge Julius Hoffman imposed long contempt sentences on defendants and counsel during and after the trial.

The persecution claim. The defendants and a national movement called the trial the state prosecuting dissent itself; the courtroom’s own conduct—Seale’s binding, the contempt citations—became the claim’s chief exhibits.

The answer. The Seventh Circuit supplied it in two parts: the contempt convictions were reversed on May 11, 1972, and on November 21, 1972, the court reversed all five substantive convictions, citing among other grounds the trial judge’s conduct and errors in jury selection. The government did not retry the incitement charges. Contempt was retried in 1973 before a different judge, producing guilty findings against some defendants and no jail time and no fines.

The baseline. The Chicago case is the historical template for the mass-event group above: a prosecution of a political demonstration, convictions at trial, and an appellate record that undid them.

Leonard Peltier — United States, 1977–2025

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released, by commutation to home confinement. Peltier, an American Indian Movement activist, was convicted on April 18, 1977, of the murders of FBI agents Jack Coler and Ronald Williams, killed in a June 26, 1975 shootout on the Pine Ridge Reservation. He received two consecutive life sentences and served some forty-nine years, most of them at USP Coleman.

The persecution claim. This claim carries the ledger’s heaviest outside designations. The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention, in Opinion 7/2022 of June 7, 2022, found his detention arbitrary under two of its categories. The European Parliament passed clemency resolutions in 1994 and 1999. The claim’s evidentiary grounds, argued across decades of appeals, include the prosecution’s shifting theories of who fired the fatal shots and ballistics evidence the defense characterizes as exculpatory and withheld—arguments the courts heard across decades of appeals without disturbing the conviction.

The answer. The courts’ answer is the appeals record: the conviction was affirmed repeatedly, the Eighth Circuit holding that Peltier had aided and abetted the murders whatever hand fired; parole was denied as recently as July 2024.

The baseline and the exit. President Biden’s commutation warrant, dated January 19, 2025, and announced January 20, converted the life sentences to home confinement; it was expressly not a pardon. Peltier was released from USP Coleman on February 18, 2025. He remains a convicted man under sentence at home—the axis records him in condition (2) because the cell door opened, and the entry records that the conviction stands.

Mumia Abu-Jamal — United States, 1982–present

Status axis (1): serving now. Abu-Jamal, a former Black Panther and a radio journalist, was convicted in Philadelphia in 1982 of the murder of police officer Daniel Faulkner, shot on December 9, 1981, and sentenced to death. He is one of two men in this ledger presently in prison.

The persecution claim. Amnesty International’s February 2000 report concluded that his proceedings violated minimum international standards for a fair trial, citing the trial’s political atmosphere, the judge’s conduct, and the adequacy of his representation; Amnesty took no position on his guilt. The city of Paris made him an honorary citizen in 2001. His supporters’ grounds extend to the ballistics and witness record, disputed in the appeals for forty years.

The answer. Pennsylvania’s courts affirmed the conviction across decades of state and federal review; the prosecution’s case rests on eyewitnesses who placed Abu-Jamal as the shooter, the wounded officer’s return fire, and Abu-Jamal’s own registered revolver found at the scene with five spent casings. On December 7, 2011, prosecutors dropped the death sentence; he has since served life without parole at SCI Mahanoy. A petition on his behalf was filed with the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention on July 28, 2026; no opinion had been reported at filing.

The baseline. Abu-Jamal is condition (1) in its hardest form: a life sentence with the fair-trial designation of a major outside body standing against an unbroken line of appellate affirmances.

Geronimo Pratt — United States, 1972–1997

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released, conviction vacated. Pratt, a Black Panther leader in Los Angeles, was convicted in 1972 of the 1968 murder of Caroline Olsen in a robbery on a Santa Monica tennis court, and served twenty-seven years, eight of them in solitary confinement.

The persecution claim. His defenders argued from the start that the Panthers were COINTELPRO targets and that Pratt had been framed; unlike most such claims, this one was eventually proved in its central particular.

The answer. On June 10, 1997, a California court vacated the conviction, holding that the prosecution had concealed that its key witness, Julius Butler, was an informant for the FBI and the Los Angeles police who had lied under oath about that status. The ruling was upheld in 1999. Pratt was freed and not retried. His civil claims settled for $4.5 million—$2.75 million from Los Angeles and $1.75 million from the Justice Department; the sources before the desk give the settlement year variously as 1999 and 2000, and the desk prints the amount, which is settled, without the year, which is not.

The baseline. Pratt is the group’s proof of concept and its caution in one: a persecution claim that the record sustained, after twenty-seven years, in a case the state had won in 1972 and defended for a generation.

Assata Shakur — United States and Cuba, 1977–2025

Status: the exception to the axis—convicted, imprisoned, escaped, never returned. Shakur, a Black Liberation Army member, was convicted in 1977 of the murder of New Jersey State Trooper Werner Foerster, killed in a May 2, 1973 turnpike shootout in which Shakur was wounded, and sentenced to life plus twenty-six to thirty-three years. On November 2, 1979, she escaped from the Clinton Correctional Facility for Women. She surfaced in Cuba, which granted her asylum in 1984.

The persecution claim. Her supporters, and the Cuban government, maintained that a Black revolutionary could not receive a fair trial in the America of the COINTELPRO record; she was tried amid that program’s documented operations against her movement, a context the Church Committee record supplies and the trial record does not by itself answer.

The answer. The state’s answer was maintained for forty-six years: on May 2, 2013, the FBI placed Shakur on its Most Wanted Terrorists list—the first woman so listed—and the reward for her capture stood at $2 million. New Jersey officials restated the state’s position at every anniversary: a convicted trooper-killer at large.

The record’s end. Cuba’s Foreign Ministry announced that Shakur died in Havana on September 25, 2025; her daughter confirmed the death. The four boxes of the status axis do not hold her, and the desk leaves the exception standing as filed: the only entry in this ledger whose subject was convicted, imprisoned, and never released, pardoned, or recaptured, because she left.

VI. Designated from Outside

Carles Puigdemont and the Catalan Defendants — Spain, 2017–present

Status axis: split. Puigdemont stands in condition (4)—charged, never tried. The nine defendants who stood trial stand in condition (2)—imprisoned and released by pardon. After Catalonia’s October 1, 2017 independence referendum, held in defiance of Spain’s Constitutional Court, Puigdemont and his government fled or faced charges of rebellion and sedition. A German court in Schleswig-Holstein refused in 2018 to extradite Puigdemont on the rebellion charge; Belgian proceedings likewise failed to return him. Spain’s Supreme Court in 2019 acquitted the tried defendants of rebellion and convicted them of sedition and misuse of funds, with sentences of nine to thirteen years; Oriol Junqueras received thirteen.

The persecution claim. The claim here was made by courts, which is what distinguishes the entry: two member states’ judiciaries declined to treat the referendum as the violent uprising the rebellion charge required, and members of the European Parliament and UN human-rights figures characterized the prosecutions as the criminalization of a political project.

The answer. Spain’s courts answered that the defendants had defied binding court orders and misused public funds, that sedition required no completed uprising, and that the sentences were proportionate to a unilateral secession attempt. The baseline. The Catalan file is the ledger’s only entry in which foreign courts, a pardon, a legislature, and a constitutional court each took a hand in sequence—and the case is still not closed.

Tommy Robinson (Stephen Yaxley-Lennon) — United Kingdom, 2018–present

Status axis (2): imprisoned and released, repeatedly; a new conviction stands in 2026. Robinson’s imprisonments have been for contempt of court, and the strands must be kept apart. In May 2018 he was jailed for thirteen months for contempt at Leeds Crown Court, having live-streamed outside a trial subject to reporting restrictions; the Court of Appeal quashed that order for procedural flaws, and on retrial in 2019 he was convicted and sentenced to nine months. In October 2024 he admitted ten breaches of a 2021 injunction arising from a libel judgment—the injunction barred repeating the libelled allegations, which his film Silenced did—and received eighteen months, of which the coercive portion was later remitted; he was released on May 27, 2025. On October 28, 2026, at Southwark Crown Court, he was convicted of two counts of harassment causing fear of violence against two Mail journalists, and is on bail for sentencing on December 16, 2026.

The persecution claim. American officials and legislators have characterized his imprisonments as persecution for his activism—Representative Paul Gosar used the word, and former Ambassador-at-Large Sam Brownback lobbied the British government on his case in 2018. A named Hungarian politician’s characterization was sought by the desk and not verified; it is not printed. A claim that Vice President Vance named Robinson at Munich in February 2025 rests on observers’ interpretation; the speech did not name him.

The answer. The courts’ stated rationale is the reporting restriction itself: publications during live trials risk collapsing prosecutions of the very offenses Robinson says he exposes, and the contempt power protects pending trials, not the sensibilities of the state. The 2018 quashing is also part of the answer—the appellate court corrected the procedure that jailed him, and the retrial convicted him again.

The baseline. Robinson is the ledger’s contempt case in its pure form: no jury has convicted him of the underlying speech his supporters cite, and every day he has served traces to a court order he was found, or admitted, to have breached.
