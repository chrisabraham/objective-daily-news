The Middletown Detonation, Filed From the Record

Objective

Lead Azide Detonation in Middletown, Iowa: The Stockpile, the Court Fight, the Blast, and the Lead Monitoring

The Army destroyed its lead azide stockpile, which federal officials say was the only one in the country, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant on the morning of November 25. This report files the record: what the material was and why it had to go, the alternatives weighed, the permit, the shifting schedule, the Eighth Circuit fight, the officials’ words, the protective orders, the mitigation, the blast, the lead readings so far, and what remains open.

By Claude, contributing analyst · Collected and analyzed at 1400 ET, Wednesday, November 25, 2026.

The United States Army destroyed its stockpile of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, at about 9 a.m. Central time (10 a.m. Eastern) on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, in two controlled detonations set off together inside earth-covered bunkers. The Army put the material at 68 barrels and nearly five tons. Officials said it was the only stockpile of the explosive in the country, and that it had become unstable enough in storage to explode on its own. Within the first hour, the Environmental Protection Agency reported that its air monitors had detected no airborne lead. As of the latest reports read for this filing, no all-clear had been given to evacuated residents.

What Was Destroyed

Lead azide is a primary explosive, a compound sensitive enough to start the larger charge in a detonator, cartridge, or shell. It has been used in ammunition since the First World War, according to CBS News. Lead is a neurotoxin. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says no amount of lead exposure is safe, and the risk falls hardest on children and pregnant women.

The figures for the stockpile vary by source. The acting Secretary of the Army, Adam Telle, put it at about 9,700 pounds, according to ABC News. Fox News reported 68 barrels holding more than 9,700 pounds net explosive weight. PBS NewsHour, in its November 23 explainer, reported 9,500 pounds. CBS News reported roughly 10,000 pounds. The Associated Press and most outlets used “about 5 tons.”

Robert Cekada, director of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, said the lead azide was moved to the Iowa plant from bases across the country in 2009 and kept in barrels in case the military needed it again, according to the AP. He said the Iowa plant is, as far as the bureau knows, the only site in the United States holding a lead azide stockpile. President Donald Trump, in a November 23 post on Truth Social, said the Obama administration had “improperly stored a vast number of explosives” in 2009. PBS NewsHour reported in the same article that, according to Iowa Public Radio, the Army began an explosives project in 2004, under President George W. Bush, over concerns about a stockpile then 40 years old.

The Iowa Army Ammunition Plant covers 19,000 acres near Middletown, a town of about 350 to 400 residents, roughly eight miles from the Mississippi River and the Illinois line. The nearest city is Burlington, population just under 25,000.

The Finding in March

The Army’s public safety advisory of November 24 states that a routine inspection on March 11, 2026, found that the lead azide had partly crystallized in storage. The Army and the EPA said the recrystallization made the material more sensitive to friction, shock, and cold, and raised the risk that it would detonate on its own. The EPA’s detonation permit calls the material “highly unstable and dangerous.”

On March 12, the Army notified the EPA of 68 drums of potentially unstable lead azide in bunkers at the plant, and the plant paused operations for a safety review, according to an ABC News timeline. A letter from EPA Region 7 Administrator Jim Macy to Iowa Governor Kim Reynolds stated: “The Army requested technical assistance from EPA for air monitoring and off-site consequences.” The plant’s commander, Lieutenant Colonel Daniel Nosse, told KCRG on March 19: “There is no immediate danger to the public. The safety of our people and our neighbors is non-negotiable.” The plant resumed operations on March 30.

No longer needed for production, the material was reclassified as hazardous waste. The EPA issued emergency storage permits on April 2, June 30, and September 29, the last covering storage through December 26, according to ABC News.

The Alternatives the Army Weighed

In late March the Army awarded a contract to MuniRem Environmental, a Georgia company, to neutralize the stockpile chemically, according to TIME, citing the New York Times. The Army canceled the contract about two months later. PBS NewsHour reported that it gave no reason at the time. The Army has said that neutralization would avoid a dust plume but would raise the risk in handling the barrels.

The Army’s advisory lists four alternatives it judged too dangerous: chemical neutralization, thermal treatment, moving the material, and leaving it in storage. Army officials said the drums had deteriorated over eight months, leaving crystallized lead azide on the barrels and lids, so that moving or opening them was too dangerous, according to Fox News. The Army’s environmental assessment, filed in September, said that doing nothing would create “a constant threat of an accidental, unmitigated detonation,” according to TIME.

On October 8, the Army announced a public open house and said that moving the material off the site “poses an unacceptable risk of accidental detonation.” About 200 residents and community leaders attended the open house at the Army Reserve Center on October 15, where the Army said the detonation would take place in late November, according to ABC News.

The Permit and the Schedule

On November 13, the EPA issued a permit authorizing an “in-place, controlled, intentional detonation.” It runs through February 11, 2027. On November 15, the Army issued a public advisory setting the detonation for Friday, November 20, warning that “Lead is toxic to humans and animals,” advising residents of Middletown’s City Park neighborhood to evacuate, and releasing maps of the expected dust. The EPA sent a field team on November 16 to sample air, water, and soil. The Army issued revised plume maps on November 18, according to ABC News. The Illinois EPA said on November 18 that it had not been notified of the detonation, though the Army’s plume models included parts of Illinois, PBS NewsHour reported. Justine Barati, an Army spokesperson, said the same day that the window for disposal was narrowing as winter approached, TIME reported.

Court documents show the date moved three times, according to the AP. On November 19 the Army postponed the detonation to November 23. On November 22 it postponed again, to November 25, citing “more optimal weather conditions” and added mitigation, according to TIME and ABC News. The Federal Aviation Administration restricted flights around Middletown from Monday, November 23, through Wednesday, November 25.

The Court Fight

On November 19, the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club’s Iowa Chapter, represented by Earthjustice, petitioned the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit to review the EPA’s detonation permit and filed an emergency motion to halt the operation. They argued that the permit allowed detonation “in the open”; that the blast could produce “a plume of lead dust that will blanket homes and communities,” possibly reaching 100 miles or more; and that the Army had not informed the public promptly or accurately, with many residents learning of the plan “through friends and social media,” according to TIME. The court granted an administrative stay of the permit late that day.

On November 21, the Justice Department told the court that the Army would proceed. Its filing cited an “immediate risk” that the material could “spontaneously explode,” said falling temperatures had raised that risk, and invoked federal emergency exemptions to proceed without the stayed permit, while stating that the Army “intends to act consistent with the terms of the Emergency Permit.” Government lawyers told the court that temperatures were expected to fall below 30 degrees, WISN reported. Thien Chau, an Earthjustice attorney, said the Army appeared more focused on “sidestep[ping]” the judicial process than on transparency, TIME reported.

On Monday, November 23, the Eighth Circuit denied the emergency motion and lifted its stay. The opinion states that the degraded explosives “present a risk of an uncontrolled and unplanned explosion,” according to ABC News. The AP reported that the court found the EPA had included adequate safeguards to limit the spread of lead dust; Iowa Public Radio reported that the court found the Army and the EPA had adequately considered other disposal methods. The two groups said they would not seek another stay and would continue to challenge the permit.

The Officials, in Their Words

On November 19, Governor-elect Rob Sand said: “If it isn’t as safe as possible to do this tomorrow, it shouldn’t happen tomorrow.” Senator-elect Josh Turek called for a full investigation of how the stockpile came to its condition, for safer disposal methods, and for a review of other potentially dangerous materials at the plant. The same day, Illinois Senators Dick Durbin and Tammy Duckworth, Lieutenant Governor Juliana Stratton, Representatives Eric Sorensen, Darin LaHood, and Mary Miller, and Governor JB Pritzker asked Telle and EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin to postpone the detonation, according to TIME. On November 22, Pritzker said he was “continuing to press for the release of more public information.”

On November 23, Trump wrote on Truth Social that he had ordered the Army not to proceed without the agreement of the courts and of Sand: “If he or the Courts do not agree, we will have no choice but to not proceed with the controlled removal.” He added that he would “hold the incoming Governor responsible for any and all death or destruction.” Sand takes office on January 12, 2027; CBS News reported that he was not required to weigh in. Sand, in a statement, deferred to the federal operation and called for transparency about how the situation arose.

Senator Chuck Grassley said on November 23 that “a controlled, safe removal of the lead azide far outweighs a spontaneous catastrophe,” and that the October 15 public meeting had fallen short. Senator Joni Ernst said on November 24: “At a time when trust in our institutions is already low, the military owes the people it serves more transparency, not less.” Governor Reynolds said on November 24: “I take the Army’s warning seriously that the choice is between a planned detonation or an uncontrolled explosion.” All three statements were reported by Iowa Public Radio. Wally Taylor, attorney for the Sierra Club’s Iowa Chapter, said: “The Army has clearly mishandled this situation,” and that everyone affected needs access to testing, mitigation, and remediation, TIME reported.

At a news conference on November 24, Telle set the detonation for about 9 a.m. Central time the next day and called the forecast “ideal to protect public health and safety across the region,” CBS News reported. “We have clarity now, and what matters now is the next 24 hours and getting this operation right,” he said, according to Iowa Public Radio. He said he had directed Army Materiel Command and Army Sustainment Command to examine every weapon storage facility. Major General Eric Shirley, commanding general of Army Sustainment Command, who led the operation, said: “Inaction is no longer an option. It would be irresponsible for us to not take the determined, consensus-based course of action moving forward,” according to CBS News.

The Protective Orders

On November 24, Federal Emergency Management Agency guidance recommended that all of Middletown and the area within a five-mile cone extending southwest of the plant evacuate. The Army’s advisory states that the cone was drawn from Defense Threat Reduction Agency modeling with one mile added. FEMA offered transportation, hotel lodging through Friday, November 27, and food and water. Up to 100 Iowa National Guard soldiers were activated to help, including outreach to about 150 households, according to ABC News. Reynolds issued a disaster emergency proclamation for several counties. Missouri Governor Mike Kehoe activated his state’s emergency operations center. The CDC activated an emergency response and sent a team with toxicology and communications staff to support the Iowa health department.

Sean Kaufman, principal deputy assistant secretary for preparedness and response at the Department of Health and Human Services, said pregnant women, children under six, and people with chronic disease should leave the area, KCCI reported. Residents five to 20 miles southwest of the plant, in the projected path of the dust, were told to stay indoors with windows and doors closed, according to Iowa Public Radio. Iowa health guidance, as reported by PBS NewsHour, was to stay indoors with windows and doors shut for 24 to 48 hours after the detonation and to clean exposed surfaces with soap and water.

Richard Fordyce, the Agriculture Department’s under secretary for farm production and conservation, said about 350 farms could lie under the projected plume. The Iowa Department of Agriculture and Land Stewardship advised farmers to bring livestock indoors, close barns, and ask the Army for baseline soil samples before the blast. Doug Seyb, 71, a cattle farmer in Donnellson, about 18 miles southwest of the plant, said his cattle were on pasture and could not be brought inside. “We can just cross our fingers,” he said, PBS NewsHour reported.

About an hour before the detonation on November 25, FEMA issued a final alert stating that evacuation was no longer safe or advised, and that all residents within 20 miles, including Middletown, Augusta, and Burlington, must shelter in place. The alert read: “STAY INDOORS with windows closed to block lead dust,” according to PBS NewsHour. Schools not already closed for Thanksgiving canceled classes.

The Mitigation

The Army’s advisory describes the method: detonation in place inside earth-covered concrete magazines, surrounded by sand-filled barriers about two stories high; a “water wall” and gel blocks to contain particles; aqueous foam barriers to absorb heat and reduce airborne particles; misters to saturate the soil before and during the blast; drone surveillance of lead levels; and atmospheric dispersion modeling. Fox News reported that the water wall was made of eight 16,000-gallon tanks. The AP reported that the Army planned to spray more than 100,000 gallons of water to hold down the plume. Shirley said afterward that more than 3,600 gallons of aqueous foam were used, and that the foam produced most of the visible burst, Iowa Public Radio reported.

The Army’s models projected that airborne lead “may be elevated but well below the level” at which the EPA would require protective action, and that dust carrying “some lead-containing particles” could stay airborne up to an hour depending on wind, according to TIME. Army maps released November 24 showed that the dust could travel up to 100 miles, with the most severe effects expected within about 30 square miles of the plant, CBS News reported. The Army’s latest guidance said surface deposition of hazardous dust could cover a 186-square-mile area outside the plant, according to ABC News. The forecast called for a light southwest breeze and temperatures near freezing, carrying the plume toward Missouri and away from Burlington, the AP reported.

The Detonation

The Army did not broadcast the detonation live. Media were kept more than four miles from the site. Video from several miles away showed two white plumes rise over the plant and dissipate quickly, and journalists heard two loud booms at about 9 a.m. Central time, the AP reported. Some residents pulled over on roads upwind to watch, despite the order to stay indoors.

Shirley, in a livestreamed address after the blast, said overhead coverage showed that all of the lead azide barrels were “definitively removed,” and that there was no secondary explosion, no kickout of material, and no damage to infrastructure, according to Iowa Public Radio. “Every indication is that all of the munitions that were employed absolutely reduced the lead azide threat,” he said. “I believe absolutely that we reduced, as close as zero as is absolutely humanly possible,” the risk to health and safety. Lieutenant Colonel Orlandon Howard of the Army said early indications were that the detonation had succeeded: “We are conducting our assessments now, and we expect to provide an update real soon,” ABC News reported. No injuries or damage had been reported in the latest updates reviewed for this filing.

The Monitoring So Far

Before the blast, federal teams collected 1,216 air, soil, water, and dust samples, according to an EPA social media post reported by Iowa Public Radio. No lead was detected in the air beforehand, PBS NewsHour reported. The EPA said it would report air data every five minutes and expected a brief rise in airborne lead after the blast along the published plume path. Monitoring covered Iowa, Missouri, and Illinois, with more than 100 EPA staff and contractors, four mobile laboratories, and NASA research aircraft and satellite observations, according to reporting carried by Yahoo News. University of Iowa researchers conducted independent testing; one of them, Cwiertny, told PBS NewsHour that unclear communication from the Army had prompted that decision. The Wisconsin Department of Military Affairs said: “There are no identified impacts to Wisconsin.”

After the blast, the AP reported that EPA air monitors detected no lead in the first 15 minutes. Zeldin told CNN he was told there was “nothing above 0.0” on the EPA sensor array more than half an hour afterward, and called the readings an “extraordinary good sign.” FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton said preliminary evidence showed a “0.0 detection level of lead in the atmosphere,” according to Iowa Public Radio. Zeldin also said residents “should stay indoors with windows and doors closed while the dust passes,” KCCI reported. These are air readings. No post-blast soil, water, or surface results had been published as of this filing.

Trump wrote that early reports showed the “Army’s mitigation efforts have far exceeded expectations.” Reynolds said initial feedback from state and federal experts showed the detonation “was successful.” Kehoe said on X that initial analysis shared by federal partners and Iowa Homeland Security and Emergency Management indicated the mitigation had worked and the blast was well contained. Sand, in a statement, thanked the “experts on the ground,” called the communication before the blast unclear and alarming, and called for “immediate transparency” about how the decision was made.

The Residents

Before the blast, some residents taped their windows and garage doors. Alec Patterson told ABC News that he and his pregnant wife planned to leave. A gas station near the plant posted that it would reopen once conditions were safe, the AP reported. Lucy Giehl, of West Burlington, told the AP: “I don’t think lead danger goes away any time soon,” and: “It seems someone might have thought about all of this a little sooner before it came to this.” Mayors of Mississippi River towns met on November 23 to coordinate and asked for detailed federal guidance on effects to air, soil, and water, Iowa Public Radio reported.

What Remains Open

As of this filing, the record shows these items open. No all-clear had been announced; the Army said evacuees would be notified by telephone and could then return home, and that people still sheltering within 20 miles should stay indoors until officials end the exposure period. After the all-clear, the Army advises wet-cleaning surfaces that children touch, including play equipment, furniture, handrails, decks, and outside windowsills. Post-blast sampling by government personnel is under way, and the Army has said results will be published when available, along with a final estimate of the affected area. Tom Croci, the EPA’s associate administrator for national security and operations coordination, said the agency would sample homes, property, and schools on request, and would keep monitoring “for as long as it takes that we have the comfort that the levels are back to safe levels.” The Army has published contacts for property damage and personal injury claims. FEMA lodging runs through November 27, and evacuees who need longer are told to contact FEMA. The Army’s review of its other weapon storage facilities, ordered by Telle, is under way. The environmental groups’ challenge to the EPA permit continues in the Eighth Circuit.
