Julius and Ethel Rosenberg: What the Record Shows About the Espionage, the Evidence Against Ethel, the Trial, and the Executions

Julius Rosenberg ran a Soviet ring that passed more than 20,000 pages of American weapons documents, and the Venona decrypts and the KGB's own notebooks establish it. Ethel Rosenberg stands in the same records with no covername and a codebreaker's memorandum stating she did not engage in the work herself, and the testimony that she typed atomic notes was given ten days before trial, contradicted by the witnesses' own grand jury testimony, and withdrawn by her brother in 2001 as a lie told to protect his wife. This report prints the whole ledger: what Julius did, what the atomic material was worth and who says so, what became of the ring, the government's lever memoranda, the trial's documented defects, the sentence and the road to Sing Sing, the antisemitism question in both directions, and what the releases of 1975 to 2024 changed, down to the case's return to the news in November 2026.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1400 ET, Friday, November 13, 2026.

The File in Brief

Julius Rosenberg ran a Soviet espionage ring that passed the United States government more than 20,000 pages of weapons documents, and the decrypted Soviet cables and the KGB's own archival notebooks establish that beyond serious dispute; Ethel Rosenberg, his wife, stands in the same records with no codename, no document in her hand, and one profile cable stating that she knew of her husband's work and did not work herself, and the only trial testimony that she performed an act of espionage, typing her brother's notes, was first given ten days before the trial opened, was contradicted by the same witnesses' grand jury testimony, and was withdrawn by her brother in 2001 as a lie told to protect his wife. Both were convicted on March 29, 1951, of conspiracy to commit espionage and executed in the electric chair at Sing Sing on June 19, 1953, after a trial whose judge, the Federal Judicial Center records, privately solicited the prosecution's views on sentence while stating in court that he had not, and after a clemency campaign that reached two presidents and protests in 48 countries. The government's own internal file, released in pieces over fifty years, shows the Justice Department advising in July 1950 that evidence against Ethel was insufficient, J. Edgar Hoover proposing her prosecution as a lever against her husband and then recommending against her execution, and a prosecutor telling a congressional committee in secret session, a month before trial, that the case against her was not too strong and that convicting her was important anyway. What declassification changed, it changed in one direction for Julius and the other for Ethel: the Venona decrypts of 1995 and the Vassiliev notebooks of 2009 deepened the record of his spying, and the grand jury releases of 2008 and 2015, Morton Sobell's admission of 2008, and the codebreaker Meredith Gardner's 1950 memorandum, released in 2024, each removed weight from the case against her. Their sons, Michael and Robert Meeropol, have sought her formal exoneration for fifty years; no president has granted it. The case returned to the news in November 2026 because a podcaster invoked the executions against a living businessman and got the manner of them wrong, and this report prints the record the invocation reached for.

The desk's method is the paper's method. The claims made about this case across seventy years, that it was a setup, that it was antisemitism, that the Rosenbergs were spies, that they were patsies, are each tested below against the documentary record: the Venona decrypts released by the National Security Agency, the Vassiliev notebooks published by the Wilson Center, the Federal Judicial Center's history of the trial, the grand jury transcripts released by the National Archives, and the FBI files the Meeropols obtained under the Freedom of Information Act. Where the record is two records, both are printed. Nothing in this report rests on a source the reader cannot open.

1. What the Record Shows Julius Did

Julius Rosenberg was a recruited Soviet agent for eight years, and the evidence of it comes from the Soviet side of the wire. He was recruited on Labor Day 1942 by the NKVD spymaster Semyon Semyonov, introduced through a Communist Party liaison, and run from 1944 by the case officer Alexander Feklisov, whose memoir is one of the sources. In the KGB's cables he carried the covername ANTENNA, changed to LIBERAL by a Moscow cable of September 2, 1944. The cable itself gives the reason for the change, and the desk prints it because the reason is routinely improved upon in retelling: the new covernames, the cable states, were selected with a view to economy of means, shorter forms replacing disadvantageous ones. No primary source found by the desk supports the common claim that ANTENNA was changed because it had been compromised.

What he ran was an engineers' ring, and its product was conventional weapons data in industrial quantity. The CIA's Studies in Intelligence, working from Feklisov's account and the Venona cables, totals the group's delivery at more than 20,000 pages of technical documents, including the entire 12,000-page design and production manual for the P-80 Shooting Star, the first American operational jet fighter, supplied through William Perl from the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics. Joel Barr and Alfred Sarant, copying from a Greenwich Village studio, turned over more than 9,000 pages covering more than 100 weapons programs: most American air and ground radars, the SCR-584 microwave radar, the AN/APQ-7 radar, the Norden bombsight, analog fire-control computers, friend-or-foe identification systems. Julius himself, working as an Army Signal Corps inspector at Emerson Radio, stole a proximity fuze and delivered it to Feklisov as a Christmas present in 1944. The CIA study records the Soviet uptake: mass production of SCR-584 replicas began in 1949, and the combination of the radar, the M-9 gun director, and the fuze is the lineage of the system that brought down Francis Gary Powers's U-2 over Sverdlovsk on May 1, 1960. Steven Usdin's study of the Vassiliev notebooks, in the Journal of Cold War Studies in 2009, states the ring's weight plainly: its primary contribution was not atomic. It was a wealth of detailed information about non-nuclear weapons systems that were critical elements of the early Cold War Soviet arsenal.

His recruits were engineers, and he recruited inside his own family and his own college. Barr, Sarant, Perl, and Morton Sobell were City College engineers; the CIA history says Barr recruited Sarant, a Venona cable of November 14, 1944 is described as reporting that Rosenberg had carried through the contracting of Sarant himself, and the desk prints both phrasings because the sources carry both. In February 1944, on the Vassiliev record, Julius recruited Russell McNutt, a Kellex engineer working on structural designs for the K-25 gaseous diffusion plant at Oak Ridge, who passed plant designs and blueprints under the covernames FOGEL and PERS and was never charged. Haynes and Klehr call McNutt the KGB's first Manhattan Project source; the scholarly roundtable on their book notes the other side, that McNutt refused a transfer to Oak Ridge and the KGB never got as much as it hoped from him. And Julius recruited his brother-in-law, David Greenglass, a machinist at Los Alamos, which is where the atomic part of the case begins.

2. The Atomic Secret, Weighed

What David Greenglass passed is a matter of trial record, and it is smaller than the sentence it carried. In January 1945, on furlough in New York, he delivered to the Rosenbergs a replica sketch of an explosive lens mold, descriptive material, a report of the experiments in his shop, and the names of scientists at Los Alamos. In June 1945 in Albuquerque he gave classified material to the courier Harry Gold, who identified himself with a torn piece of a Jell-O box panel matching a half Greenglass held. In September 1945 he delivered some twelve pages, including a cross-section sketch of the implosion bomb, replicated at trial as showing 36 high-explosive lenses with two detonators each. Judge Kaufman, sentencing, told the Rosenbergs their conduct had put the A-bomb into Russian hands years before American scientists predicted Russia would perfect it. The question the record since 1953 has actually argued is narrower: what those twelve pages were worth to a program that was also receiving the work of Klaus Fuchs and Theodore Hall.

Every source the desk read agrees on the ranking, and they disagree on everything below it. Fuchs, a theoretical physicist inside the implosion work, delivered detailed descriptions of the plutonium bomb with dimensions; Hall, a 19-year-old physicist, delivered the design and the personnel; Greenglass, a machinist, delivered sketches of the molds his shop machined. Mark Kramer of Harvard, editor of the Journal of Cold War Studies, working from the nine published volumes of the Soviet atomic project's own documents, holds that the Greenglass sketch provided confirmation of information from Fuchs and Hall and that confirmation had value, because Beria's Special Committee always sought multiple sources to confirm what espionage brought it. Richard Rhodes, in Dark Sun, holds more: that Greenglass's information on implosion, however limited, was the first news the Soviets had of the radical new approach. Against both, the Soviet documents themselves are read for Igor Kurchatov's assessment, that the Greenglass material provided at best limited corroboration of what had already been obtained from Fuchs and Hall; Feklisov, the case officer, claimed Fuchs's data was thousands of times more valuable, a judgment Ronald Radosh answers by noting Feklisov is not a reliable judge of the sketches' importance; and when a copy of the sketch was made public in 1966, Michael Meeropol states, the nuclear scientists who examined it called it a secret of nothing, with no dimensions and errors in it. The desk prints the spread as it stands. No source in the file claims Greenglass alone gave the Soviet Union a workable design, and no source in the file claims his material was the most important atomic information the ring's wider network produced, because the two men who produced more were never in the Rosenbergs' dock.

4. What the Record Shows About Ethel

The Federal Judicial Center's sentence on the trial evidence is the shortest summary of Ethel Rosenberg's position, and the desk prints it whole: none of the documentary evidence introduced by the prosecution tied the Rosenbergs to espionage. The case rested on witnesses, and of the four who carried it, only two, David and Ruth Greenglass, testified to any act by Ethel. Seton Hall Law School's three-year review of the released files, published in December 2016, reduces the government's case against her to three claims: that in November 1944 she asked Ruth to carry Julius's recruitment offer to David and urged her to at least talk to him; that she typed David's espionage notes; and that the Soviets gave the couple gifts, including a console table said to be adapted for microfilm work, which was never produced in evidence. Of the three, only the first existed at the time of indictment. The typing claim, the one prosecutor Irving Saypol built his closing on, Ethel sat at that typewriter and struck the keys, blow by blow, against her own country, has a documented birthday, and it is not in 1945.

The typing claim first appears in the FBI file on February 23 and 24, 1951, ten days before the trial opened, after eight months in which neither Greenglass had mentioned it. Ruth Greenglass, re-interviewed, volunteered the additional information that Ethel had typed David's September 1945 notes on a bridge table. David, re-interviewed two days later, furnished in substance the same information. Before Ruth's re-interview, on the record of the released files, prosecutors had reminded her that she was still subject to indictment and that her husband had not yet been sentenced. The transcripts of those two February interviews have never been released or located in government files. Against the trial testimony stand the same witnesses under oath eighteen months earlier. Ruth's grand jury testimony of August 3, 1950, released in 2008 after her death, contains no typing and no September 1945 meeting, and has her transcribing David's information in longhand herself; a KGB cable of January 8, 1945, reports Greenglass's material as a hand-written plan, which matches the grand jury version and not the trial version. David's grand jury testimony of August 7, 1950, released on July 15, 2015, under court order after his death, contains no typing and no September meeting, and has him answering, of his sister: I said before, and say it again, honestly, this is a fact: I never spoke to my sister about this at all. The New York Times, reporting the 2015 release, printed the caution the desk also prints: the grand jury testimony does not necessarily contradict the trial testimony, since it does not mention typing, but it might have undermined his credibility had the defense been able to read it. The defense could not read it. It was secret for sixty-five years.

In 2001, David Greenglass withdrew the typing testimony himself, on the record, and stated his reason. Interviewed by Sam Roberts of the New York Times for the book The Brother, and on CBS's 60 Minutes II on December 5, 2001, he said: I don't know who typed it, frankly, and to this day I can't remember that the typing took place. I had no memory of that at all, none whatsoever. He said his trial account rested entirely on his wife's recollection; that he frankly thought his wife did the typing; that Ruth had put Ethel in it, and, in his words, what am I going to do, call my wife a liar. His stated arithmetic was one sentence: my wife is more important to me than my sister. He expressed no remorse: as a spy who turned his family in, I don't care. I sleep very well. Roberts's assessment, printed by the Associated Press, is the one the file supports: handwritten or typed, the notes contained little or nothing new, but from the prosecution's perspective the Remington was as good as a smoking gun in Ethel Rosenberg's hands.

The Soviet record, opened later, assigns Ethel no covername, and the covername fact is the hinge of the modern argument. In Venona, Julius is ANTENNA and then LIBERAL, David Greenglass is KALIBR, and Ruth Greenglass is OSA, the Russian word for wasp. Ethel appears by name, twice in the deciphered cables the analysts count: on September 21, 1944, when Liberal and his wife recommended Ruth as an intelligent and clever girl whose flat might serve the work, and on November 27, 1944, in the profile Moscow requested of her, which the National Security Agency's own release prints and the desk quotes from the primary document: a party member since 1938, sufficiently well developed politically, knows about her husband's work and the role of METR and NIL, in view of delicate health does not work, is characterized positively and as a devoted person. Alexander Feklisov, who ran Julius, wrote in his 1999 memoir that Ethel had never participated in his covert activities, and gave his proof: she was never given a code name in the secret cables with the Center, even Ruth Greenglass, despite her rather small contribution, had one. In August 1950, eleven days after Ethel's arrest, the codebreaker Meredith Gardner, whose work on those cables had identified LIBERAL, summarized the November 1944 profile in a handwritten top secret memorandum: she was a party member, a devoted wife, and she knew about her husband's work, but due to ill health she did not engage in the work herself. The memorandum stayed classified for seventy-four years. The National Security Agency released it to the Meeropols in 2024, on their 2022 Freedom of Information request; the family announced it on September 10, 2024, and Representative McGovern's account dates the securing of the release to August. The Associated Press's neutral wording is the desk's: the memorandum does not itself use the words not a spy; that characterization is the family's, built on Gardner's sentence and on the missing covername.

The counter-reading exists, is held by the historians who know the Soviet files best, and is printed here at the same weight. Harvey Klehr, John Earl Haynes, and Mark Kramer read Alexander Vassiliev's notebooks from the KGB archive as showing Ethel in contact with KGB officers and active in recruiting David Greenglass and Russell McNutt. Klehr, quoted by the Associated Press on the Gardner memorandum's release, stated the position in one sentence: Ethel may not have been a spy, but she was an active participant in her husband's spy network, which is to say guilty of the conspiracy she was charged with, whether or not she passed classified information. Kramer called the memorandum's exonerating interpretation debatable. Klehr's settled formulation, from 2021, is that Ethel was rightly convicted, even if wrongly executed. Morton Sobell, who had every reason of his own to weigh his words, gave the other side's shortest version in 2008, at the end of the next-but-one section of this report: she knew what he was doing, but what was she guilty of? Of being Julius's wife.

5. The Lever: The Government's Own File

The prosecution of Ethel Rosenberg as an instrument against her husband is not an inference drawn by her defenders. It is stated in the government's internal documents, in the government's own word for it: lever. On July 17, 1950, the day of Julius's arrest, an internal FBI memorandum recorded the Justice Department Criminal Division's advice that there was insufficient evidence to issue process against Ethel at this time. Seton Hall's review of the files found no new evidence against her discovered between that memorandum and her arrest on August 11, nor between her arrest and her indictment. J. Edgar Hoover wrote to Attorney General McGrath proposing, to bring about a change in Julius's attitude, that proceeding against his wife might serve as a lever in this matter. The Federal Judicial Center records the result in one line: the lever failed to sway either of them, even up to the last days before the execution.

The design was still being stated aloud, in secret, a month before the trial. On February 8, 1951, Assistant U.S. Attorney Myles Lane told the Joint Congressional Committee on Atomic Energy, in closed session: the case is not too strong against Mrs. Rosenberg, but I think it is very important that she be convicted, too, given a stiff sentence. He gave the committee the reasoning: the only thing that will break this man Rosenberg is the prospect of a death penalty or getting the chair, plus that if we can convict his wife too, and give her a sentence of 25 or 30 years, that combination may serve to make this fellow disgorge. Deputy Attorney General William P. Rogers, looking back, confirmed the hope and its failure in four words: she called our bluff. And three days before sentencing, on April 2, 1951, Hoover wrote to the Attorney General recommending thirty years for Ethel, not death, on the stated ground that she was the mother of two small children and would in a sense be presumed to be acting under the influence of her husband. A scholarly review of the released memoranda finds every FBI official whose sentencing memoranda survive, Hoover, Ladd, Ford, and Whelan, opposed the death penalty for her. The judge imposed it anyway. In June 1953 the administration's final offer, delivered through the Director of the Bureau of Prisons, was life for cooperation; the Rosenbergs refused it in writing, and the FBI's thirteen-page list of questions prepared for Julius at Sing Sing, in case the couple broke, contained exactly one question concerning Ethel: was your wife cognizant of your activities?

6. The Trial's Documented Defects

The trial ran from March 6 to March 29, 1951, in the Southern District of New York, and the defects now alleged against it divide into those the documents establish and those they do not, and the desk keeps the two piles separate. Established, on the Federal Judicial Center's own history: Judge Irving Kaufman stated in court that he had not asked the prosecution for a sentencing recommendation and would bear the responsibility alone, and he had, in the Center's words, privately solicited the view of the prosecution, other judges, and Department of Justice officials. The FBI memoranda released in the 1970s under the Meeropols' Freedom of Information suit carry the specifics. An FBI memorandum of March 16, 1951, records a Justice Department official telling the Bureau that Kaufman would impose death if the Rosenbergs were convicted, if he doesn't change his mind. Two days before sentencing, Roy Cohn, the young assistant prosecutor, told an FBI agent that Kaufman personally favored death for the Rosenbergs and a prison term for Sobell. The day before sentencing, Kaufman asked U.S. Attorney Irving Saypol to obtain the Justice Department's views and to make no recommendation in open court, and Saypol obliged. Vern Countryman's reading of the full file concludes that the Department was not prepared to recommend death and that Kaufman concealed that; other summaries of the same file, in which Saypol privately carried a death recommendation, do not reconcile cleanly with it, and the desk prints the disagreement rather than a winner.

Alleged, by the participant with the strongest motive to exaggerate: Roy Cohn himself. In his posthumous 1988 autobiography Cohn claimed that his influence procured the assignments of both Saypol and Kaufman to the case, that Kaufman had told him before the trial began that he would impose death on Julius, and that death for Ethel was imposed on Cohn's personal recommendation, in words he quotes himself using: she's worse than Julius, she's the older one, she's the one with the brains. In the same book Cohn denied participating in illegal ex parte discussions, and a standard reference work reports that the American Bar Association ultimately exonerated both Cohn and Kaufman for their conversations. Martin Siegel, Kaufman's last law clerk, writing in 2023 with the FBI files, Cohn's memoirs, and interviews with former clerks, treats secret back-channel discussions between Kaufman and the prosecution team, continuing throughout the trial, as established. What no source in the file verifies is the causal claim at the center of Cohn's boasting, that his advice produced Ethel's sentence. The documents establish the channel. They do not establish whose voice in it decided.

Two structural facts complete the defect ledger, and neither is alleged; both are arithmetic. The strongest evidence the government possessed, the Venona decrypts, could not be shown to the jury, because showing them would have exposed the code-breaking program to the Soviets; the jury that convicted on witness testimony never saw the documentary record that convicted Julius in history's accounting and that contained, for Ethel, the words does not work. And the sentence was imposed by the judge alone. No jury recommended death, a feature of the Espionage Act procedure that became, two years later, the ground of the last legal fight of the case.

7. The Sentence and the Road to Sing Sing

Kaufman sentenced on April 5, 1951, and his words from the bench are the most quoted document in the case. I consider your crime worse than murder, he said. Plain deliberate contemplated murder is dwarfed in magnitude by comparison with the crime you have committed. He then assigned them a war: but in your case, I believe your conduct in putting into the hands of the Russians the A-bomb years before our best scientists predicted Russia would perfect the bomb has already caused, in my opinion, the Communist aggression in Korea, with the resultant casualties exceeding 50,000, and who knows but that millions more of innocent people may pay the price of your treason. One word of the peroration varies between transcript reproductions, determine against demonstrate; the Korea passage is identical in every copy the desk read. Sobell, whose material was not atomic, received thirty years. David Greenglass, the witness, received fifteen the next day.

The sentence's uniqueness is stated two ways in the record, and the desk prints both, because the difference between them was litigated. The institutional summaries, the CIA's review and the Federal Judicial Center's historian among them, call the Rosenbergs the only Americans ever put to death in peacetime for espionage, and the first civilians executed on such charges. Kaufman himself, in his opinion of January 2, 1953, rejected the premise: the sentences were not imposed for peacetime espionage but for wartime espionage, he wrote; this court would not have the power to impose these sentences for peacetime espionage. The conspiracy was charged across 1944 to 1950, years that include the war, and on the wartime character of the acts, in his holding, the death power rested. To the argument that Russia in 1944 was an American ally, he answered from the Supreme Court's Gorin decision: the Espionage Act makes no distinction between friend or enemy, and punishes delivery of defense information to the advantage of any foreign nation.

The clemency fight ran on two continents and reached the presidency twice. President Truman left office on January 20, 1953, without acting on the petitions. Albert Einstein had written to him on January 12, conscience compelling him to urge commutation, prompted by the Nobel chemist Harold Urey's letter to the New York Times of January 5; Urey also wired President Eisenhower, in June, that a man of David Greenglass's capacity was wholly incapable of transmitting the physics, chemistry, and mathematics of the atomic bomb to anyone. The Apostolic Delegation states that Pope Pius XII's appeal was communicated to the Justice Department in December 1952 and renewed on February 13, 1953; the White House, the State Department, and the Justice Department each said that month that they had no record of receiving it, and the desk prints the appeal and the denial of receipt together, as the record holds them. Jean-Paul Sartre called the trial a legal lynching which smears with blood a whole nation. French President Vincent Auriol made representations to Eisenhower directly. The State Department tracked protest in 48 countries. Eisenhower denied clemency on February 11, 1953, in a written statement: by their act these two individuals have in fact betrayed the cause of freedom for which free men are fighting and dying at this very hour.

The last forty-eight hours were a fight over which statute had sentenced them. A Tennessee lawyer, Fyke Farmer, retained by neither Rosenberg, argued that atomic secrets were governed by the Atomic Energy Act of 1946, which permitted death only on a jury's recommendation, and that no jury had recommended it here. Kaufman refused to hear the interlopers. On June 17, 1953, Justice William O. Douglas granted a stay until the question could be determined in the lower courts. Chief Justice Fred Vinson convened the Supreme Court in special term the next day, over Justice Black's objection to the convening, and at noon on June 19 the Court, six to three, vacated the stay in a per curiam the desk quotes in full substance: we think the question is not substantial; a conspiracy was charged and proved to violate the Espionage Act in wartime; the Atomic Energy Act did not repeal or limit the provisions of the Espionage Act. Justice Frankfurter afterward called the proceeding the most disturbing single experience I have had during my term of service on the Court. Less than an hour later Eisenhower denied clemency a second time, in words the New York Times reported: by immeasurably increasing the chances of atomic war, the Rosenbergs may have condemned to death tens of millions of innocent people all over the world; when in their most solemn judgement the tribunals of the United States have adjudged them guilty and the sentence just, I will not intervene in this matter.

The executions were moved three hours early so that they would not fall on the Sabbath. Sing Sing's chair ordinarily worked at 11 p.m.; June 19, 1953, was a Friday, and 11 p.m. falls after sundown. On the defense's request, with Kaufman's stated concern and the Justice Department's agreement, the hour was advanced to 8 p.m. The prison was a New York State facility used because the federal system operated no electric chair. The executioner was Joseph Francel. Julius Rosenberg was pronounced dead at 8:06 p.m. Ethel Rosenberg received the normal course of three shocks; the attendants unstrapped her, the doctors found her heart still beating, and two more shocks were applied, five in the count carried by the strongest summaries and by a Supreme Court dissent's citation of the record, with eyewitnesses reporting smoke rising from her head. She was pronounced dead at 8:16 p.m. They were the first American civilians executed for espionage, and they remain the only ones executed for it in the Cold War.

8. The Antisemitism Question, in Both Directions

Of all the claims carried by this case, the antisemitism claim is the one the desk can document least as event and most as structure, and it is printed here as the attributed argument it is, in both directions, with the undisputed facts stated first. The undisputed facts: Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, Judge Kaufman, U.S. Attorney Saypol, prosecutor Cohn, defense counsel Emanuel Bloch, the Greenglasses, and Morton Sobell were Jewish; nearly every principal in the courtroom was. The jury of twelve, eleven men and one woman, one of them Black, contained no Jewish juror, in a city whose population was roughly a quarter to a third Jewish, and the teaching record built on the Federal Judicial Center's materials notes that both prosecution and defense struck Jewish veniremen.

The overcompensation argument, in the words of those who make it: Robert Meeropol, the younger son, states that antisemitism came out in subtle ways, that the judge and the prosecutors had to prove their loyalty and show they weren't siding with my parents because they were Jewish, so they were overzealous and harsh; he calls it reverse antisemitism. Michael Meeropol, the elder, holds the same position with a limit attached, and the desk prints the limit with the claim: they were not arrested because they were Jewish; he believes antisemitism played a role in getting Kaufman, Saypol, and Cohn to work extra hard to convict his parents, and in Kaufman's wish to appear strong by sentencing them to death, proving to the gentile population that there were good Jews who would take care of the dangerous Communists. A juror interviewed in 1975 told the family it was good that the Jews were taking care of the dangerous traitor Jews. The academic form of the argument holds that the government appointed a Jewish judge and a mainly Jewish prosecution team with the apparent intention of neutralizing Jewish-related issues, choosing men who embodied an anti-Communist Jewish consensus, and that Kaufman's public synagogue attendance before sentencing set a patriotic religious Jew against the Rosenbergs' Communist loyalty. The communal record of those years runs in the same channel: the major Jewish organizations campaigned to dissociate American Jews from Communism, testified that Judaism and Communism are utterly incompatible, accused the defense committee of exploiting the case, and feared, in Edward Shapiro's summary, that the trial would be a godsend to anti-Semites; a 1953 American Jewish Committee staff pamphlet supported the death sentence. J. Edgar Hoover, for his part, stated publicly that Jews were not disproportionately represented in the Communist Party, which the scholarship prints as the government's own awareness of the case's charge.

The counter-argument, at the same weight: the standard history hostile to the Rosenbergs, Radosh and Milton's The Rosenberg File, discusses the Jewish aspects in greater detail than any other and largely dismisses their significance; the Meeropols' own books relegated their parents' Jewishness to the margins of the case; and contemporaries answered the antisemitism charge by noting it was pressed hardest while the Soviet bloc ran antisemitic campaigns of its own, the Doctors' Plot among them, a paradox the defense never escaped. On the arrest itself there is no dispute in the file, including from the sons: the case began in the Venona cables and in Greenglass's identification of his brother-in-law, not in any selection of Jews. What the desk could not find is as much a part of this section as what it found: no reputable source in the file documents a specific antisemitic incident, a slur in a named newspaper's trial coverage or an act of violence, with a verifiable citation. The claim that the case was an antisemitic proceeding is an interpretation of documented pressures. The claim that it was untouched by the Jewishness of everyone in the room is an interpretation of the same record. The record carries the pressures; the interpretations are their owners'.

9. What the Files Changed, 1975–2024

The case the public argued about for forty years was argued in the dark, and the lights came on in a documented sequence, each stage of which moved the argument. On February 20, 1975, Michael and Robert Meeropol filed Freedom of Information requests for the whole file of their parents' case; they sued on July 14, 1975, and over the following decade the agencies retrieved some 500,000 pages and released some 200,000. The FBI memoranda in those pages are the source of the lever, of Hoover's sentencing recommendation, and of the Kaufman contacts printed above. In July 1995 the National Security Agency made the first public release of the Venona decrypts, 49 messages on the atomic effort; six releases in all made public approximately 3,000 translations, the agency's current figure, about 2,900 in its earlier wording. Venona settled Julius. It identified ANTENNA and LIBERAL, traced the ring, and contained, for Ethel, two mentions and no covername. On September 11, 2008, the National Archives opened the grand jury transcripts under court order in the historians' and journalists' suit, 40 witnesses that day and 43 of 46 in the Archives' later summary of the order; Ruth Greenglass's testimony, with its longhand and its absent typing, was among them, David's withheld because he lived and objected. That same day, Morton Sobell, 91 years old, told the New York Times what he had denied for 57 years. Yeah, yeah, yeah, call it that, he said, of spying. Of the Greenglass atomic material he used the word junk, of value only to corroborate other sources; of Julius he confirmed the conspiracy; of Ethel he said the sentence quoted in section four, adding a letter to the paper a week later denying he knew anything of Julius's atomic activities. In 2009 the Wilson Center published Alexander Vassiliev's 1,115 pages of notebooks from the KGB archive, the basis of Spies, which deepened the record against Julius, added McNutt, and supplied the counter-reading on Ethel that her defenders must answer. On July 15, 2015, David Greenglass's grand jury testimony was released under Judge Hellerstein's order of May 19, David having died the year before; the last withheld transcript, William Danziger's, followed in 2017. In 2024 the Gardner memorandum closed the sequence to date: the government's own codebreaker, eleven days after the arrest, writing that she did not engage in the work herself, in a document the government classified for seventy-four years and the defense never saw.

10. The Case in November 2026

The occasion of this report is brief, and the desk prints it at the size it occupies in the record. On the night of Monday, November 2, 2026, at a live taping of the Pivot podcast with Kara Swisher, Scott Galloway, a professor at New York University, said of Elon Musk: Elon Musk is a traitor to the West. We lined up the Rosenbergs against a wall. He added that Musk should be held to the same standards as every other traitor in the West. On November 3, on his own Prof G Pod, he said it again in fuller form: we took the Rosenbergs and put them up against a wall and executed them for being traitors, and I realize this is an incendiary statement, but I'll make it, and I believe it. I think Elon Musk is a traitor to the West. The context, on the reporting, was the restriction of Starlink service in Ukraine. The coverage, TMZ's and Fox's among it, carried the correction the desk also carries: the Rosenbergs were not lined up against a wall. They were strapped into an electric chair in a state prison in Ossining, New York, on June 19, 1953, and Galloway's standard for traitors rests on a manner of death that did not occur. TMZ noted he did not explicitly call for Musk's execution; the physicist Brian Keating reported on November 10 that Galloway told him he opposes capital punishment; the Megyn Kelly program of November 10 treated the remarks as a call for execution. The three characterizations stand in the file together. On November 12, Mediaite reported that Bill O'Reilly had called for the prosecution of Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, amid allegations of a Chinese espionage affair, and had drawn a parallel to Julius and Ethel Rosenberg; the desk verified the underlying broadcast segment and O'Reilly's prosecution argument, and prints the Rosenberg parallel as Mediaite's report, his exact words not having been found in any transcript the desk could open.

The other November news of the case was the case itself, still open. On November 5, 2026, Bloomberg's Disclosure podcast published an episode titled, as a question, Did Roy Cohn Frame Ethel Rosenberg?, in which Michael and Robert Meeropol discussed the Gardner memorandum and the fifty-year Freedom of Information campaign behind it. The campaign's current state is a matter of dated record. The sons petitioned President Obama, with 60,000 signatures, and he left office in January 2017, in Robert Meeropol's words, without ever acknowledging the request. The Gardner memorandum relaunched the campaign for President Biden; the Office of the Pardon Attorney requested the campaign's materials from Representative McGovern's office, which is to say the request was considered, and Biden left office in January 2025 without acting and without explaining. Representative McGovern apologized to the family on the House floor on February 10, 2025, in a member's speech, which is the only official federal acknowledgment in the file. New York City's Council had stated in a proclamation of September 28, 2015, Ethel's hundredth birthday, that the government wrongfully executed Ethel Rosenberg; the count of its signers is given as thirteen by the Rosenberg Fund for Children and eleven in other summaries, and the desk prints the proclamation, not the count. The Fund marked the seventy-third anniversary of the executions on June 19, 2026, with the campaign still open. No exoneration, pardon, or official revision of the verdict against Ethel Rosenberg has issued from any administration. Julius's espionage is established by two hostile archives that agree with each other. Ethel's execution rests on testimony her own brother withdrew. Both statements are in the record, and the record, seventy-three years after Sing Sing, is where the argument still lives.

Where the Sources Disagree

The desk prints its cocked hat. On the value of the Greenglass material, the file holds corroboration of real worth (Kramer, from the Soviet atomic project's own volumes), the first news of implosion (Rhodes), limited corroboration at best (Kurchatov, as read from the same Soviet documents), and a secret of nothing (the 1966 scientists, as reported by Michael Meeropol); it holds unanimity only on the ranking below Fuchs and Hall. On Ethel's participation, it holds the Venona and Gardner record, no covername, does not work, did not engage in the work herself, against the Vassiliev record as read by Klehr, Haynes, and Kramer, an active participant in the network, rightly convicted of the conspiracy and wrongly executed. On the character of the execution, it holds peacetime, in the institutional summaries, against wartime, in the sentencing judge's own opinion, which adds that he had no power to impose death for the peacetime offense. On the judge's conduct, it holds the Federal Judicial Center's finding that Kaufman solicited privately what he denied soliciting publicly, and Cohn's denial of illegal ex parte discussion beside his boast that the sentence was his recommendation, and a reported bar association exoneration of both men, and the unresolved question of whether the Justice Department was prepared to recommend death at all. On Ruth Greenglass's bargain, it holds no formal deal, in the Judicial Center's history, against on the condition, in the historians' court filing, with agreement only on what the bargain bought. On small facts the file disagrees with itself in the open: Harry Gold's release, 1965 against 1966; the Venona corpus, about 2,900 against approximately 3,000; one word of Kaufman's sentencing peroration; the number of shocks that killed Ethel Rosenberg, five on the strongest count, after the agreed fact that her heart was beating when the normal course ended; the Gardner memorandum's release, received in August 2024 on one account, publicly announced on September 10 on another; the signers of the 2015 proclamation, thirteen against eleven. None of these disagreements is resolved here, because the record does not resolve them, and a newspaper that rounds a disputed fact into print has stopped reporting it.
