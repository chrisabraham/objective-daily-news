The Hunger Games, Filed From the Record

Objective

The Politics of The Hunger Games: The Author’s Record, the Text, and the Fight Over Its Name

The sixth Hunger Games film, Sunrise on the Reaping, opened Friday to an estimated $120 million in North America and $225 million worldwide, and the argument over what the story means opened with it: an actress in the film called it “anti-fascist, populist art”; a commentator with a large online following answered that the story’s readers had not read the same book; protesters in France performed the series’ rebel song in the street during its first week. This report sets out the documented record in the order the evidence stands: what the author has said she built, what the text contains, the peace settlement the text builds on and the historical peace settlement printed beside it, the Capitol’s own stated bargain as the films depict it, what scholars and historians have made of it, what movements have done with it, and what is now being said about it. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0800 ET, Monday, November 23, 2026.

The sixth Hunger Games film opened Friday and the argument over what the story means opened with it. The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, directed by Francis Lawrence and adapted from Suzanne Collins’s 2025 novel, took an estimated $120 million in North America and $225 million worldwide in its first weekend, according to Rentrak estimates reported Sunday—the fifth film in the series to open above $100 million, and Lionsgate’s largest opening of the year. Joseph Zada plays the young Haymitch Abernathy; Ralph Fiennes plays President Snow; Maya Hawke plays the victor Wiress. Critics were divided—the film’s Rotten Tomatoes critics’ score stood near 70 percent in its first readings and 67 percent by Sunday—while the verified audience score stood at 95 percent, the highest in the franchise.

In the days around the opening, the franchise’s politics were claimed, in public and by name, for incompatible positions. An actress in the film called it “anti-fascist, populist art”; a commentator with a large online following answered that the story’s readers had not read the same book; protesters in France performed the series’ rebel song in the street during its first week. This report sets out the documented record in the order the evidence stands: what the author has said she built, what the text contains, the peace settlement the text builds on and the historical peace settlement printed beside it, the Capitol’s own stated bargain as the films depict it, what scholars and historians have made of it, what movements have done with it, and what is now being said about it. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

What the author said she was doing

Collins has described her sources in interviews with her publisher and with School Library Journal since 2008, and the account has not changed. The first source she names is the Greek myth of Theseus and the Minotaur, in which Athens, as punishment, periodically sent seven youths and seven maidens to Crete to be devoured. “Even as a kid, I could appreciate how ruthless this was,” Collins told Scholastic. “Crete was sending a very clear message: ‘Mess with us and we’ll do something worse than kill you. We’ll kill your children.’ And the thing is, it was allowed; the parents sat by powerless to stop it.” Katniss Everdeen, she said, is “in her own way a futuristic Theseus.”

The Games themselves she describes as “an updated version of the Roman gladiator games, which entails a ruthless government forcing people to fight to the death as popular entertainment.” The country’s name is taken from the same shelf: “Panem itself comes from the expression ‘Panem et Circenses,’ which translates into ‘Bread and Circuses’”—the phrase from Juvenal’s Satires for a populace kept compliant with food and spectacle.

The modern source was television. “I was channel surfing between reality TV programming and actual war coverage when Katniss’s story came to me,” Collins said. “One night I’m sitting there flipping around and on one channel there’s a group of young people competing for, I don’t know, money maybe? And on the next, there’s a group of young people fighting in an actual war. I was really tired, and the lines between these stories started to blur in a very unsettling way.”

Behind the war coverage stands her father. Collins told Scholastic that the trilogy’s subject matter—“severe poverty, starvation, oppression, and the effects of war”—was “probably my dad’s influence. He was career Air Force, a military specialist, a historian, and a doctor of political science.” Britannica identifies him as Lt. Col. Michael J. Collins, who spent a year on active duty in Vietnam, had recurring nightmares about the war for the rest of his life, and taught his children war and its consequences as a deliberate course. Collins has said the historical figure of Spartacus—the gladiator who led a slave revolt against Rome—“really becomes more of a model for the arc of the three books, for Katniss.”

In her tenth-anniversary interview with publisher David Levithan, Collins stated the frame in terms of just war theory—what gives a people the moral right to wage war, and what conduct is permitted inside one. “In The Hunger Games Trilogy, the districts rebel against their own government because of its corruption,” she said. “The citizens of the districts have no basic human rights, are treated as slave labor, and are subjected to the Hunger Games annually. I believe the majority of today’s audience would define that as grounds for revolution. They have just cause but the nature of the conflict raises a lot of questions.” In the interviews on record, Collins names her targets as a ruthless government, war as spectacle, and the corruption of the state the districts rebel against. She does not name a modern ideology as the Capitol’s model.

What the text builds

Panem, as the novels construct it, is a single wealthy metropolis set over a ring of captive producers. The Capitol sits in the Rocky Mountains. Twelve districts surround it, each assigned one industry—coal, agriculture, fishing, textiles, power, lumber, transport, and the rest—with nearly all production shipped to the Capitol. District residents may not travel to other districts or leave their own; the districts are fenced and policed. The Capitol’s enforcement arm, the Peacekeepers, serves twenty-year terms under a ban on marriage, and is recruited principally from the Capitol and from District 2, the masonry district that also houses the Capitol’s weapons production and its mountain fortress, the Nut—a fact concealed from the other districts, to whom District 2 is presented as stone quarries only. District 2 was rewarded with better living conditions for its loyalty in the first rebellion and is the last district to defect in the second.

Katniss’s home, District 12, is the coal district, and the text places it in what was once Appalachia. Its population divides between the Seam, the miners’ quarter where Katniss’s family lives in poverty, and a merchant class of shopkeepers—a two-tier structure the geographer Tom Paradis of Butler University, in his study A Place Called District 12, identifies as the segregated reality of historical Appalachian coal towns.

The trilogy’s second state is the one most often left out of summaries. District 13, believed destroyed, survives underground as a militarized society: uniformed, rationed, scheduled, its citizens assigned duties and meals by the state. In Mockingjay, Katniss finds her Capitol prep team—Flavius, Venia, and Octavia, stylists imprisoned by District 13 for taking extra bread—chained and beaten in a cell, and secures their release. District 13’s president, Alma Coin, leads the rebellion; at the war’s end she declares herself interim president, declines to hold the promised election, and proposes a final, symbolic Hunger Games using the children of Capitol leaders, put to a vote of the surviving victors. Katniss votes yes—in exchange for the right to execute Snow—and at the execution, having concluded that Coin ordered the bombing that killed her sister, she turns her arrow on Coin instead. The trilogy’s last political act is the killing of the revolution’s own president by the revolution’s own symbol.

The 2020 prequel, The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes, fills in the Capitol’s internal order. Status there runs by bloodline. The Snows are an old Capitol family whose fortune, invested in munitions factories in District 13, was destroyed in the war; the young Coriolanus Snow lives in the family penthouse in concealed poverty, maintaining appearances while his grandmother repeats that “Snow lands on top.” Against the old names—Snow, Heavensbee, Crane, Cardew—the novel sets the Plinths, a District 2 munitions family that bought its way into the Capitol after the war; Strabo Plinth funds the Academy’s rebuilding, and his son Sejanus, Capitol-educated, is treated by classmates as an outsider who is not Capitol-born. The Capitol of the prequel is an aristocracy of inherited names into which new money may purchase entry but not acceptance.

The punitive peace: two settlements in the record

The order described above rests, in the text, on a peace settlement. In the first novel, the history is read aloud in District 12 before the reaping, and Katniss repeats its terms: “Then came the Dark Days, the uprising of the districts against the Capitol. Twelve were defeated, the thirteenth obliterated. The Treaty of Treason gave us the new laws to guarantee peace and, as our yearly reminder that the Dark Days must never be repeated, it gave us the Hunger Games.” The treaty’s operative clause, as the series presents it, is a tribute levied as penance: in penance for their uprising, each district shall offer up a male and a female between the ages of 12 and 18 at a public reaping, to be delivered to the custody of the Capitol and transferred to a public arena to fight to the death until a lone victor remains. The settlement is therefore not a fine, a territorial cession, or a disarmament schedule. Its recurring payment is children, collected annually, and its stated purpose in the text is remembrance—the defeated are to be reminded, every year, of the rebellion and its outcome.

A second punitive peace stands in the historical record, and the desk prints its terms beside the first without joining them. The Treaty of Versailles, signed June 28, 1919, ended the First World War between Germany and the Allied powers. Its Article 231, known as the war guilt clause, reads in part: “The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies.” Article 231 supplied the stated basis for reparations; the sum was left open in the treaty and was set in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks. The treaty also stripped Germany of territory and colonies and restricted its armed forces.

Two contemporary judgments on that settlement are also matters of record, and they point in opposite directions. Marshal Ferdinand Foch, the Allied supreme commander, considered Versailles too lenient, because it did not permanently detach the Rhineland from Germany. A remark widely attributed to Foch on the treaty is quoted in Paul Reynaud’s Mémoires (1963) as: “This is not a peace. It is an armistice for twenty years.” The attribution itself is part of the record: Winston Churchill also attributed the remark to Foch, and the reference literature describes Churchill’s version as famous but apocryphal. John Maynard Keynes, who had served at the Paris Peace Conference as the British Treasury’s chief representative, resigned from the British delegation in June 1919 and published The Economic Consequences of the Peace in December of that year, arguing against the treaty’s reparations settlement. The Second World War began in September 1939, twenty years and two months after the treaty was signed. The two settlements, the fictional and the historical, stand in this report as parallel exhibits. No published scholarly analysis drawing a structural comparison between the Treaty of Treason and the Treaty of Versailles was found in the desk’s search for this report, and the report draws no such comparison itself.

The text also records where the second rebellion first appears, and in what order. In Catching Fire, the Victory Tour’s first stop is District 11, where an old man in the crowd whistles Rue’s four-note signal, the crowd raises the three-finger salute, and the old man and two others are executed. After the tour, Katniss learns from a broadcast in the mayor’s house, and then from two refugees, Bonnie and Twill, that District 8 has risen and its uprising has failed; later in the same novel she learns that Districts 3 and 4 have also risen. District 12, in the same period, receives a harsher Peacekeeper force and the public whipping of Gale Hawthorne, but no District 12 uprising is reported in the novel. That is the sequence the text gives. The desk found no published analysis attributing that sequence to the relative prosperity of Districts 8 and 11, and District 11 is not established in the text as a better-off district; the claim is therefore not printed here as an interpretation, only the sequence itself.

The protection bargain, as the Capitol states it

The Capitol’s case for the order above is stated, in the films, by the Capitol itself. In the “Together as One” address released June 25, 2014, as the first teaser for The Hunger Games: Mockingjay—Part 1, President Snow, played by Donald Sutherland, addresses the districts with the captive victor Peeta Mellark standing beside him: “Since the Dark Days our nation has known only peace. Ours is an elegant system, conceived to nourish and protect. Your districts are the body, the Capitol is the beating heart. Your hard work feeds us, and in return, we feed and protect you. But if you resist the system, you starve yourself. If you fight against it, it is you who will bleed. I know you will stand with me, with us, with all of us, together as one.” The address closes with the slogan: “Panem today. Panem tomorrow. Panem forever.”

The slogan’s documented origin is that film address of 2014. It was subsequently used in the marketing of The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes—it appears as a title on that film’s soundtrack disc, “Panem today, panem tomorrow, panem forever: together as one”—but the desk could not verify the phrase as a line of the 2020 Ballad novel itself, and it is not attributed to the novel in this report. One commentary on the 2014 address, at UInterview, noted a resemblance in form to Alabama Governor George Wallace’s 1963 inaugural pledge of “segregation today, segregation tomorrow, segregation forever”; the resemblance is recorded here as that commentary stated it.

The protection claim is also argued inside the Ballad novel, against the Games. In the passage summarized by LitCharts, Sejanus Plinth, the District 2-born student in the Capitol, objects to Dr. Volumnia Gaul that the tributes are citizens of Panem, that the anthem describes a Capitol that protects, and asks: “Isn’t that what the anthem says the Capitol does? ‘You give us light. You reunite’? It’s supposed to be everyone’s government, right? … Well, then it should protect everyone. That’s its number-one job! And I don’t see how making them fight to the death achieves that.” The bargain and the objection to it are both in the record the series itself supplies.

The historical shelf

Two bodies of documented history are regularly set beside the fiction by analysts, and both are matters of record independent of it.

The first is Appalachian. The coal country Collins’s District 12 mirrors was, within living memory of the novels’ setting, a company order: absentee owners—HistoryNet’s account of the West Virginia mine wars names investors from New York, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Ohio, and Britain—bought the land and mineral rights, housed miners in company towns, and policed them through private detective agencies, principally the Baldwin-Felts, whose operatives the miners called “gun thugs.” The order was challenged at the Battle of Blair Mountain in August and September 1921, the largest armed labor uprising in American history, in which striking miners fought sheriff’s deputies, mine guards, and militia for four days while bombs were dropped on them from private airplanes, before federal troops intervened. Paradis’s study connects District 12’s design—its class structure, its company-town geography, its musical culture—to this Appalachian record.

The second is Berlin. The published comparisons between Panem and interwar Germany run, in the record, through the films’ own design departments. Producer Nina Jacobson has said she compared the era of The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes to Weimar Germany and hired the German production designer Uli Hanisch—of Babylon Berlin—to rebuild the Capitol, shooting in Berlin’s Olympiastadion and the Centennial Hall in Wrocław, structures that “make the humans feel small by design.” Director Francis Lawrence has said the Capitol’s look in the earlier films drew on the monumental Berlin—Germania—that Hitler planned and never built, and the prequel’s Capitol on Berlin in the reconstruction years after 1945. Hanisch, returning for Sunrise on the Reaping, told Wallpaper* the designers compared Panem’s history to Berlin fifteen years after the Second World War, with its layers of Prussian, Nazi, and Soviet building.

The electoral geography of the republic that preceded that Berlin is also documented. The historian Dick Geary’s survey of the voting record, published in History Today, shows the Nazi Party rising from 2.6 percent of the vote in 1928 to 37.3 percent in July 1932, with its first breakthroughs and its strongest support in Protestant rural areas—the farming communities of Schleswig-Holstein and Lower Saxony—polling almost twice as heavily in Protestant as in Catholic districts; working-class “Red Berlin” and Westphalia were, throughout, among the party’s weakest ground. Nazi rhetoric against the capital is in the primary record: Joseph Goebbels, the party’s Berlin leader, wrote in Der Angriff in January 1928 of the Kurfürstendamm’s cafés and cabarets as the seat of an “asphalt democracy,” calling Berlin a “pit of iniquity” and a “stone desert”; in his speech at the Berlin book burning of May 10, 1933, he consigned metropolitan writing to the category of “asphalt literature”—literature, in the lexicon’s later definition, “no longer rooted in its homeland.”

What the scholars have made of it

The fullest academic treatment of the franchise’s politics is Rebecca Nell Hill’s “Capital or the Capitol?: The Hunger Games Fandom and Neoliberal Populism,” published in American Studies (57.1/2), the journal of the Mid-America American Studies Association. Based on interviews with fans and a survey of reader commentary, Hill argues that readers who agree on nothing else converge on the series, and that the convergence marks a shift in American populism toward an anti-statist framing of inequality she calls “neoliberal populism.” Hill documents three standing reader interpretations, distributed across the political map: liberals and progressives who read the series as an interracial struggle of the “have-nots” against the wealthy “haves”; conservatives and radical white nationalists who read it as a rural struggle against totalitarian and cosmopolitan “liberal fascism”; and revolutionary third-worldists who read its characters as anti-colonial rebels against the American empire. The scholarly record, in other words, does not show a settled reading contested by newcomers; it shows three incompatible readings, each with years of standing, held simultaneously by the book’s own audience.

A definitional objection has been published against the most common label. An essay at SpeakFreely, “The Real Enemy in The Hunger Games,” argues—on the political scientist Roger Griffin’s widely used definition of fascism as palingenetic ultranationalism, a myth of national rebirth—that the Capitol cannot qualify: Panem has no other nations to be ultranationalist against, no rebirth myth, and no racism, leaving what the essay calls the visual form of National Socialism minus the nationalism. The essay’s own thesis—that the text’s real subject is communism—is a further reading, not a consensus; it is recorded here as part of the field. The general problem of the word was stated by George Orwell in Tribune in 1944, in the essay “What is Fascism?”: “It will be seen that, as used, the word ‘Fascism’ is almost entirely meaningless. In conversation, of course, it is used even more wildly than in print. I have heard it applied to farmers, shopkeepers, Social Credit, corporal punishment, fox-hunting, bull-fighting, the 1922 Committee, the 1941 Committee, Kipling, Gandhi, Chiang Kai-shek, homosexuality, Priestley’s broadcasts, Youth Hostels, astrology, women, dogs and I do not know what else.” Orwell concluded that “bully” was about as near a synonym as the word had come; in 1946, in “Politics and the English Language,” he restated the point: the word “has now no meaning except in so far as it signifies ‘something not desirable.’”

The appropriation record

Whatever the story means, what has been done with it is a matter of dated record. The three-finger salute—in the fiction, an old District 12 sign of respect that becomes the rebellion’s mark—was taken up by protesters in Thailand within days of the military coup of May 2014. The junta announced that those displaying it would be arrested; that November, students who raised it at screenings of Mockingjay—Part 1 in Bangkok were escorted out and detained by plainclothes police. Thai protesters glossed the three fingers as liberty, equality, fraternity. The salute was used in Hong Kong’s Umbrella Movement in 2014 and again in 2019–20. After the Myanmar coup of February 1, 2021, medical workers at Yangon General Hospital posed raising it, and it spread through the civil disobedience movement. On October 28, 2026—Youth Pledge Day—students across Indonesia raised it in demonstrations over corruption, economic pressure, and the government’s handling of forest fires, under banners carrying the series’ line, “If we burn, you burn with us.”

In the United States, the appropriation ran through labor. In the Black Friday protests of 2014, the fan-activist Harry Potter Alliance’s “Odds in Our Favor” campaign joined the OUR Walmart campaign and the Fight for $15: Walmart and fast-food workers raised the three-finger salute at rallies and sang rewritten versions of “The Hanging Tree,” the rebel song from Mockingjay. “The issues brought to life by the Hunger Games—economic inequality and hunger—are realities for millions of Americans including those with full-time jobs at places like Walmart and McDonalds,” the Alliance’s Andrew Slack said at the time.

The record is current. During the French cost-of-living protests of November 2026, the Orchestre du Nouveau Monde performed “The Hanging Tree” at the Place de la République on November 5, ahead of a march, and orchestras performed it again in Paris on November 21—the new film’s opening weekend—with crowds singing and raising the three fingers, in footage carried by Reuters. Director Francis Lawrence, asked about the French performances, said: “The fact that this song resonates with people—the way it fits into these stories—and that people can take it and imbue it with even greater meaning, is very moving. But it’s also truly surreal.”

The labeling fight of November 2026

The present dispute began on the film’s press circuit. Asked by Variety how the film’s political themes spoke to her, Maya Hawke—who plays Wiress—said: “I think that one of the extraordinary things about The Hunger Games is that it’s anti-fascist, populist art, that is also popular.” She added that the work “informs children on how to think about change.” In a parallel interview with Cosmopolitan, she stated her thesis as the corruption of power—“It’s not the person in power; it’s what power does to the person”—and said: “To make something that is both popular and anti-fascist is pretty crazy. What the books and films are doing is like protest art in a pretty explicit way.” The A.V. Club, writing on the franchise’s “young adult” label, endorsed her formulation as “a much better descriptor.”

The answer came from the conservative commentator Isabel Brown, in a video published November 19, 2026, titled “I Don’t Think We Read The Same Hunger Games, TBH.” Brown’s stated description of Collins’s book, in the video’s own framing, is a critique of “centralized government authority and totalitarianism.” She disputed the application of the word fascism to the story’s politics and to its contemporary targets, arguing that the Capitol represents an elite set over the districts—in her posts accompanying the video, an elite of wealth and culture against the producing districts—and that the story’s warning is aimed at concentrated power rather than at any one modern party. Commentary at Geeks+Gamers, disputing Hawke’s label from the other side of the same question, pointed to the text’s second state: District 13 under Coin, “the rigidly controlled society,” as evidence the franchise “warns against more than one kind of tyranny.”

Each reading’s strongest documented basis is in the record above. For the anti-fascist reading: a one-man dictatorship maintained by militarized police, public execution, and spectacle, its capital built by the films’ designers from Nazi Berlin’s monumental architecture and Weimar-era reference, chosen deliberately. For the populist-against-an-elite reading: an economy of extraction by a hereditary, consuming metropolis from disarmed producing districts; an author whose stated sources are Rome, Theseus, and Spartacus; and a climax in which the heroine refuses the revolution’s own successor regime. For the definitional objection: fascism, on the scholarly definition most often cited, requires a nationalism Panem structurally lacks, and Orwell’s warning about the word is eighty-two years old. The readings are incompatible. The record supports each in part and none in whole, and the record is what this paper prints.

The decadence question

A separate criticism, in print since the first films, concerns not the Capitol’s politics but its portrayal. Writing in Flavorwire at the close of the original film series in 2015, one critic observed that the Capitol citizens’ indifference is “symbolized by their bodily adornment, which is coded as a sort of gender transgression; men and women coloring their hair and shaving parts of their heads, and making themselves unrecognizable and androgynous,” set against districts where “the women don prairie dresses and the guys wear pants.” Halperin continued: “The fact that the noble districts all seem to be populated by family-oriented heterosexuals while the capitol is effete and swinging provides a disturbing, if probably accidental, subtext.” The same essay noted the exception the films make: Cinna, Katniss’s stylist, played by Lenny Kravitz “in a particularly understated manner,” becomes in the essay’s phrase the “‘good’ effeminate character, in opposition to the campier Capitol denizens”—the announcer Caesar Flickerman and President Snow, “delightful preening baddies, but preening baddies nonetheless.” The academic version of the argument appears in Susana Rocha Teixeira’s “Troubled Contemporary Masculinities in The Hunger Games” (ffk Journal, 2017), which holds that beneath a progressive surface the series expresses “nostalgia and a desire for ‘conventional’ masculinities such as the working class hero.”

The visual vocabulary in question has a documented pedigree in European propaganda. Goebbels’s 1928 essay, cited above, fixed the type: the cosmopolitan capital of cafés, cabarets, stock tips, and foreign tongues, set against sixty million industrious provincials—“the spirit of the asphalt democracy.” The Soviet state’s version was the epithet “rootless cosmopolitan,” applied during the anti-cosmopolitan campaign of 1946–53 chiefly to Jewish intellectuals and artists, and now classified in the reference literature as an antisemitic trope. The charge that a decadent, deracinated, feminized metropolis preys on a virtuous hinterland was a standard instrument of twentieth-century regimes of more than one kind.

The counter-evidence is likewise in the text and its criticism. Cinna is not a decorative exception in the novels: he is the trilogy’s moral center among Capitol citizens—the designer of the mockingjay dress, beaten to death by Peacekeepers in front of Katniss for it, whose posthumous work arms the rebellion. The prep team Katniss rescues from District 13’s cells are Capitol citizens portrayed as victims of the revolution’s discipline, and Katniss defends them, in the CliffsNotes summary’s words, as “helpless children who didn’t want any harm to come to Katniss.” Tigris, a former stylist whose face has been surgically altered past the human, shelters Katniss’s squad in her shop at the war’s climax. And the newest book reverses the geography of sexual repression the criticism assumes: in Sunrise on the Reaping, as Out magazine’s survey of the series’ queer record notes, it is Haymitch who says of district life that “loving differently can get you harassed by Peacekeepers, fired from jobs, arrested even,” while the Capitol tolerates what the districts punish. The criticism and the counter-evidence stand in the record together. Neither is this paper’s verdict, because this paper does not issue one.

The state of the question

The franchise’s sixth film is in theaters. Its author’s stated intentions are on record and are classical: a tribute state, a slave revolt, bread and circuses, a just-war argument conducted for young readers. Its text contains two tyrannies and kills both of their heads. Its readers, on the scholarly evidence, have never agreed about it, and its symbols have been carried in the streets of Bangkok, Hong Kong, Yangon, Jakarta, Paris, and American picket lines by movements that agree about nothing else. The argument of November 2026 is the old case reopened on schedule by a new film, the exhibits unchanged and the labels contested. The exhibits are printed above.

Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader.
