A Participant’s History, Filed Against the Record—the Article, the Author, the Corps’ Vietnam Missions, and the Contested Dioxin Record

Objective

The U.S. Army Chemical Corps in Vietnam: George J. Hafner’s Article and the Record Around It — Special Report

On Monday, November 16, 2026, The Army Historical Foundation published “U.S. Army Chemical Corps Operations in Vietnam,” by Lieutenant Colonel George J. Hafner, Chemical Corps, United States Army, retired—a participant’s history of his own branch’s war, written from his service with the 1st Cavalry Division in Quang Tri Province in 1968 and 1969. Hafner’s argument is that the Chemical Corps’ Vietnam role has been narrowed in public memory to a single mission, defoliation, and that the narrowing misstates the record twice over: the Air Force flew the great majority of the spraying while the Corps developed, managed, and employed tactical herbicides in ground operations, and the larger part of the Corps’ war was a set of combat-support missions—riot control agents dropped from helicopters, personnel detectors flown at treetop level, flame expedients, tunnel denial—that his article catalogues from his own service and from the division’s operational reports. This report files the article itself and what it argues; the author, as the public record supports him; the documented record of the Corps’ Vietnam operations from official histories and the technical literature; the contested record printed as contested and counted by instrument, never blended—the dioxin science and its fights, the presumptive conditions the Department of Veterans Affairs pays on and the Acts of Congress that added them, the 1984 class settlement and what it paid, the Vietnamese claims and the courts that heard them, and the allied-veteran cases in Seoul and Canberra; and the questions the record does not settle, printed as open.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0930 ET, Tuesday, November 17, 2026.

The Record in Brief

The article. “U.S. Army Chemical Corps Operations in Vietnam” was published by The Army Historical Foundation on its website on Monday, November 16, 2026. Its author, Lieutenant Colonel George J. Hafner, Chemical Corps, USA-Ret., served in Vietnam from July 1968 to July 1969 with the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) in Quang Tri Province, first commanding the 26th Chemical Detachment and then serving as chemical officer of the division’s 2d Brigade. The article’s thesis is stated at its opening: the Corps’ Vietnam service is associated in public memory almost entirely with herbicide defoliation, above all Operation Ranch Hand and Agent Orange, and that association both overstates the Corps’ share of the spraying—the Air Force flew about 95 percent of the spray missions, on the figures Hafner cites from the Institute of Medicine—and understates everything else the Corps did. The body of the article is a catalogue of that everything else, division by division function and mission by mission, drawn from his own service, from the 1st Cavalry Division’s operational reports for 1968 and 1969, and from a 1971 study of chemical support in the division by the 14th Military History Detachment. It closes with two numbers: fewer than 60 officers and men on full-time chemical duties in a division of the 1st Cavalry’s size, and 39 Chemical Corps soldiers lost in the war, on the count of the U.S. Army Chemical Corps Museum’s 2012 study that Hafner cites.

The author. The Foundation’s biography, printed with the article in the present tense, describes Hafner as a retired career officer of the Chemical Corps. The article itself documents his Vietnam year in detail: the detachment command, the brigade staff post, the operations he arrived into, and the missions he personally flew, led, recommended, or oversaw, each of which this report files in Section 2 as his account. The desk searched for a death record and found none; an obituary exists for a George J. Hafner of New Jersey, a former Catholic priest who died in 1980, and the dates and the life are a different man’s—the officer was in Vietnam in the months the other man married—and the desk does not merge them. Beyond the article and its biography, the public record the desk could reach carries almost nothing about Hafner: no post-Vietnam assignments, no retirement date, no interviews. What prints about him in this report is what his article and its publisher support, and no more.

The documented record. The mission set Hafner describes is the mission set the official histories record. The Chemical Corps in Vietnam ran chemical defense and detection for a chemical attack that never came in confirmed form; supported airmobile operations with riot control agents delivered by helicopter and cluster munition; flew the XM-3 airborne personnel detector, the “people sniffer”; denied tunnels, caves, and bunkers with CS and explosives; built flame field expedients; and took part in the herbicide war at ground level—perimeter and base-area spraying, ground defoliation, and crop and food denial—while the Air Force’s UC-123s flew the fixed-wing program the record counts at 6,542 missions in the HERBS file. The herbicide totals stand in the literature as a ladder, not a number: about 19 million gallons sprayed between 1962 and 1971 on the National Academies’ accounting, about 11 million gallons of it Agent Orange; the 2003 revision by Stellman and his co-authors raised the total to about 77 million liters and the dioxin estimate to 366 kilograms, against the National Academy of Sciences’ 1974 estimate of 106 to 163 kilograms, and the committee charged with weighing the revision stated that it could validate neither side’s exposure model. Section 4 prints the ladder with the instrument named at every rung.

The contested record. Around the herbicide program stands a second record, fifty years long, of science, benefits law, and litigation, and it does not resolve into one account. The Department of Veterans Affairs pays presumptive service connection today for nineteen conditions associated with herbicide exposure, a list built by the Agent Orange Act of 1991 and enlarged by Congress in 2021 and again in the PACT Act of 2022, in several cases past the point the National Academies’ evidence categories had reached. The 1984 class settlement, reached hours before trial, paid $180 million from seven manufacturers without any admission of liability; the fund, with interest, ultimately distributed about $197 million to some 52,000 veterans and families, an average of about $3,800. The Vietnamese victims’ association’s suit against 37 companies was dismissed in 2005, the dismissal affirmed in 2008, and review denied by the Supreme Court in 2009; the Vietnamese figures for the exposed and the harmed—4.8 million exposed and about 3 million victims on the association’s count—stand in print as claims, with no United States official estimate beside them. South Korea’s courts, hearing their own veterans, first ordered payment and then, in 2013, sustained payment for 39 veterans with skin disease while sending the larger award back. Australia’s royal commission in 1985 found no link, in a report its veterans never accepted, and the Australian government acknowledged a link with cancer in 1994. The United States has spent more than $610 million since 2007 on dioxin remediation and health and disability assistance in Vietnam, without compensation and without an admission; the largest hotspot, at Bien Hoa airbase, is still being cleaned. Section 5 files each of these in its own forum, with its own numbers, unblended.

What does not settle. The record does not settle the individual question—whether a given veteran’s illness came from his exposure—because the National Academies’ framework answers a different question, association across populations, and says so. It does not settle the Vietnamese totals, which no instrument both sides accept has ever counted. It does not settle the ground-spray volumes, which the surviving mission files never captured, so every herbicide total in print is a floor under an unknown remainder. It does not settle the tonnage of CS the United States expended, for which published estimates differ by two thousand tons. And several of Hafner’s most specific episodes—the improvised napalm sling load that struck a Chinook’s rotor blades, the four hundred bags of captured rice he recommended contaminating in place, the malaria rates he reports rising after the spraying program’s suspension—rest on his testimony and the citations he gives for them, and the desk found no independent record against which to check them. They are printed in this report as his account, which is what they are. Section 7 lists the open questions in full.

1. The Article: What Hafner Argues

Publication. “U.S. Army Chemical Corps Operations in Vietnam” appeared on the website of The Army Historical Foundation on Monday, November 16, 2026. The byline reads: Lieutenant Colonel George J. Hafner, Chemical Corps, USA-Ret. The article is a single-author history of one branch’s war, written by an officer who served in it, and it carries fifteen endnotes. The sources Hafner cites are the record a participant would cite: the Institute of Medicine’s 1994 volume Veterans and Agent Orange; the 1st Cavalry Division’s Operational Reports—Lessons Learned for 1968 and 1969; the 14th Military History Detachment’s January 25, 1971 study, “Chemical Support within the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile)”; Field Manual 1-100 (1963); Albert Mauroni’s history of the Corps in Army History 99 (Spring 2016); Reid Kirby’s “Operation Snoopy” in the Army Chemical Review (January–June 2007); the Department of the Army’s 1971 study, “Employment of Riot Control Agents in Vietnam”; J. B. Neilands’s 1970 account of the chemical war; John E. Thiel’s 2012 U.S. Army Chemical Corps Museum study of the Corps’ dead; and the National Museum of the United States Army’s article on the tunnel rats. The article’s own source note records that its author consulted these materials in May 2026.

The thesis. Hafner’s argument is stated at the article’s opening and governs everything after it. In public memory, he writes, the Chemical Corps in Vietnam is the defoliation program: Operation Ranch Hand, Agent Orange, dioxin. Against that memory he sets two corrections. The first is a correction of share. The spraying the public remembers was flown overwhelmingly by the Air Force—about 95 percent of it, on the Institute of Medicine figures he cites—while the Corps, in his framing, developed, managed, and employed tactical herbicides in ground operations: perimeter spraying, ground defoliation, crop and food denial at the level of the division and the base camp. The second correction is a correction of scope. Defoliation of any kind was one mission among many. The bulk of the article is the catalogue of the others, and the catalogue is the argument: a branch remembered for one chemical is shown at work across the whole range of chemical support to airmobile infantry.

The catalogue. The missions Hafner describes, in the article’s own order of business, are these. Riot control agents delivered from the air: persistent CS dropped from CH-47 helicopters in loads of about twenty-four fifty-five-gallon drums at a lift, the drums bursting at about one hundred feet and the agent persisting on the ground for as long as thirty days; and the E-158 cluster bomb, a CS munition whose effects he gives at about ten minutes of incapacitation. Personnel detection: the XM-3 airborne detector, the “people sniffer,” which sampled the air for ammonia given off by human beings and registered it as an ammonium chloride cloud in its chamber, its readings coded at levels he gives as ALPHA through DELTA; Hafner describes flying the detector missions himself, at treetop height and eighty to ninety miles an hour, two detectors aboard, Cobra gunships engaging the targets the detector marked with smoke, and he cites field reporting for a success rate of 80 to 85 percent under favorable conditions. Flame: fougasse mines and Husch flares as field expedients, and flame in the defense of Landing Zone Grant in March 1969, on the operational reporting he cites. Improvised aerial napalm: drums of napalm sling-loaded under CH-47s and dropped on signal, a system he describes from his own unit’s practice, including the drop in which a load struck the helicopter’s own rotor blades. Ground herbicide work: defoliation and crop destruction at ground level, and mosquito control by malathion, whose aerial suspension late in 1969 he reports, on the attribution of the division chemical officer who followed him, Colonel Phillip O. Bauer, was followed by malaria rates rising from about 2 percent to about 15 percent. Tunnel, cave, and bunker denial: two-man teams working with demolition cord and bags of CS, blowers forcing the agent through the workings, in support of the tunnel rats. And food denial: a captured rice cache of about four hundred bags at about fifty pounds a bag, which Hafner recommended be contaminated in place rather than carried out—about one hundred bags of persistent CS worked into the rice in ten drums—a recommendation he records was approved by the division G-3 and carried out by a team of five.

The close. The article ends with the two numbers the In Brief carries. A division of the 1st Cavalry’s size, Hafner writes, kept fewer than sixty officers and men on full-time chemical duties. And the Corps’ dead in Vietnam, on Thiel’s 2012 count for the Chemical Corps Museum, which Hafner adopts, numbered thirty-nine. The article makes no claim beyond its catalogue and its correction: it does not argue the herbicide controversy, does not address the dioxin science, and does not mention the litigation or the benefits law that Sections 5 and 6 of this report file. Its subject is what the Corps did, and its contention is that the record of what the Corps did is larger than the one mission the public kept.

2. The Author

The biography as published. The Army Historical Foundation prints an “About the Author” block with the article, in the present tense. It describes George J. Hafner as a retired career officer of the United States Army Chemical Corps. The desk treats him as a living author: the biography is present tense, the article is dated November 16, 2026, and its source note records research access in May 2026. The desk searched for a death record and found none. One same-name obituary exists in print—a George J. Hafner of Medford, New Jersey, a former Catholic priest who married in May 1969 and died in 1980—and it is a different man’s life: in the months of that wedding the officer was in Vietnam. The two records are not merged in this report. Beyond the article and its biography, the reachable public record carries almost nothing further about Hafner: no post-Vietnam assignments, no retirement date, no interviews, no later writings that the desk could find. His rank during the Vietnam tour itself is not stated in the article or the biography; the rank in the byline is his retirement rank. What this section prints about him is what his article and its publisher support, and no more.

The tour. Hafner served in Vietnam from July 1968 to July 1969, with the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) in Quang Tri Province, in the northern part of South Vietnam. From July to October 1968 he commanded the 26th Chemical Detachment, the division’s chemical unit. From November 1968 to July 1969 he served as chemical officer of the division’s 2d Brigade. The two posts frame the article’s evidence: the detachment command put him over the men and equipment that flew and emplaced the division’s chemical munitions, and the brigade staff post put him where chemical support was planned and requested. The operations he describes at first hand date from those twelve months, in that division’s area, and the article does not claim a wider vantage.

His account, at full standing. The episodes in which Hafner appears as an actor, rather than as the catalogue’s compiler, are filed here as his account, which is the standing this report gives them throughout. He flew XM-3 detector missions at treetop level, at eighty to ninety miles an hour, and describes the drill: the detector’s reading, the smoke marker, the Cobra gunships working the marked target. He describes the improvised napalm system his unit rigged—drums sling-loaded beneath a CH-47 and released over the target—and the drop in which the load came up into the rotor blades, an incident he records against his own side’s practice. He records the rice cache: about four hundred bags of captured rice, his recommendation to the division that the rice be denied in place with persistent CS rather than lifted out, the G-3’s approval, and the five-man team that did the work. He records the flame defense at Landing Zone Grant in March 1969 on the operational reporting he cites. And he records, attributed to Colonel Bauer, the malaria figures that followed the malathion suspension—a suspension that came late in 1969, after Hafner’s own tour had ended in July, so that this last item reaches the article at one remove, and is printed here at one remove. The desk found no independent record against which to check the napalm system, the rotor-blade incident, the rice-cache specifics, or the detector’s success rate; Section 7 lists them among the open questions. They stand in this report as the testimony of the officer who was there, identified as such at every printing.

3. The Corps’ Vietnam Operations in the Documented Record

The mission set, corroborated. The catalogue in Hafner’s article is not a private recollection at variance with the official histories; it is the mission set those histories record for the Chemical Corps in Vietnam. The Corps’ first duty in country was the one that never fired: chemical defense—detection, protection, and decontamination held ready against a chemical attack that never came in confirmed form. Around that standing duty the Corps built the support missions the airmobile war demanded. Chemical units supplied and serviced the division’s riot control agents and flame weapons, flew and maintained the personnel detectors, ran the ground herbicide work at base and perimeter level, and put small teams into the tunnel systems with explosives and CS. The 14th Military History Detachment’s 1971 study of chemical support inside the 1st Cavalry Division, which Hafner cites, documents the same set from inside the same division, and the Department of the Army’s 1971 study of riot control agent employment documents the riot-control mission at theater level. Where Hafner’s article and the official record overlap, they agree on what the missions were. The disagreements in this file begin later, over the herbicides’ quantities and effects, and they are filed in Sections 4 and 5.

Riot control agents. CS—the agent the Army classed as a riot control agent and treated as nonlethal—was the Corps’ most expended munition in Vietnam, used to flush tunnels and bunkers, to deny ground, to cover extractions, and to drive enemy soldiers into the open under friendly guns. It was delivered by grenade, by blower, by cluster munition, and by the drum drops from helicopters that Hafner describes. The E-158 tactical CS cluster bomb, on the Borden Institute’s Medical Aspects of Chemical Warfare (chapter 13), carried 264 CS-filled canisters. No consolidated official total for CS expenditure in Vietnam was found in the record the desk consulted. The published estimates stand as a ladder: about 7,000 tons, the figure carried by D. Hank Ellison’s Chemical Warfare during the Vietnam War: Riot Control Agents in Combat and by Milton Leitenberg’s contemporary count, which set CS within some 90,000 tons of chemical agents of all kinds; and about 9,000 tons in a general science reference. The two-thousand-ton spread between the published estimates is itself part of the record, and Section 7 carries it as an open question. The record also carries the qualification on the agent’s classification: CS was classed nonlethal, and deaths were reported where it was used in heavy concentrations in confined spaces—tunnels and bunkers among them.

Detection. The XM-3 airborne personnel detector—the “people sniffer,” the subject of Reid Kirby’s “Operation Snoopy” in the Army Chemical Review, one of Hafner’s own citations—was the Corps’ answer to an enemy under canopy. Mounted in helicopters and flown low, it sampled the air for ammonia, a human signature, and converted the reading into the ammonium chloride indication Hafner describes. The detector’s documented role matches his account of its employment: flown at treetop level over suspected positions, its contacts marked for the gunships flying with it. What the wider record does not supply is a verified effectiveness figure; the 80 to 85 percent success rate in the article is the figure Hafner cites from field reporting under favorable conditions, and it is printed in this report as his cited figure and nothing more.

Tunnels and flame. The tunnel war put chemical soldiers underground by proxy: two-man teams, on Hafner’s account and on the National Museum of the United States Army’s history of the tunnel rats, which he cites, sealed and charged tunnel and bunker complexes with demolition cord and CS, and used blowers to drive the agent through workings too extensive to clear by hand, supporting the infantry volunteers—the tunnel rats—who entered what could be entered. Flame, the Corps’ oldest munition, appears in the record as Hafner presents it: largely a field expedient in Vietnam—fougasse mines, Husch flares, improvised aerial delivery—rather than a standard-issue system, and used in the perimeter fight, as at Landing Zone Grant in March 1969 on the operational reporting he cites. The improvised napalm delivery system itself, and the rotor-blade incident within it, rest on Hafner’s testimony; the desk found no corroboration in the official histories consulted, and prints them as his account, in Section 2.

Ground herbicide work and defense. At ground level, the Corps’ herbicide role in the documented record is the one Hafner claims for it and no larger: base-area and perimeter spraying, ground defoliation along lines of communication, and crop and food denial in the division’s area, alongside the insecticide work—malathion against the mosquito, in a theater where malaria was a standing consumer of infantry strength. The fixed-wing defoliation program that dominates public memory was the Air Force’s, and it is filed separately, in Section 4, because the record keeps it separately: different aircraft, different chain of command, and the only mission file—the HERBS file—from which the herbicide totals in print are built.

4. The Herbicide Program: Ranch Hand and the Ladder of Totals

The program. Operation Ranch Hand, the Air Force’s fixed-wing herbicide campaign, flew UC-123 aircraft on spray missions over South Vietnam from 1962 to 1971. On the Institute of Medicine’s accounting, the UC-123s sprayed 88 percent of all herbicides used; Hafner’s article, citing the same literature, puts the Air Force share at about 95 percent of the spraying. The mission record that survives is the HERBS file, built from the Ranch Hand mission tapes and analyzed in William A. Buckingham Jr.’s Operation Ranch Hand (Office of Air Force History, 1982): 6,542 spray missions. The file is not the whole program. It retains an estimated 86 to 88 percent of the fixed-wing missions, and the helicopter and ground spraying—the Corps’ own share among it—is missing from it. Every total built on the file is therefore a floor under an unknown remainder, a qualification the literature itself states and this report repeats at every rung of the ladder below. The program’s end came in stages: the last Agent Orange Ranch Hand mission flew on April 16, 1970, and the last fixed-wing herbicide sortie on January 7, 1971.

The turn. The program’s contraction began with a laboratory finding. In 1969 Bionetics Laboratories reported that 2,4,5-T—one of Agent Orange’s two components—carried the contaminant TCDD, a dioxin, and that it caused birth defects in rodents. In April 1970 the Department of Defense suspended the use of 2,4,5-T; Agent Orange spraying halted in 1970; and crop destruction, the food-denial mission, ended in February 1971. The science and law that followed from the Bionetics finding occupy Sections 5 and 6. What belongs to this section is the arithmetic: how much was sprayed, and how much dioxin it carried—the two numbers on which everything after depends, and on which the record has never agreed with itself.

The ladder. The totals stand in print as a ladder, each rung an instrument, and this report prints them unblended. The National Academy of Sciences’ 1974 accounting, the first, estimated the TCDD released at 106 to 163 kilograms. The Institute of Medicine’s Veterans and Agent Orange series, the standing National Academies accounting, puts the herbicides sprayed between 1962 and 1971 at about 19 million gallons, of which about 11 million gallons were Agent Orange; its own table, in metric, gives 67.8 million liters of herbicides in total and 42.6 million liters—about 11.26 million gallons—of Agent Orange, used from 1965 to 1970. The revision came in 2003, when Jeanne Mager Stellman and her co-authors published a recomputation in Nature (April 17, 2003): a revised total of about 77 million liters of herbicides—about 2.49 million gallons more than the earlier accounting—a mean TCDD concentration in Agent Orange of about 13 parts per million against the earlier figure of about 3, and a total TCDD estimate of 366 kilograms, against the 1974 figure of 106 to 163. Stellman’s team noted, as the file requires, that Army and Navy ground spraying was absent from the inventories on which even the revision rests, so the revised totals are floors as well. Industry-side critics, Ginevan and co-authors among them, argued the exposure estimates lower. The Institute of Medicine committee charged with weighing the two sides stated that it could validate neither side’s exposure model. That statement—the referee declining to certify either instrument—is where the arithmetic rests: two totals for the herbicide volume, two for the dioxin, a stated floor under both, and no validated model for converting either into the dose any individual soldier or civilian received.

5. The Contested Record

The shape of the contest. The herbicide program generated a second war, fought for fifty years in committee rooms, claims offices, and courts, and its record does not resolve into one account. Each forum asked its own question, under its own standard, and produced its own numbers. This section files them separately, as they were produced: the National Academies’ evidence reviews; the Department of Veterans Affairs’ presumptive list, which is a creature of statute; the 1984 class settlement, which was a bargain and not a finding; the Vietnamese claims, in their own association’s figures and in the American courts’ disposition; and the allied veterans’ cases in Seoul and Canberra, where two other countries’ institutions weighed the same exposure and reached different resting places.

The evidence reviews. The Agent Orange Act of 1991 (Public Law 102-4) directed the National Academies to review the evidence associating herbicide exposure with disease, in updates issued on a biennial cycle, and to sort each condition into evidence categories running from “sufficient” association down through “limited or suggestive” and “inadequate or insufficient” to “limited or suggestive evidence of no association.” The framework answers a population question—whether a condition appears among the exposed more often than chance and confounding explain—and says so; it does not answer, and does not claim to answer, whether a given veteran’s illness came from his exposure. Across the updates the categories moved. The 2018 update (Update 11) placed monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, MGUS, in the “sufficient” category, and hypertension moved to “sufficient” in the same cycle. The reviews are the scientific spine of the benefits law that follows, but the spine and the law are not the same document, and the distance between them is part of this record.

The presumptive list. Under 38 CFR 3.309(e), the Department of Veterans Affairs today pays presumptive service connection for nineteen conditions associated with herbicide exposure: AL amyloidosis; bladder cancer; chronic B-cell leukemias; chloracne; type 2 diabetes; Hodgkin’s disease; hypertension; hypothyroidism; ischemic heart disease; MGUS; multiple myeloma; non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma; Parkinson’s disease; parkinsonism; early-onset peripheral neuropathy; porphyria cutanea tarda; prostate cancer; respiratory cancers; and soft tissue sarcomas. A veteran who served in the covered places and develops a listed condition is presumed exposed and service-connected; he does not have to prove his own dose. The list was built by statute as much as by review. The Agent Orange Act of 1991 set the mechanism. The FY2021 National Defense Authorization Act, effective January 1, 2021, added bladder cancer, hypothyroidism, and parkinsonism. The PACT Act of August 10, 2022 (Public Law 117-168) added hypertension and MGUS and expanded the presumed-exposure locations to Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Guam, American Samoa, and Johnston Atoll; the Blue Water Navy presumption, for the sailors who served offshore, had taken effect January 1, 2020. In several instances Congress wrote conditions onto the list ahead of, or beyond, the point the Academies’ categories had reached—a legislative judgment laid over a scientific one, printed here as both.

The 1984 settlement. The veterans’ class action against the manufacturers ended on May 7, 1984, hours before trial, before Judge Jack B. Weinstein of the Eastern District of New York. Seven manufacturers—Dow, Monsanto, Diamond Shamrock, Hercules, Uniroyal, T.H. Agriculture & Nutrition, and Thompson Chemical—paid $180 million into a fund, then the largest settlement of its kind, without any admission of liability. The class covered veterans of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. The fund’s arithmetic is the part of the settlement that outlasted its headlines: with interest, it ultimately distributed about $197 million to some 52,000 veterans and their families—an average of about $3,800 a case, and a maximum of $12,800 paid over ten years—before the court ordered it closed on September 27, 1997. The settlement’s critics said the number in the headline and the number in the checks were different settlements; Peter Schuck’s account of the litigation records the characterization of the bargain as nuisance value, a phrase this report prints as attributed criticism, which is what it is. The settlement also closed the courthouse door behind it: later suits by veterans whose illnesses appeared after the settlement were held barred, in the Stephenson litigation of 2003 among them.

The Vietnamese claims. The Vietnamese case was filed on January 31, 2004, when the Vietnam Association for Victims of Agent Orange/Dioxin (VAVA) sued 37 companies in the Eastern District of New York. Judge Weinstein dismissed the suit on March 10, 2005; the Second Circuit affirmed on February 22, 2008; and the Supreme Court denied review on March 2, 2009. The figures for Vietnamese harm stand in print as a ladder of claims, with no United States official estimate beside them—the desk found none in the record consulted. VAVA’s count: 4.8 million people exposed, about 3 million of them victims. The figures commonly printed from the Vietnamese government: 400,000 people killed or maimed, and 500,000 children born with birth defects. The Vietnam Red Cross’s printings differ between its own publications: up to 1 million people disabled or with health problems in one, and 3 million affected including 150,000 children with serious birth defects in another. The spread among these figures is wide, the instruments behind them are not shared, and no count exists that the parties to the dispute all accept. The American response to Vietnamese harm has run through assistance and cleanup rather than compensation, and it is filed in Section 6.

Seoul. South Korea’s veterans, who had served alongside the American forces and under the same spraying, sued in their own courts from 1999, some 16,000 plaintiffs. A Seoul appeals court in January 2006 ordered Dow and Monsanto to pay about 63 billion won—about $61 to 62 million—to 6,795 veterans and families. On July 12, 2013, the Supreme Court of Korea split the result: it reversed the large award and sent it back, and it upheld an award of 466 million won—about $415,000—to 39 veterans for chloracne, the skin condition, in the first Korean ruling recognizing an epidemiological correlation between the exposure and that condition. Dow stated that it disagreed with the ruling. The Korean record therefore stands divided against itself: the broad claim unproven to its highest court’s satisfaction, and a narrow claim, for the one condition whose signature is on the skin, sustained.

Canberra. Australia’s forum was a royal commission. The Evatt Royal Commission, under Justice Phillip Evatt, delivered its final report in July and August 1985, and its headline finding was that the defoliants were not shown to be linked to Australian veterans’ health problems—“not guilty,” in the report’s own chapter heading. The commission conceded that certain of the chemicals could cause some cancers and that a connection was, in its words, “unlikely but not fanciful.” The finding was never accepted by the veterans it concerned; controversy followed the commission itself, including 1988 claims that false evidence had been put before it. In October 1994, after the Institute of Medicine’s Veterans and Agent Orange appeared, the Australian government acknowledged a link between the exposure and cancer. The Australian record thus contains both documents: a royal commission’s acquittal in 1985 and its own government’s acknowledgment, nine years later, of the class of harm the commission had not found proven.

6. The Law and the Cleanup

The legal frame. The law of the chemical war was argued while it was being fought. In 1966 the United Nations General Assembly adopted a resolution calling for strict observance of the 1925 Geneva Protocol, the treaty barring chemical weapons in war. The United States position, stated by President Nixon in 1969, was that the Protocol did not cover riot control agents or herbicides. The United States ratified the Geneva Protocol in 1975. On April 8, 1975, President Ford issued Executive Order 11850, by which the United States renounced the first use of herbicides in war—except for vegetation control within its own bases and their defensive perimeters—and renounced the first use of riot control agents in war except in defensive modes to save lives. In 1993 the Chemical Weapons Convention barred the use of riot control agents as a method of warfare outright. The frame, in sequence, is therefore: a use the United States maintained was outside the old treaty, a renunciation issued as the war ended, and a treaty prohibition adopted a generation later that covers the riot-control half of Hafner’s catalogue by name.

The cleanup. The dioxin that outlasted the war sits in the soil of the airbases where the herbicides were stored, mixed, and loaded. Twenty-eight hotspots have been identified in Vietnam, all of them former United States bases. The largest completed work is at Da Nang airport, where about 90,000 cubic meters of soil were treated, at a cost the Congressional Research Service puts at about $116 million; the remediation was completed in November 2017 and the project closed in November 2018. The largest hotspot is Bien Hoa airbase, with about 500,000 cubic meters of contaminated soil; work began there in 2019 and 2020 and continues. The cost estimates for Bien Hoa span a wide range—$137 million to $794 million in the Congressional Research Service’s printing, with a project figure of about $430 million in Associated Press reporting. Since 2007 the United States has provided more than $610 million for Vietnam in this account—about $401.5 million for environmental remediation and about $209.3 million for health and disability assistance, on the figures published in ISEAS Perspective 2026/55—as assistance, without compensation and without an admission of liability. The funding paused in January 2025 under Executive Order 14169 and the foreign-assistance freeze; most of it had been restored by March 2025. The cleanup is the one part of the contested record still physically in progress, and its completion dates and final costs are not in the record yet.

7. What the Record Does Not Settle

The individual question. Whether a particular veteran’s cancer, or a particular child’s birth defect, was caused by that veteran’s exposure is not answered anywhere in this record. The National Academies’ framework measures association across populations and states its own limit; the VA’s presumptive list answers the question administratively, by statute, without deciding it medically in the individual case; and the courts that heard individual causation put to proof dismissed it, settled it, or split it. The question most claimants actually asked is the one no forum in this file has answered in their case.

The Vietnamese totals. No instrument both sides accept has ever counted the exposed and the harmed in Vietnam. The figures in print—VAVA’s 4.8 million exposed and about 3 million victims, the government figures of 400,000 killed or maimed and 500,000 children, the Red Cross’s differing printings—stand as claims at different values, and there is no United States official estimate to set beside them. The record contains the dispute, not its resolution.

The missing spraying. The HERBS file retains an estimated 86 to 88 percent of the fixed-wing program and none of the helicopter and ground spraying. Every herbicide total in print—the National Academies’ 19 million gallons and Stellman’s 77 million liters alike—is therefore a floor under an unknown remainder, and the size of the remainder is not recoverable from the surviving files. The same gap covers the Corps’ own ground work: its volumes were never separately totaled.

The exposure models. The Institute of Medicine committee charged with weighing the Stellman revision against the industry-side critique stated that it could validate neither side’s exposure model. The conversion from gallons sprayed over a province to dose received by a person in it remains unvalidated in both directions, and every downstream estimate that depends on the conversion inherits the gap.

The CS tonnage. Published estimates of the CS the United States expended in Vietnam differ by about two thousand tons—about 7,000 tons in Ellison’s and Leitenberg’s counts, about 9,000 in a general science reference—and no consolidated official total was found. The tonnage of the Corps’ most-used munition is an open figure.

Hafner’s uncorroborated episodes. Four of the article’s most specific passages rest on the author’s testimony and the citations he gives: the improvised aerial napalm system and the drop that struck the Chinook’s rotor blades; the rice cache, its four hundred bags and the hundred bags of persistent CS worked into them; the XM-3’s 80 to 85 percent success rate, which he cites from field reporting the desk could not locate; and the malaria rates he attributes to Colonel Bauer for the period after the malathion suspension, which postdated his own tour. The desk found no independent record against which to check any of the four. They are printed in this report as his account, and they remain, in the record’s terms, one witness deep.

The author’s later record. The public record the desk could reach carries nothing of Hafner’s service after July 1969, no retirement date, and no later account from him of the war or of the controversy that grew out of the herbicide program. Whether he wrote or spoke elsewhere on the subjects his article raises is not established. The article of November 16, 2026 stands, in the reachable record, alone.

The unfinished cleanup. Bien Hoa’s remediation continues, with published cost estimates spanning $137 million to $794 million and no completion date in the record. Whether the remaining hotspots are all treated, and on what schedule, is open. So is the standing question underneath the whole file: the United States funds the cleanup of the bases and assistance to the disabled, and has never paid compensation or admitted liability, and no proceeding pending in any forum in this record would change that posture.

The Filed Clippings

Everything the desk read for the report above, clipped and filed as a bureau files it. Each item is stamped subjective, a source with a position, printed because it is useful, and described by what it says. The desk’s report is the only part of this file stamped objective. Thirteen items.

Subjective
The Army Historical Foundation—“U.S. Army Chemical Corps Operations in Vietnam,” by George J. Hafner (November 16, 2026)

This report’s primary document: a retired Chemical Corps officer’s catalogue of his branch’s Vietnam missions from his 1968–69 service with the 1st Cavalry Division—CS drum drops and the E-158, the XM-3 people sniffer flown at treetop level, flame expedients, improvised aerial napalm, ground defoliation and crop denial, malathion mosquito control, tunnel denial, and the rice-cache operation—arguing that the Air Force flew about 95 percent of the spraying and that defoliation was one mission among many; fifteen endnotes citing the division’s operational reports, the 14th Military History Detachment’s 1971 study, the Institute of Medicine, and the Chemical Corps Museum’s count of the Corps’ 39 dead; and a present-tense author biography describing a retired career officer.

Subjective
Institute of Medicine—Veterans and Agent Orange: Health Effects of Herbicides Used in Vietnam (1994), chapter as reproduced at NCBI Bookshelf

The first volume of the National Academies’ Agent Orange review series and the source Hafner cites for the Air Force’s share of the spraying: the committee’s assembly of the herbicide program’s history and exposure record, the UC-123 program’s predominance, and the evidentiary framework—association across populations, sorted into evidence categories—that the later biennial updates inherited and that the VA’s presumptive system was built on.

Subjective
Stellman, Stellman, Christian, Weber and Tomasallo—“The extent and patterns of usage of Agent Orange and other herbicides in Vietnam,” Nature (April 17, 2003)

The 2003 recomputation of the spray record from the mission data and procurement records: a revised total of about 77 million liters of herbicides, a mean TCDD concentration in Agent Orange of about 13 parts per million against the earlier 3, a total dioxin estimate of 366 kilograms, and the authors’ own statement that ground spraying is absent from the inventories, making the revised figures floors.

Subjective
William A. Buckingham Jr.—Operation Ranch Hand: The Air Force and Herbicides in Southeast Asia, 1961–1971 (Office of Air Force History, 1982), full text as reproduced at Docslib

The official Air Force history of the fixed-wing herbicide program: the Ranch Hand UC-123 operations from 1962 to 1971, the mission record that became the HERBS file and its 6,542 missions, the program’s expansion from defoliation to crop destruction, and the decisions—the 2,4,5-T suspension among them—that wound it down.

Subjective
Department of Veterans Affairs, Public Health—“Diseases Associated with Agent Orange” (accessed November 17, 2026)

The VA’s own printing of the presumptive list as it stands: the nineteen conditions for which service connection is presumed for herbicide-exposed veterans, from AL amyloidosis and chloracne through the bladder cancer, hypothyroidism, and parkinsonism added in 2021 and the hypertension and MGUS added under the PACT Act, with the exposure locations the presumptions cover.

Subjective
Agent Orange Record—“The U.S. Lawsuit” (accessed November 17, 2026)

The veterans’ class action assembled in one file: the Reutershan suit and the consolidated litigation before Judge Weinstein; the May 7, 1984 settlement at $180 million without admission of liability; the class spanning the United States, Australia, and New Zealand; the fund’s distribution of $197 million to about 52,000 veterans and families at an average of about $3,800 and a maximum of $12,800 over ten years; the fund’s closing in 1997; and the later Stephenson litigation, in which suits by veterans whose illnesses appeared after the settlement were held barred.

Subjective
Embryo Project Encyclopedia—“In re Agent Orange Product Liability Litigation (1979–1984)” (accessed November 17, 2026)

The litigation’s chronology from the Arizona State University encyclopedia: the veterans’ suits against the manufacturers from 1979, the transfer to Judge Weinstein in 1983, his focus on causation, the May 1984 trial date, and the out-of-court settlement of May 7, 1984 at $180 million for medical expenses and compensation, paid out between 1988 and 1994, which Weinstein concluded was fair.

Subjective
Phan Xuan Dung—“Vietnam–US Cooperation in Addressing the Long-term Health Effects of Agent Orange,” ISEAS Perspective 2026/55 (2026)

The assistance ledger in one paper: more than $610 million in United States support since 2007, divided between about $401.5 million of environmental remediation and about $209.3 million of health and disability assistance; the 28 identified hotspots, all former United States bases; Da Nang completed and Bien Hoa in progress; and the January 2025 funding pause under the foreign-assistance freeze, with most funding restored by March 2025.

Subjective
Royal Commission on the Use and Effects of Chemical Agents on Australian Personnel in Vietnam—Final Report, Volume 8: Conclusions, Recommendations and Epilogue (July 1985), text as held by the National Library of Australia

Justice Phillip Evatt’s conclusions volume: the chapter headed “Agent Orange Not Guilty,” the commission’s findings that the veterans’ case was not made out at the standards it applied, its concession that a connection was unlikely but not fanciful, and its recommendations—the document Australian veterans contested from its publication and that stood against the Australian government’s 1994 acknowledgment of a cancer link.

Subjective
Executive Order 11850—“Renunciation of Certain Uses in War of Chemical Herbicides and Riot Control Agents” (April 8, 1975), as printed by the National Archives

President Ford’s order in full: the United States renounces first use of herbicides in war except for vegetation control within its own bases and their immediate defensive perimeters, and first use of riot control agents in war except in defensive military modes to save lives, with any other use requiring the President’s advance approval.

The museum’s history of the volunteers who entered the tunnel systems, one of the sources Hafner cites: the scale of the Cu Chi and Iron Triangle workings, the rats’ equipment and methods, and the support around them—the demolition and chemical work, CS among it, by which complexes were sealed, gassed, and blown when they could not be cleared by hand.

Subjective
J. B. Neilands—“Vietnam: Progress of the Chemical War,” Asian Survey (1970)

A contemporary account of the chemical war written while it was under way, and one of Hafner’s fifteen endnotes: the herbicide and riot-control programs as they stood in 1970, the quantities then in print, the Bionetics finding on 2,4,5-T and TCDD, and the scientific and political argument already formed around the spraying—the first draft of the contested record, dated before the war ended.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
