Breaking News—The Impeachment File: Eight Articles, a Two-Day Clock, and the Vote It Forces

Objective

Pete Hegseth Impeachment: Thomas Massie Files Eight Articles and Forces a House Vote — Special Report

Representative Thomas Massie, a Kentucky Republican, refiled eight articles of impeachment against Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth on the House floor on Monday, November 9, 2026, as a privileged resolution, starting a clock that requires House leaders to act within two legislative days, with a vote possible as early as Wednesday. The articles charge Hegseth with waging the Iran war in violation of three provisions of the War Powers Resolution of 1973, ignoring laws that minimize civilian casualties, extrajudicial killings in the strikes on suspected drug-trafficking boats, suppressing the free speech of Senator Mark Kelly, seizing Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro without authority, and waging an unlawful war in Yemen. Hegseth said on Monday that he is not worried and will defend his record at a Pentagon press conference on Tuesday; President Trump has backed him; Speaker Mike Johnson opposes the effort; House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries is a hard yes. If the House votes to impeach, Hegseth would be the third Cabinet secretary impeached in American history, and removal would require two-thirds of the Senate. The desk files the filing, the eight articles one by one, the procedure, the count, every answer on the record, the September first filing, and what remains open.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1549 ET, Monday, November 9, 2026.

The File in Brief

Representative Thomas Massie of Kentucky, a Republican, stood on the House floor on Monday afternoon, November 9, 2026, read a 34-page resolution, and refiled eight articles of impeachment against Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth as a privileged resolution, and that single filing sets the week: House leaders must act within two legislative days, a vote on the articles themselves, on a motion to table them, or on a motion to refer them to committee is expected by Wednesday, and a simple majority of the House would impeach the secretary. The articles charge high crimes and misdemeanors. Three of them allege violations of the War Powers Resolution of 1973 in the war with Iran: waging the war with no declaration of war, no statutory authorization, no attack on the United States, and no imminent threat; continuing it after both houses of Congress directed the removal of United States forces in House Concurrent Resolution 86; and continuing hostilities past the resolution’s 60-day termination period and its further 30-day withdrawal period, a span Massie’s office states at more than 90 days. The remaining five allege ignoring the laws that minimize civilian casualties, extrajudicial killings in the lethal strikes on suspected drug-trafficking boats, retaliation against Senator Mark Kelly of Arizona for protected speech, the seizure of Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores in Operation ABSOLUTE RESOLVE, and an unlawful war in Yemen in Operation ROUGH RIDER. The answers stand on the record beside the charges. Hegseth said on Monday, “We’re not worried about that,” and announced a Pentagon press conference for Tuesday, his first there since May. President Donald Trump has backed his secretary in public, in an Oval Office remark and in a “60 Minutes” interview broadcast on Sunday. Speaker Mike Johnson opposes impeachment while hostilities continue. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said on Sunday that he is “a hard yes.” Massie first filed the same eight articles on September 15; the House left for its campaign recess the next day, the notice expired, and Monday’s filing restarts the clock in a lame-duck House that Republicans still control until January, one week after midterm elections in which they lost both chambers. If the House impeaches Hegseth, he becomes the third Cabinet secretary impeached in the history of the republic, after William Belknap in 1876 and Alejandro Mayorkas in 2024, and conviction and removal would then require two-thirds of the Senate, where Republicans hold 53 of 100 seats. The articles are allegations in a charging document filed by one member of the House. They are findings by no court, no tribunal, and no inspector general, and the desk files them at that grade, with every answer the record holds.

1. What Was Filed on Monday

The filing was one of the first legislative acts of the lame-duck session, and it was made in person, on the floor. The House returned to Washington on Monday, November 9, for the final weeks of the Republican-controlled Congress elected in 2024. Massie told the New York Post shortly before he rose that he was re-filing the articles, and that “I think it’s more likely to pass after the election than before the election,” a reference to the November 3 midterms, in which Republicans lost control of the House and the Senate, with the new majorities to be seated in January. On the floor he read the resolution’s operative charge in its own words: “Secretary Hegseth abused the powers of his high office through the following means: Executing an unlawful order to commence hostilities despite the absence of a declaration of war, specific statutory authorization, or a national emergency created by an attack upon the United States, its territories or possessions, or its armed forces.” The resolution’s closing sentence on its sponsor’s reading is also in the record: “Hegseth, by such conduct, has demonstrated that he will remain a threat to the Constitution if allowed to remain in office, and has acted in a manner grossly incompatible with his duties and the rule of law.” Massie’s office issued its announcement the same day, under the headline that he is forcing the House to vote, stating that his actions “will force the House to vote on eight specific impeachment charges against the Secretary of Defense,” and publishing the summary of each article that Section 2 of this report follows. The New York Times carried the filing as its breaking news of 3:30 p.m. ET: Representative Thomas Massie had called up impeachment charges against Pete Hegseth, accusing him of violating the Constitution, and the move started a clock that will force lawmakers to vote on the question within two days. The desk’s file agrees with the Times’ two-day statement, and adds the instrument: the resolution was filed as privileged, a question of the privileges of the House, and privilege is what starts the clock.

Massie stated his own terms for stopping, and his own advice to the president, in the same release. On November 4 he had written on X: “There is one contingency in which I won’t reintroduce the articles of impeachment: Hegseth resigns.” Hegseth did not resign, and the filing followed. In Monday’s statement Massie cast the effort in comparative terms: “My effort to impeach Secretary Hegseth is a much gentler version of what’s coming when the Democrats assume control of the House and Senate next year. They will hold hearings and trials. President Trump should replace Secretary Hegseth now while he can still get a replacement confirmed in the Senate.” He stated the question he puts to his colleagues in a post on November 9: “I’ve seen some of my colleagues say Hegseth has been effective and they agree with the war, so they’ll vote not to impeach. That’s not the question though. The question is whether he committed impeachable acts, and the answer is yes, eight times.” Massie is a member of the House Judiciary Committee, on his office’s statement. He lost his Republican primary in May to a challenger backed by the president, and he leaves Congress in January at the end of this term. Those two facts—the committee seat and the January departure—are the whole of his standing in the file: he files from inside the committee of jurisdiction, and he files as a member with no election left to contest.

2. The Eight Articles, One by One

The eight articles divide into three groups: three on Iran and the War Powers Resolution, two on the conduct of operations, and three on operations and authority beyond Iran. The summaries below are Massie’s office’s own summaries of November 9, printed in the resolution’s order. The desk prints them as the sponsor’s statement of his charges.

Article I—Waging War in Contravention of the War Powers Resolution of 1973, Section 2(c). The article charges Hegseth with waging the Iran war despite no declaration of war, no statutory authorization, no attack on the United States, and no imminent threat, and establishes, in the sponsor’s summary, that the war was unlawful from its inception.

Article II—Waging War in Contravention of the War Powers Resolution of 1973, Section 5(c). The article charges Hegseth with continuing the Iran war after both houses of Congress directed the removal of United States forces through H. Con. Res. 86, and focuses on his refusal to obey Congress’s express direction to terminate unauthorized hostilities.

Article III—Waging War in Contravention of the War Powers Resolution of 1973, Section 5(b). The article charges Hegseth with continuing hostilities in Iran after the expiration of the War Powers Resolution’s 60-day termination period and any potential 30-day withdrawal period, and establishes, in the summary’s words, that he treated mandatory statutory deadlines as discretionary. Massie’s standing formulation of the first three articles is one sentence, repeated in his release: “By engaging in hostilities in Iran for more than 90 days without congressional authorization, Secretary Hegseth is breaking the law and must be held accountable.”

Article IV—Ignoring Laws That Minimize Civilian Casualties. The article charges Hegseth with ignoring laws that minimize civilian casualties. The reporting of the resolution adds its central instance: the articles describe a United States strike that hit an elementary school in Iran as a foreseeable consequence of the secretary’s direction, and they accuse him of fostering what the resolution calls a war-making culture at the department, quoting his stated preference for “maximum lethality, not tepid legality” and his dismissal of “stupid rules of engagement.” The desk files the school strike and the culture passage as the resolution’s allegations, stated in the resolution’s terms.

Article V—Extrajudicial Killings. The article charges Hegseth with replacing maritime law enforcement with unauthorized lethal military targeting of suspected drug traffickers, resulting in at least 243 deaths, on the sponsor’s count. The article also addresses denial of quarter, secondary strikes on survivors, failure to rescue shipwrecked persons, and the creation of artificial legal authorities for the campaign. CNN’s account of the same operations, filed below, counts at least 80 vessels destroyed and 243 people killed since the operations began last September, and states that the Pentagon has provided no public evidence for its claims against the people killed. The two counts of the dead agree at 243.

Article VI—Suppressing Free Speech. The article charges Hegseth with using Defense Department authority to retaliate against and intimidate Senator Mark Kelly, a Democrat of Arizona, for constitutionally protected speech questioning unlawful military orders. Reuters’ file supplies the sequence beneath the article: last year Hegseth sought to prosecute Kelly after Kelly and five other members of Congress with military and intelligence backgrounds urged United States troops to refuse illegal orders; Hegseth accused them of sedition.

Article VII—Kidnapping of a Sovereign Foreign Leader. The article charges Hegseth with directing Operation ABSOLUTE RESOLVE to seize Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores in Venezuela without constitutional or legal authority. The resolution’s characterization of the January operation, as CNN reports its language, is “an act of war against a sovereign country executed under the guise of narcotics law enforcement.” The president’s own characterization stands in Section 4 of this report.

Article VIII—Unlawful War in Yemen. The article charges Hegseth with initiating Operation ROUGH RIDER in Yemen without congressional authorization or an imminent threat to the United States, while also violating war powers reporting requirements.

The resolution closes in the form the House’s charging documents use, and CNN prints the sentence. “In all of this, Secretary Hegseth has acted in a manner contrary to his trust as Secretary of Defense and subversive of constitutional government, to the great prejudice of the cause of law and justice, and to the manifest injury of the people of the United States.” One defense from the administration stands on the record from the first filing: Attorney General Todd Blanche said in September that Hegseth follows the law. No fuller administration answer to the eight articles, article by article, stands in Monday’s file.

3. The Clock: How a Privileged Resolution Forces a Vote

The procedure is short, and every step of it is a recorded vote. The Constitution gives the House “the sole Power of Impeachment” and the Senate the power to try all impeachments. A member may raise impeachment as a question of the privileges of the House. When a resolution is filed in that form, House leaders have two legislative days to schedule its consideration. Massie stated the mechanics in his own words before the filing: “The ball is in their court once I give notice, so they have two legislative days. They could deal with it tonight, tomorrow or the next day.” The accounts agree on the deadline the clock produces: action by Wednesday, November 11, with a vote possible as early as Wednesday and, on Massie’s own statement, as early as Monday night. At the hour of consideration, leadership has three courses, and the file names all three in the same order in outlet after outlet: the House votes directly on the articles; a member moves to table the resolution, a motion that is not debatable and that ends the resolution if a majority agrees; or the House refers the resolution to committee, where it would sit for the remainder of the Congress. A motion to table is a vote on the effort’s survival that carries no vote on the charges themselves, and it is still a recorded vote, taken in public, one week after an election. If the tabling motion fails and the House passes the resolution, Hegseth is impeached, and the matter passes to the Senate.

The Senate’s part of the procedure carries the higher bar, and the file states it without variation. Impeachment is the charge; conviction at a Senate trial requires two-thirds of the senators, 67 of 100 when the full Senate votes. No Cabinet official has ever been convicted. CNN’s file adds the Senate’s two shorter courses, on a Republican aide’s account: the Senate could move to dismiss the articles, or refer the matter to a committee for investigation, and each course requires a majority vote. No trial date exists, and none can exist before a House vote that has not yet been held. The resolution Massie refiled on Monday is, on his own description, “99.99% the same” as the September text, with minor corrections, and he was required to refile it because the September notice expired during the recess, a point Section 5 takes up.

4. The Count: The House as It Stands, and the Senate Behind It

No public whip count exists in Monday’s file, and the desk prints the arithmetic and the stated positions, and no prediction dressed as either. Impeachment in the House requires a simple majority of the members voting. Republicans hold the House until the new Congress convenes in January, by a margin the New York Post states at five seats, which makes a handful of Republican votes the whole question if the Democratic members vote together. Reuters reports that congressional sources say the resolution may have the simple majority needed to pass the Republican-controlled House before Democrats take over on January 3. ABC News’ arithmetic, as carried in the accounts filed below, runs the same way from the other side: if every Democrat supports the resolution, three Republican defections would be enough to pass it. Massie’s own estimates stand in two dated versions. Before Election Day he told Roll Call: “I give it better than a 50 percent chance of passing,” and he has said he expects a handful of Republicans. On Monday he said the chances are better after the election than before it. Two private expectations are in the file at a lower grade: MS NOW reported that two Republican lawmakers privately expect the measure to succeed if it reaches a direct floor vote, and CNN reported before the filing that several House Republicans expected the measure to pass. Private expectations are printed here as what they are; they are commitments by no one.

The stated positions on the record at collection time run as follows. For the resolution: Massie, its sponsor, a Republican. Hakeem Jeffries, the Democratic leader, announced his support on Sunday, November 8, on ABC’s “This Week”: “I’m a hard yes on the Hegseth resolution. Pete Hegseth has got to go.” Jeffries is expected to become Speaker when the new House is seated in January. Representative Adam Smith of Washington, the senior Democrat on the House Armed Services Committee, said: “I think there are enough Republicans who want him gone.” Against, or uncommitted, on the record: Speaker Johnson, whose position is in Section 5; Representative Don Bacon of Nebraska, retiring, who told ABC News he would weigh the debate before deciding, adding, “I do think the secretary has made some bad mistakes”; and House Oversight Committee Chairman James Comer, who declined to back the effort. In the Senate, conviction appears unlikely on every account in the file: two-thirds is required, and Republicans hold 53 of 100 seats in the chamber that would try the case, a Republican Senate sitting until January. Behind the arithmetic stands the midterm result that frames it: voter anger over the Iran war helped Democrats win control of Congress in last week’s elections, on Reuters’ account; the president and the vice president have attributed the losses to the conflict with Iran and the ensuing rise in gas prices, on USA Today’s account; and some Republican lawmakers have expressed concern, on Reuters’ account, that Hegseth’s strained relationship with Congress will undermine the administration’s push for a $1.5 trillion defense spending package, while others have urged the White House to replace him while a Republican Senate can still confirm a successor.

5. The Answers on the Record: The Secretary, the President, the Speaker

Hegseth answered on Monday in person, briefly, and in advance of a fuller answer scheduled for Tuesday. Asked whether he had enough Republican support to defeat the impeachment push, he said: “We’re not worried about that.” He said he is ready to defend his record at the Pentagon. He announced a press conference for Tuesday at the Pentagon, his first there since May, at which the accounts agree he will address the impeachment effort and defend his tenure. CNN adds two Monday statements to the file: Hegseth said he agreed with the president that he would again launch the Iran war if he had a do-over, and he described disagreements in Congress over policy decisions at the Pentagon as “disagreements with success,” saying that those who take issue with the military operations under his watch are “taking issue with the troops.” His fuller answer predates the filing by four days. In a video posted on November 5, filmed outside the Defense Department, he said: “A disgruntled, defeated congressman along with all his Democrat allies has also dropped sham articles of impeachment against yours truly, just like they tried against President Trump twice. It’s all about the politics of personal destruction. Didn’t work against President Trump, and it won’t work with me. You can rest assured of that.” In the same video he defended the Iran war as a “courageous and historic effort” and pressed Congress for a reconciliation bill of at least $350 billion for the department. His spokesman’s statement stands beside his own: Chief Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell told the New York Post last week that “Secretary Hegseth will not back down or be deterred by politicians seeking to score partisan points or launch phony investigations where no wrongdoing exists,” that the department will continue to drive the president’s agenda “despite threats of sham witch hunts from Democrats,” and that the secretary “is 100% committed to leading the Department until the end of President Trump’s term in office.” Hegseth styles his office Secretary of War, a usage Reuters notes as part of his rebranding of the department; his constitutional title, and the title in the resolution, is Secretary of Defense.

The president’s backing stands in three dated statements, and the desk files all three. On Thursday, November 5, Trump told reporters that Hegseth has done a “fantastic job,” and he told ABC’s Jonathan Karl in a separate conversation: “I have every intention to keep him.” On Sunday, November 8, he told reporters: “He’s guilty of two things: basically winning two wars. I believe we saved the Middle East and Israel combined.” In the “60 Minutes” interview broadcast on Sunday, as CBS’s transcript carries it and Reuters reports it, he said of the impeachment push: “They want to impeach him. Very unfair. We had two wars, we won both,” referring to Iran, where Reuters describes the war as at an impasse, and to Venezuela, where United States forces deposed President Maduro in a January raid on his compound in Caracas. The White House’s social media account has posted messages praising Hegseth and his accomplishments at the Pentagon in recent days. Separate reporting in the same file states that the president had been asking advisers and allies whether Hegseth should stay on after the midterms; the White House has announced no change in his status, and the public statements above are the administration’s position as it stands.

The Speaker’s opposition stands in a September statement and a current one, and the two differ in their grounds. In September, Johnson said the resolution would “immediately be tabled” once members returned, called the effort “a publicity stunt by someone who wants attention,” and said the secretary had done “an extraordinary job at the Department of War.” His current ground, as Reuters reports it, is the timing: impeachment “in the middle of hostilities abroad” would be “a terrible thing for our allies and our enemies to see.” The Speaker controls the floor schedule within the two-day rule; the rule limits that control to two legislative days, and the September exercise of it is the subject of the next section.

6. The First Filing: September, the Recess, and the Expired Notice

Monday’s filing was the second filing of the same resolution, and the history of the first is short and complete in the record. Massie first introduced the eight articles on September 15, 2026, reading the 34-page resolution on the House floor and giving notice of his intent to raise a question of the privileges of the House. The vote the notice would have forced never occurred. On September 16, Speaker Johnson cancelled the remaining floor session and sent the House home a day early into its seven-week campaign recess, ahead of the midterm elections. The two-legislative-day clock ran out with the House away, the notice expired during the pro forma sessions that followed, and the resolution lapsed without a vote, a tabling motion, or a referral. Johnson denied at the time that the timing had anything to do with Hegseth. Massie’s account of the same week is that leadership cancelled Congress to avoid the vote, and he has said he had kept party leaders in the dark in September so they could not shut the House down early to stop him. Both accounts of the motive stand in the file; the sequence itself—filing on the 15th, adjournment on the 16th, a seven-week recess, an expired notice—is agreed by every account the desk read. A separate Democratic effort stands in the background of the same record: Representative Yassamin Ansari of Arizona filed a six-article impeachment resolution against Hegseth in April, with eight Democratic cosponsors, and no vote has been held on it. The resolution that will be voted on this week, if any is, is Massie’s.

7. The History the Vote Would Join

A Cabinet secretary has been impeached twice in 250 years, and the file states both cases in full, because they are the whole of the precedent. The first is William Belknap, Secretary of War under President Ulysses S. Grant, impeached by the House in 1876 on charges of taking kickbacks and corruption. Belknap resigned on the day of his impeachment vote, in a tearful meeting at the White House, and the House impeached him anyway; the Senate tried him and acquitted him, falling short of the two-thirds required. Belknap remains, on Reuters’ statement, the first and to date the only civilian leader of the United States military to be impeached, which is the narrower record Hegseth’s case would enter. The second case is Alejandro Mayorkas, President Joseph Biden’s Secretary of Homeland Security, impeached by House Republicans in 2024 by a narrow margin over his handling of immigration at the southern border; the Democratic-controlled Senate voted to kill both articles without a trial. CNN’s statement of the count is the one the desk adopts: if this week’s vote succeeds, Hegseth would be the third Cabinet secretary to be impeached. Two-thirds of the Senate has never convicted a Cabinet official. Hegseth’s answer to the precedent is on the record in his November 5 video, in his comparison of his case to the president’s: the effort was tried against Trump twice, he said, and failed twice, and it will fail with him.

Five items stand open at the hour of collection, and the desk files them open. First, the vote itself: no vote on the articles, on tabling, or on referral had been held at 1549 ET on Monday, and no hour for a vote had been published; Massie’s own range runs from Monday night to Wednesday. Second, the count: no member outside the statements in Section 4 had published a position in the accounts the desk read, and no whip count from either party’s leadership stands in the file. Third, the administration’s article-by-article answer: the record holds the secretary’s general answer, his spokesman’s statement, the president’s backing, and the attorney general’s September statement that Hegseth follows the law; it holds no point-by-point response to Articles I through VIII, and the secretary’s Tuesday press conference is the next scheduled occasion for one. Fourth, the Senate’s course: dismissal, referral to committee, and trial are all described in the file as possibilities, and no Senate leader’s statement of intent stands in Monday’s accounts. Fifth, the underlying operations: the articles’ factual allegations—the school strike in Iran, the 243 deaths in the boat campaign, the operations in Yemen and Venezuela—stand in the file as allegations supported by the reporting cited in the clippings below, weighed against the administration’s position that the wars were won and the law was followed, and they have been adjudicated by no body. The contradictions in Monday’s record are small and the desk prints them in the open: the margin of the Republican House majority is stated at five seats by the Post and implied at a handful by every other account, with the defection arithmetic stated at three Republicans in ABC’s version and at a handful in Massie’s; the expected day of the vote is stated as Wednesday in most accounts and as early as Monday night in the sponsor’s; and the secretary’s title is stated as Secretary of Defense in the Constitution, the resolution, and most reporting, and as Secretary of War in the secretary’s own usage and in statements by his defenders. None of the three conflicts changes the filing, the clock, or the charges.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
