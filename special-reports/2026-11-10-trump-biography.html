An Objective Biography—Origins, the Building Business, the Brand, Two Presidencies, the Courts, and the Money

Objective

Donald J. Trump: The Documented Record, 1946 to 2026 — Special Report

Donald John Trump, born June 14, 1946, in Queens, New York, is the 45th and 47th President of the United States, the only president impeached twice, and the first former president convicted of a felony. This report files his documented record whole, from the record and not from either camp’s telling of it: the Queens real-estate family and the disputed size of the father’s money; the education and the Vietnam-era draft file; the building career, the $3.4 billion debt workout of 1990, and the six corporate Chapter 11 filings that stand beside the documented fact that he never filed for bankruptcy personally; the brand era of The Apprentice, licensing, Trump University, and the Trump Foundation; three marriages and the personal record; the 2016 election, the Mueller report in its own words, two impeachments and two acquittals, the 2020 election and January 6; the four criminal cases of 2023 and their four different ends—one conviction, argued on appeal on November 5, 2026, with no decision issued as this report files, and three dismissals; the two Carroll verdicts and the New York fraud judgment, whose $464 million penalty stands vacated on appeal while its liability finding stands affirmed, the whole case now before the state’s highest court undecided; the 2024 election and the second term’s orders, tariffs, wars, and shutdown, and the 2026 midterm elections, called for the Democratic Party in both chambers of Congress with two Senate races uncalled and no state certification yet issued; and the money, kept throughout in the three separate ledgers the record itself keeps it in—his own claims, the estimators’ published figures, and the courts’ findings. Allegations are attributed, denials are carried, adjudicated outcomes are stated as outcomes, and where the record disagrees with itself the disagreement is printed, not averaged.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1150 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026.

The File in Brief

The man first, because everything else in this file is downstream of him. Donald John Trump was born on June 14, 1946, in Jamaica Hospital, Queens, the fourth of five children of Fred Trump, a builder of middle-income housing in Brooklyn and Queens, and Mary Anne MacLeod Trump, a Scottish immigrant who arrived in New York in 1930 with a domestic worker’s visa. He was sent at thirteen to the New York Military Academy, attended Fordham University for two years, and took a bachelor’s degree in economics from the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania in 1968. He never served in the armed forces: the Selective Service record documents four student deferments, a medical classification of 1-Y in October 1968 attributed by Trump to bone spurs, a draft-lottery number, 356, too high to be called, and a permanent 4-F classification in 1972. The diagnosis behind the medical classification is not documented; the reporting on it is contested; both facts are printed in section 2. He took the presidency of his father’s company in 1971, moved its ambitions from the outer boroughs to Manhattan, and built the public identity—developer, casino operator, television host, licensor of his own name—on which, in 2015, he opened a campaign for the presidency. He has been married three times and has five children. He is, as this report files, the sitting President of the United States, twenty-two months into a second term that the midterm elections of November 3, 2026, have just answered with a Congress of the opposite party.

The father’s money is the first contested number in the file, and the contest is between a framing and a document review. Trump’s own account, given in 2015 and repeated since, is that his father gave him “a small loan of a million dollars,” repaid with interest. The New York Times’ investigation of October 2, 2018, built on more than 100,000 pages of Fred Trump’s tax returns and financial records, found transfers of at least $413 million in 2018 dollars across Donald Trump’s lifetime, loans of at least $60.7 million mostly never repaid, and a child who was a millionaire in inflation-adjusted terms by age eight. The family and the White House denied the investigation’s tax-scheme characterizations as “100 percent false”; the transfer figures themselves were never rebutted with documents, and no official disposition of the state tax review announced the same day has ever been published. The report prints the range, $1 million as the framing of one loan and $413 million as the documented-review total of all transfers, and does not split it.

The business record is a rise, a collapse that stopped one step short of personal ruin, and a reinvention as a licensor. The documented spine: the Grand Hyatt conversion of 1980 and Trump Tower in 1983 made him a Manhattan figure; the Atlantic City casinos and the Plaza Hotel made him, by 1990, a debtor of about $3.4 billion on the New Jersey regulators’ count, $832.5 million of it personally guaranteed, kept from personal bankruptcy by a bank workout that capped his personal spending and took mortgages on nearly everything he owned. Six Chapter 11 petitions by Trump-controlled entities followed between 1991 and 2009; in each, the property kept operating, creditors took less than face value, and his ownership fell. He has never filed a personal bankruptcy petition; that sentence and the six filings are both the record, and the report prints them together, as it prints his own statement—“I have never gone bankrupt”—beside them. The public casino company, DJT, lost money in every year of its existence, about $640 million cumulatively, while paying him more than $44 million on one review of its filings. The Apprentice, from 2004, rebuilt the fortune on fame: about $197 million in salary and fees on the tax records the Times examined, and about $230 million more in the licensing income the fame generated. Trump University ended in a $25 million settlement in November 2016, its fraud allegations never adjudicated; the Trump Foundation ended dissolved under court supervision, with a $2 million court-ordered payment and nineteen paragraphs of admissions in the settlement stipulations.

The legal record of the 2020s is the densest part of the file, and its state at filing is this. Of the four criminal cases brought in 2023, one produced a conviction: a Manhattan jury’s unanimous verdict on May 30, 2024, on 34 felony counts of falsifying business records, followed on January 10, 2025, by a sentence of unconditional discharge—the conviction final, the punishment none. His appeal was argued before a five-judge panel of the Appellate Division, First Department, on November 5, 2026; the desk’s pre-publication check of November 10 finds no decision issued, and none is claimed here. The two federal cases were dismissed after the 2024 election under the Justice Department’s policy against prosecuting a sitting president, the documents case on a judge’s ruling that the special counsel’s appointment was unconstitutional, its merits never reached; the Georgia case was dismissed in full on November 26, 2025, by the successor prosecutor after the elected district attorney’s disqualification. The civil record stands differently: two juries found against him for E. Jean Carroll—sexual abuse and defamation, $5 million in 2023; defamation, $83.3 million in 2024—and both judgments were affirmed on appeal; the $5 million judgment is final, the Supreme Court having denied review on June 29, 2026, while the $83.3 million judgment is before that Court now, its enforcement stayed. A New York judge’s finding of persistent fraud in his financial statements stands affirmed; the $464 million penalty that accompanied it was vacated in August 2025 as an excessive fine; both sides have carried the case to the New York Court of Appeals, where, the desk’s check finds, it stands briefed and undecided, seventeen state attorneys general having filed in support of the state on November 5, 2026. Beyond Carroll, accusations of sexual misconduct by other women are counted by their counters at between 16 and 28; all are alleged, all are denied, none has produced a criminal charge, and Carroll’s is the only one adjudicated. Section 9 files the whole docket, matter by matter.

The political record is two presidencies separated by a defeat he has never conceded. He won the presidency in 2016 while losing the popular vote by 2.87 million; lost it in 2020 by 7.06 million votes and 306 to 232 in the Electoral College; and won it back in 2024 with 312 electoral votes and a popular-vote plurality of 2.28 million, the first non-consecutive return since Grover Cleveland. Between the defeat and the return stand the documented events of January 6, 2021—a riot at the Capitol during the electoral count, five deaths in the medical examiner’s count for the two days, about 140 injured police officers, a second impeachment, and a Senate vote of 57 to 43 for conviction, ten short—and, in January 2025, his own proclamation pardoning nearly all of those convicted for that day. The Mueller investigation’s two operative sentences are printed verbatim in section 7: on coordination, the investigation “did not establish” it; on obstruction, the report “does not exonerate him.” His first-term Gallup average, 41 percent, is the lowest full-term average in that series, and he is the only president in the series never to reach 50 percent. The second term, through this filing, contains 274 executive orders by August 2026 on the Federal Register’s numbering, a reconciliation statute carrying a $5 trillion debt-limit increase, a tariff program the Supreme Court held unlawful under its stated authority on February 20, 2026, the longest government shutdown on record at 43 days, two wars against Iran in the record’s count, and immigration enforcement whose own administration counts more than 3 million departures against independent estimates near 1 million, the range printed with its holders. On November 3, 2026, the voters took both chambers of Congress from his party; the desk’s pre-publication check finds the Associated Press’s calls standing—Democrats at 54 Senate seats with two races uncalled, and past the 218-seat House majority with about twenty races uncalled—and no state’s certification yet issued, the first county certifications falling due November 13. The numbers in this paragraph that are projections are labeled as projections in section 10, and nothing in this report treats an uncalled race as decided.

The money, in sum, is three numbers that are not one number. His own claims run from $8,737,540,000 in his 2015 campaign release to “in excess of TEN BILLION DOLLARS” in his own announcement of it, and include his sworn deposition testimony of 2007 that his net worth goes up and down “with attitudes and with feelings, even my own feelings.” The estimators’ figures run from Forbes’s $200 million family-share entry in 1982, through removal from the Forbes 400 for 1990 to 1995, $4.5 billion from Forbes and about $3 billion from Bloomberg at the first campaign, a decline to about $2.1 to $2.5 billion across the first term, and a rise on the Trump Media listing and the crypto ventures to Forbes’s highest figure for him, $7.3 billion in September 2025, and $7.0 billion in September 2026, with Bloomberg’s latest cited reading at about $7.1 billion. The courts’ figure is of a different kind: an adjudicated finding that the statements of financial condition issued in his name overstated his assets and net worth, by $812 million to $2.2 billion in given years on the Attorney General’s figures, which the trial court substantially credited. No audited personal balance sheet exists in the public record to reconcile the three ledgers, and this report does not pretend one does. His 2025 financial disclosure, released in June 2026, reported more than $2.2 billion of income in a year, more than $1.4 billion of it crypto-related—an income figure, printed as one, and not a net worth.

1. Origins: Fred Trump, Mary Anne MacLeod, and the Queens Upbringing

The paternal line begins in Kallstadt, in the Palatinate. Friedrich Trump emigrated to the United States in 1885, made money in the restaurant and hotel trade of the Pacific Northwest and the Klondike, returned to Germany to marry, and was ordered expelled by Bavaria in 1905 for having emigrated without completing military service; the family settled in Queens. His son Fred, born in 1905 in the Bronx, began building houses in Queens as a teenager in his mother’s company, Elizabeth Trump & Son, and became one of New York’s largest builders of middle-income housing—garden apartments in Brooklyn and Queens by the tens of thousands of units, financed substantially with federal housing money, and held, as a rule, rather than sold. Fred Trump’s method is documented in the assessments of his own era: low cost, tight management, political fluency, and rents kept at the level his tenants could pay. His own controversies are part of the record the son inherited: a 1954 Senate Banking Committee inquiry into windfall profits on FHA-insured projects, in which Fred Trump testified and was not charged; and, in 1927, his arrest at a Ku Klux Klan parade riot in Queens, a documented arrest in the police record of the event, the charge dismissed—filed here as an arrest record, which is all it is, with no finding attached to it in either direction.

Mary Anne MacLeod was born in 1912 in Tong, on the Isle of Lewis in Scotland, the youngest of ten children of a fisherman and crofter. She immigrated in May 1930, her visa numbered 26698, entered for domestic service, became a naturalized citizen in 1942, and married Fred Trump in 1936. The couple had five children: Maryanne, a federal judge; Fred Jr., an airline pilot who died in 1981 at 42 of alcoholism-related illness; Elizabeth; Donald, born June 14, 1946; and Robert, who died in 2020. The family lived in Jamaica Estates, Queens, in a brick house Fred Trump built. The household record, as the biographies assembled from family and school sources describe it, was prosperous, disciplined, and competitive; Donald Trump’s own accounts describe a father he admired and a childhood of energy that the family answered, in 1959, by sending the thirteen-year-old to military school. The reason for the enrollment is reported rather than documented—the biographical record, including family accounts, describes a boy whose behavior his parents, and principally his father, judged to need the structure—and it is printed as reported.

Kew-Forest School, in Forest Hills, was his school until 1959. The childhood behavior record rests on the recollections of classmates and teachers collected by biographers decades later; it is consistent in describing a forceful, disruptive, athletic child, and it is recollection, graded as such in the desk’s research file and carried here in one sentence because a biography prints what the record holds and labels what the record is.

2. The Father’s Money

No fact in this file is more argued over relative to its documentary mass than the size of Fred Trump’s support. The two public accounts are these. Donald Trump’s, first given in 2015: “I got a very small loan from my father of one million dollars,” repaid with interest—a framing he has repeated, with the borrowing described against his father’s opposition to the Manhattan move. The New York Times’ account, published October 2, 2018, after a year-long examination of more than 100,000 pages of Fred Trump’s tax returns, partnership documents, bank statements, and audits, confidential records provided to the paper: Fred Trump transferred to his children, and principally to Donald, at least $413 million in 2018 dollars across his lifetime. The components the Times printed include salary from the family company rising to millions a year in the 1980s; loans of at least $60.7 million, most never repaid; trust funds and gifts beginning in childhood—Donald Trump a millionaire, in inflation-adjusted terms, by age eight, drawing the equivalent of about $200,000 a year by then and more than $1 million a year at 22; and crisis support in 1990 and 1991, including a $3.35 million purchase of casino chips by Fred Trump’s lawyer at Trump Castle in December 1990, made without gambling, ahead of a bond payment the casino could not otherwise meet—a transaction New Jersey regulators determined functioned as an unapproved loan, for which the casino admitted a violation and paid a penalty whose components the sources print as $30,000 and $65,000. The Times described the transfer machinery as including discounted partnership interests, inflated invoices through a family purchasing company, and properties passed at understated values. Fred Trump’s estate at his death on June 25, 1999, was estimated by the family at $250 million to $300 million; its value for tax purposes, on the Times’ record analysis, was about $51.8 million. The outer-borough portfolio built by Fred Trump was sold in 2004 for $737.9 million, of which Donald Trump’s share was $177.3 million.

The responses are on the record. The family’s statement in 2018, through a Trump Organization lawyer, called the Times’ tax characterization “highly defamatory and utterly false”; the White House called the report “100 percent false” and a hit piece. Neither response produced documents rebutting the transfer totals. The official aftermath is a null result, printed as one: New York State’s tax department announced a review on the day of publication; no disposition of that review has ever been announced, and the criminal limitations periods applicable to the years in question have expired. The estate itself closed in litigation: Fred Trump’s 1991 will, which cut out the children of Fred Jr., who had died a decade earlier, was contested by Fred Jr.’s son and daughter; the confidential settlement of April 2001 ended the contest, its full terms sealed, the one figure public being more than $2.7 million disclosed by Mary Trump, Fred Jr.’s daughter, in her own later account. The desk’s finding, stated once: the $413 million figure is a reported figure resting on documents the Times examined and quoted; the $1 million figure is the subject’s framing of a single loan; both are printed wherever this report touches the inheritance, and no third figure exists in the record to arbitrate between them.

3. Military School, Fordham, Wharton, and the Draft File

The New York Military Academy, at Cornwall-on-Hudson, held him from 1959 to 1964. The school record documents enrollment, promotion to cadet captain, and participation in sports; the biographical record adds, as reported recollection, a forceful cadet career that included a demotion and a transfer of rooms after an incident with a roommate. He attended Fordham University in the Bronx from 1964 to 1966 and transferred to the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania, from which he received a bachelor of science in economics on May 20, 1968. Two education claims require the label treatment. The first is Trump’s repeated statement that he graduated first in his class at Wharton. The 1968 commencement program, a document, lists the honors graduates of his class and his name is not among them; no class ranking placing him first exists in the record. The claim is printed as his statement, contradicted by the program. The second is the mechanism of the transfer itself, which biographers have reported rested in part on an interview arranged through a family friend of an admissions officer; it is reported, not documented, and is carried as such.

The draft file is a Selective Service record, and it is unusually complete. It documents four student deferments, classification 2-S, across 1964 to 1968, the standard deferment of the era for a full-time student. In October 1968, after graduation and two physical examinations, he was classified 1-Y—qualified for service only in time of war or national emergency—on medical grounds. His account, given at various times, is that the ground was bone spurs in his heels; his accounts have varied on the details, including which foot, and he said in 2016 that he could not recall precisely, and later that the spurs were in both feet, and that a doctor provided a letter. On December 1, 1969, the draft lottery assigned his birth date the number 356 of 366, a number never reached; the call that year stopped far below it. On February 1, 1972, his classification became 4-F, permanently unqualified. The diagnosis itself is not documented: Dr. Larry Braunstein, the Queens podiatrist named in the record as the signer of the letter, died in 2007; his daughters told the Times in December 2018 that the examination was, in their father’s telling to the family, a favor to Fred Trump, whose buildings Braunstein rented office space in—family recollection, reported, with no corroborating document, and the Times said so in its own account. Michael Cohen, Trump’s former lawyer, testified to Congress in 2019 that Trump instructed him to handle press inquiries about the draft and that Trump told him “you think I’m stupid” about the prospect of Vietnam—sworn testimony by a witness himself convicted of lying to Congress, printed as testimony, which is what it is. What is documented, and what this report states as the file’s bottom line, is the classifications and the lottery number; what is alleged is the favor; and the two do not occupy the same evidentiary ground, in either direction.

4. The Developer: The Outer Boroughs, the Hyatt, the Tower, Atlantic City

He joined the family company after Wharton and became its president in 1971, at 25; the company took the name the Trump Organization under his presidency, the legal instrument of the renaming not being in the public record. The company’s business was Fred Trump’s: thousands of apartments in Brooklyn and Queens. Its first appearance in the legal record of this biography came in October 1973, when the Department of Justice sued Trump Management for racial discrimination in rentals, alleging that applications from Black renters were marked and steered. The case ended on June 10, 1975, in a consent decree: the company agreed to furnish the Urban League with listings and to rent without regard to race, admitting no liability. Donald Trump’s account, then and since, is that the company settled to avoid an onerous decree and admitted nothing; the government’s complaint and the decree are documents, and the report prints the decree’s terms and the denial together. A 1978 enforcement action followed, the Justice Department alleging the decree was being violated; its formal caption and closure are not settled in the sources the desk could reach, and it is filed here as an open item rather than a finding.

The Manhattan move is the documented break. In 1976 he took an option on the Commodore Hotel, a failing railroad hotel beside Grand Central Terminal, and assembled the financing for its conversion on the strength of a forty-year city tax abatement whose value across its term is estimated by the Times at about $360 million and in other accounts at about $400 million, nominal dollars. The building reopened in 1980 as the Grand Hyatt. Trump Tower followed: the Bonwit Teller site on Fifth Avenue, acquired in 1979, its Art Deco bas-reliefs destroyed in 1980 during demolition after a public dispute over their promised donation to the Metropolitan Museum of Art—Trump’s stated reason at the time was cost and delay, the museum’s position that it would have taken them, both on the record. The tower opened in 1983: 58 stories as counted by its floors’ numbering, which begins at 10, a discrepancy between numbering and structure that is documented and was the first instance of a pattern the record repeats in his building descriptions. His own triplex in it he would later describe, in the financial statements at issue in section 9, as 30,000 square feet; its documented size is 10,996 square feet.

The 1980s expansion ran on borrowed money: the Trump Shuttle airline, bought from Eastern Air Lines in 1989 and surrendered to its lenders in default in 1990; the Plaza Hotel, bought in 1988 for about $390 million, a price Trump himself described at the time as buying a Mona Lisa—“not a real estate investment”—and which the hotel’s cash flow never carried; Mar-a-Lago in Palm Beach, bought in 1985 and later converted in part to a private club; the Wollman Rink reconstruction of 1986, in which he took over a city project that had run six years and finished it in months under budget, an episode that is documented and that he has cited since as the model of his method; and the United States Football League, whose 1986 antitrust suit against the NFL, in which he was a central owner-advocate, won a verdict of one dollar, trebled to three, and ended the league. Atlantic City was the center of it: the Trump Plaza casino in 1984, the Castle in 1985, and the Taj Mahal, bought unfinished from Resorts International and opened in April 1990 as the largest casino in the world, financed with junk bonds at interest the property could not service from its first year. By the summer of 1990 the structure was insolvent on a cash-flow basis, and the file turns to the workout.

5. The Workout and the Six Filings

The New Jersey Casino Control Commission and the Division of Gaming Enforcement, which had regulatory access to the casino books, put the organization’s total debt in 1990 at about $3.4 billion; other contemporary tallies ran to about $5 billion counting entity and contingent obligations. Of the $3.4 billion, $832.5 million was personally guaranteed by Donald Trump on the Commission’s record, a figure later press summaries put at about $900 million. In June 1990 the organization could not make scheduled payments; nine banks lent a further $65 million in what the Commission called an eleventh-hour save, and in August more than seventy lenders agreed a workout: deferral of principal and reduced interest on about $1 billion of bank debt, second and third mortgages on nearly all his properties, a chief financial officer acceptable to the banks, bank approval over transfers among his companies, and a cap on his personal spending drawn from the businesses—$450,000 a month for the rest of 1990, falling on a schedule to $250,000 a month by 1992. The Trump Shuttle went to its lenders in 1990; the yacht Trump Princess was sold in 1991 to Prince Alwaleed bin Talal for a reported $20 million; the Plaza interest was reduced and sold by 1995. The workout, not any court filing, is what stood between the structure and a personal bankruptcy—a distinction that matters to the next paragraph’s count.

Chapter 11 is a reorganization, not a liquidation: the debtor keeps operating, collection halts, and creditors accept less, later, or in equity. The American Bankruptcy Institute, counting from the dockets, counts six petitions by Trump-controlled entities between 1991 and 2009. They are: Trump Taj Mahal Associates, July 16, 1991, prepackaged, its bondholders taking 50 percent of the casino’s equity for reduced interest and extended maturities; Trump Castle Associates, March 9, 1992, bondholders taking a 50 percent interest on parallel terms; the Trump Plaza casino, March 1992, on the same pattern; the Plaza Hotel’s operating entity, November 1992, carrying more than $550 million of debt, six lenders taking 49 percent, Trump retaining 51 percent on paper with no salary and no operating role; Trump Hotels & Casino Resorts, the public company, November 21, 2004, carrying about $1.8 billion of debt, his ownership falling from 47 percent to 27 percent while other common shareholders were reduced to about 0.05 percent; and Trump Entertainment Resorts, February 17, 2009, filed days after he resigned its chairmanship, from which he emerged in 2010 holding a 10 percent stake paid for the use of his name. A seventh filing, by the same company in 2014 after his control had ended and his stake was extinguished in it, is not counted in the six by the convention the desk follows, and the convention is stated so the count can be checked. Sources that say four group the 1992 filings by event; the docket count is six.

The personal question is answered by the same record. No personal bankruptcy petition by Donald J. Trump—Chapter 7, 11, or 13—appears in the federal court record, and the Institute states the point directly: he has never filed a personal bankruptcy, and his business interests have sought Chapter 11 protection six times. His own statements—“I have never gone bankrupt”; the filings described as the use of “the laws of the country”—are accurate in the narrow legal sense and incomplete as a description of proceedings in which creditors of entities he controlled, chaired, and partly owned accepted losses and equity in place of cash. Both halves are printed because both are in the record. The public company between those filings is its own ledger: Trump Hotels & Casino Resorts, taken public in June 1995 at $14 a share under the ticker DJT, bought the Taj Mahal and the Castle from Trump personally in 1996 for a reported $890 million and $485 million, transactions in which he stood on both sides, and then reported a net loss in every year of its existence—about $637 million to $647 million cumulatively on the filings’ reviews, the range a matter of which partial-year lines are counted—while paying him, on a Washington Post review of the same filings, more than $44 million in salary, bonuses, and other payments. A $100 investment in the 1995 offering was worth about $10 by April 2004 on that paper’s calculation. The company’s 2004 filing is the fifth of the six above.

6. The Brand Era: The Apprentice, Licensing, Trump University, the Foundation

The Apprentice premiered on NBC on January 8, 2004, created and co-produced by Mark Burnett, with Trump as host of a business-competition format ending in a boardroom elimination. He hosted fourteen seasons, seven under each title, through February 2015; NBC ended its business relationship with him on June 29, 2015, after the remarks about Mexican immigrants in his campaign announcement. The ratings record is Nielsen’s: the first season averaged 20.7 million viewers and ranked seventh, its finale 28.1 million; the decline ran season by season to his last, which averaged about 7.6 million and ranked 67th. His repeated description of the program, including in its later seasons, as “the No. 1 show on television” is contradicted by those rankings after the first season and is printed as his statement against the instrument. The money, on the two records that hold it: his 2015 Federal Election Commission disclosure reported about $213 million to $214 million of earnings from the show across eleven years; the tax records the Times examined put his direct Apprentice earnings at about $197 million and the licensing and endorsement income the fame generated at about $230 million more. The accounts do not conflict; they cover different periods and categories. The producers’ accounts of the construction are reported, named, and on the record: editors who described “reverse-engineering” episodes so that his boardroom decisions appeared reasoned, a boardroom built as a set on a vacant Trump Tower floor because his offices were judged unsuitable for the image, and, in October 2024, the apology of John D. Miller, the NBC marketing chief who wrote that the network had “created the narrative that Trump was a super-successful businessman who lived like royalty” and called it at best a substantial exaggeration. Burnett himself has given no comparable on-the-record account. The predicate fact for the word rehabilitation, where this report uses it as the producers’ frame and not the desk’s, is documented in the tax records: in the year before the premiere his core businesses had reported $89.9 million in net losses, and ten months after it his casino holding company entered Chapter 11.

The business model of the era was licensing: from the late 1980s, and dominantly after 2004, his entities sold the use of his name—an upfront fee, royalties on condominium sales or hotel revenue, and, in hotel deals, a separate management contract—contributing no land, no construction capital, and no equity, and bearing no construction debt, on the agreements that litigation has made public. The name on a building is therefore not, by itself, evidence of ownership, in either direction: Trump Tower and the golf portfolio were owned; many condominium and hotel projects carrying the name, in the United States and abroad, were licensed. Product licensing ran alongside: Trump Steaks, withdrawn within months in 2007 after, in the Sharper Image chief executive’s words, selling “almost no steaks”; Trump Vodka, Trump Mortgage, a magazine, a travel site, bottled water—the list is documented and most of its members are defunct. The Times’ tax-record analysis attributed about $230 million of licensing and endorsement income to the post-Apprentice years, including $8.8 million from ACN, a multi-level marketing company he endorsed in paid speeches.

Trump University was incorporated in New York in 2004, 93 percent his, and launched on May 23, 2005, selling real-estate seminars—a free session, a three-day seminar at about $1,495, mentoring packages to $34,995. It was not an accredited university and granted no degrees; the state Education Department told it in May 2005 that the word “university” and unlicensed operation violated state law, and it operated under the name until 2010. In 2014 a New York judge held him personally liable for operating without the required license. Three actions followed—two California class actions and the New York Attorney General’s suit, which alleged an elaborate bait-and-switch: instruction promised as handpicked by Trump, by the Attorney General’s account taught by instructors he had not chosen, one of whom had met him. Trump denied the allegations throughout and cited a 98 percent internal approval figure that the plaintiffs argued was solicited under pressure. On November 18, 2016, ten days after he won the presidency and ten days before trial, the parties settled all three cases for $25 million, with no admission of wrongdoing; the court estimated claiming students would recover roughly 80 to 90 percent of what they had paid. The settlement and its approval are documented and adjudicated as such; the fraud allegations were never adjudicated, settlement having ended the cases before any verdict, and this report files them as allegations resolved by payment, no more and no less.

The Donald J. Trump Foundation, formed in 1988, closed on two records: the Washington Post’s 2016 examination of its tax filings—$20,000 in 2007 for a six-foot portrait of Trump at a charity auction, $10,000 in 2014 for a second, $12,000 in 2012 for a signed football helmet and jersey, $258,000 applied to settle legal obligations of his businesses, and a $25,000 contribution in 2013 to a political group supporting Florida’s attorney general, whose office was then weighing whether to join the Trump University investigation, for which the foundation’s own tax form answered yes to a question about prohibited self-dealing and paid a $2,500 excise tax—and the Attorney General’s suit of June 2018. The foundation stipulated in December 2018 to dissolve under court supervision. On November 7, 2019, Justice Saliann Scarpulla ordered Trump to pay $2 million in damages, finding he had breached his fiduciary duty by allowing a January 2016 veterans fundraiser to be run by his campaign for political purposes; the settlement stipulations carried nineteen paragraphs of factual admissions, including that the board had not met since 1999 and that the foundation had engaged in the related-party transactions described. He paid the $2 million to eight named charities in December 2019, and the foundation’s remaining $1.8 million was distributed with it. It was a civil charity-enforcement action; no criminal charges were brought in the matter. The court characterized the $2 million as damages, not a fine, and the report follows the court.

7. The Personal Life Record

He has been married three times. Ivana Zelníčková, a Czech model, married him in 1977; their children are Donald Jr., Ivanka, and Eric. The marriage ended in a divorce decree of December 12, 1990, granted on grounds of cruel and inhuman treatment. He announced his candidacy on June 16, 2015, at Trump Tower. 9. The 2020 Election, January 6, and the Second Impeachment

The 2020 result is certified record: Biden 306 electoral votes, Trump 232; Biden 81,283,501 popular votes, Trump 74,223,975, a margin of about 7.06 million; every state certified, and Congress completed the count in the early hours of January 7, 2021. Trump did not concede, then or afterward; his consistent position, that the election was stolen, is recorded here as his position, against the findings below. Attorney General Barr stated on December 1, 2020, that the Justice Department had “not seen fraud on a scale that could have effected a different outcome.” The joint statement of federal and state election officials of November 12, 2020, called the election “the most secure in American history” and stated there was no evidence any voting system deleted, lost, or changed votes; Trump fired the official who issued it, Christopher Krebs, five days later.

January 6, 2021, is filed from the timeline. 10. The Legal Record, 2021 to 2026

Four criminal cases were brought against Donald Trump in 2023. One ended in conviction. Two federal cases were dismissed after the 2024 election under the Justice Department’s policy against prosecuting a sitting president. One state case was dismissed in November 2025 by a successor prosecutor. He pleaded not guilty in every criminal case, has denied wrongdoing in every matter in this section, and has described the prosecutions collectively as politically motivated; his denials are recorded at each matter’s head by this sentence and apply to all of them.

The Manhattan case. On March 30, 2023, a Manhattan grand jury indicted him on 34 counts of falsifying business records in the first degree. The prosecution’s theory rested on 34 records—11 invoices, 12 ledger entries, 11 checks—documenting $420,000 paid in 2017 to his former lawyer Michael Cohen, recorded as legal expenses, which reimbursed Cohen for a $130,000 payment Cohen made to the performer Stormy Daniels in late October 2016 under a nondisclosure agreement. Cohen had pleaded guilty in federal court in 2018 to campaign-finance violations for that payment and said he acted at Trump’s direction; Trump denied directing it and denied the affair. On May 30, 2024, after a six-week trial, a jury of twelve convicted on all 34 counts; he became the first former or sitting president convicted of a felony. On January 10, 2025, Justice Merchan imposed an unconditional discharge: no prison, no probation, no fine—the conviction final, the punishment none. The appeal, briefed by Sullivan & Cromwell, argues evidentiary error, reliance on official-act evidence after the Supreme Court’s immunity ruling, and a legal theory elevating time-barred misdemeanors into felonies through an uncharged federal election-law predicate. Recheck, November 10, 2026: the appeal was argued before a five-judge panel of the Appellate Division, First Department, on November 5, 2026, Robert Giuffra Jr. for Trump and Steven Wu for the District Attorney; the panel did not rule from the bench, announced no timetable, and no decision appears in the record as this report files. The New York civil fraud case. 11. The Return: 2024 and the Second Term

He announced the candidacy on November 15, 2022, and won the primaries by March 2024.
