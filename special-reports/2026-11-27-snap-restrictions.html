SNAP Twenty-Seven Days After November 1: The Beverage Restrictions, the Expanded Work Requirements, and the Proposed End of Broad-Based Categorical Eligibility

Filed from the record: the state of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program twenty-seven days after the November 1, 2026 restrictions—twelve states enforcing beverage and candy limits at the register, Virginia’s carbonation line and the drinks it leaves eligible, the first wave measured three times in the working-paper literature, the stigma the surveys recorded, the court ruling that vacated five waivers and the suit pending against the remaining ones, the work requirements of the 2025 reconciliation law, the proposed rule that would end broad-based categorical eligibility for a projected 6.3 million people, and the costs filed by the stores. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1200 ET, Friday, November 27, 2026.

On November 1, 2026, four more states—Montana, North Dakota, South Carolina, and Virginia—began refusing SNAP benefits for soda and other sweetened drinks at the register, bringing to twelve the number of states where the restrictions are in force. The refusals are the visible edge of a larger contraction. Behind them stand the expanded work requirements of the reconciliation law signed on July 4, 2025, which states have been applying at recertification through this year, and a proposed rule published on November 10, 2026, under which the Department of Agriculture’s own analysis projects that 6.3 million people would lose benefits entirely. Twenty-seven days after November 1, the record holds: the waiver list and its chronology, the Virginia rule in its particulars, three working papers measuring the first wave of restrictions, a federal court ruling that has already vacated five states’ waivers on the ground that the agency lacked the authority it used, a second lawsuit filed November 18 against the rest, and the register technology through which all of it is enforced. This report files that record in the order it ran. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

The Waivers

For most of its history, the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program has let participants buy any food for home consumption, soda and candy included; the Food and Nutrition Act defines eligible food broadly, and the Department of Agriculture historically declined to narrow it state by state. In 2018, under the first Trump administration, the department rejected restriction requests from Maine and Nevada, citing administrative cost, retailer burden, and a lack of evidence of health benefit—a position the current litigation quotes back to it. The position reversed in 2025. Beginning in May 2025, the department approved state requests, styled as demonstration projects under section 17(b) of the Food and Nutrition Act, 7 U.S.C. § 2026(b), to exclude specified items from the definition of food for purchase within their borders. As of July 2026, twenty-three states held approved restriction policies, tracked on the department’s SNAP Food Restriction Waivers page.

The restrictions took effect in waves. Indiana and Utah began on January 1, 2026; Idaho and Oklahoma on February 15; Louisiana on February 18; Texas on April 1; Florida on April 20; Arkansas on July 1. Montana, North Dakota, South Carolina, and Virginia followed on November 1. Nebraska, whose first waiver was vacated by the court ruling filed below, holds a re-approved waiver taking effect December 1. Wyoming, Kansas, and Missouri are scheduled for February 2027; Hawaii and Ohio for April 1, 2027—Ohio’s start, first set for the fall of 2026, was delayed at the department’s request; Nevada for February 1, 2028. Each state wrote its own list. Retailers are the enforcement mechanism: following the waivers is a condition of authorization to accept SNAP, point-of-sale systems must be reprogrammed state by state, and the department’s guidance allows a compliance grace period of ninety days from each start date.

November 1

The four November lists differ from one another, and the differences are the story of the patchwork. Montana restricts high-sugar beverages, energy drinks, candy, and prepared desserts. North Dakota restricts sweetened beverages, energy drinks, and candy. South Carolina restricts candy, energy drinks, soft drinks, and sweetened beverages—but leaves diet and zero-sugar sodas eligible, a line Virginia drew the other way. Virginia restricted beverages only, and only carbonated ones; no food item and no candy lost eligibility there. What a SNAP card buys now depends on the state the register stands in, and in three of the four November states on the candy aisle as well as the soda aisle.

Virginia

Virginia’s project is the narrowest of the four and the most precisely specified. The state requested the demonstration on November 4, 2025; the department approved it on December 10, 2025, and, after the state twice revised its start date, the approval notice ran in the Federal Register with an effective date of November 1, 2026. Under the state’s SNAP Healthy guidance, benefits may no longer buy soda, diet soda, “zero” or artificially sweetened soda, carbonated energy drinks, or carbonated drinks with five grams of added sugar or more per serving. The line the state drew is carbonation plus sweetener: non-carbonated sweetened drinks—lemonade, iced tea, sports drinks, juice and juice drinks—remain eligible, as do milk, infant formula, unsweetened seltzer, and non-carbonated energy drinks. A lemonade and a cola with the same sugar content now part at the register.

The department of social services states that benefit amounts, issuance schedules, and eligibility are unchanged, and that restricted drinks may be bought in the same transaction with other payment. Retailers carry the implementation: every SNAP-authorized retailer in Virginia, including online sellers and third-party fulfillment platforms, must block the restricted items, and the state supplies a list of affected products by Universal Product Code. Charlottesville station WVIR recorded the first days on both sides of the line—a state official describing a deliberate choice to restrict “only a specific narrow set of beverages,” a shopper answering that “you’re still taking away people’s choices,” and a recipient asking what is restricted next. Virginia pairs the restriction with Virginia Fresh Match, which doubles SNAP dollars spent on fruits and vegetables at participating outlets.

What the First Wave Measured

The November states are the second wave; the first, ten states whose restrictions began between January and April 2026, has been measured three times, in working papers not yet peer-reviewed. The baseline they measure against is the department’s own: in its analysis of 2011 point-of-sale data, published in 2016, soft drinks were the single largest commodity category purchased with SNAP dollars, at 5.4 percent, and sweetened beverages in the aggregate took 9.25 percent of SNAP household food spending.

Frisvold and Lozano-Rojas, in National Bureau of Economic Research Working Paper 35613 (August 2026), using Nielsen panel data, found the waivers decreased soda purchases by 13 percent for the average SNAP household, with effects persisting up to two months after implementation, and by 18.5 percent among households with enough non-SNAP funds to have offset the restriction entirely. Allcott, Finkelstein, Grummon, and Notowidigdo, in Working Paper 35659, using purchase records for 15,261 SNAP households, found purchases of excluded drinks fell 12.4 percent in the first half of 2026—about 404 fluid ounces per covered person per year, or thirty-four twelve-ounce cans. In states that excluded only some sugary drinks, households partially substituted to the drinks left eligible, offsetting up to 39 percent of the reduction in calories from the restricted items. Cooper, Seim, and Song, in Working Paper 35739, found restricted spending down 11 percent, purchased calories and sugar down 5 to 8 percent, and shelf prices essentially unchanged. The three papers, using different data, land within two points of one another.

The Register

The restrictions are enforced where the benefit has been deliberately made invisible. For four decades the program paid in paper coupons, printed like currency and torn from booklets at the register, visible to everyone in the checkout line. Electronic benefit transfer, piloted in Reading, Pennsylvania, in 1984, replaced the coupons with a card; by June 2004 every state had converted. The Department of Agriculture’s own history records that EBT let recipients pay “in the same way other customers used debit cards” and “reduced program stigma”; the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia’s 2004 assessment of the conversion lists “reduced stigmatization associated with the receipt of benefits” among its effects. In 2008 the farm bill renamed the program itself, replacing “food stamp” in the statute in part to shed the stigmatized phrase.

The restriction regime runs on the same card and the same register. Eligibility is now decided item by item inside the transaction: the eligible groceries process against the benefit, the restricted drinks do not, and the difference must be covered by a second form of payment or the items are put back. The Allcott survey work measured the register’s other output. In before-and-after surveys, the restrictions caused statistically significant increases of 0.10 to 0.17 standard deviations in three measures of recipients’ perceptions of stigma, among them feeling judged when paying with SNAP benefits and perceiving the state’s SNAP policies as disrespectful or as taking away personal freedom. Monetized through contingent-valuation surveys, the authors put the stigma cost of nationwide restrictions at $430 million to $660 million per year, against modeled benefits of about $1.1 billion per year, roughly 70 percent of it reduced publicly funded health care costs. The numbers are the authors’ model, and the desk files them as such.

The Courts

The legal record is ahead of the policy record. In March 2026, five SNAP recipients in Colorado, Iowa, Nebraska, Tennessee, and West Virginia, represented by the National Center for Law and Economic Justice, sued in the District of Columbia in Aragon v. Rollins, arguing that the department had exceeded its authority and skipped required procedure. On June 22, 2026, Judge Amy Berman Jackson granted summary judgment and vacated the five approvals. The ruling held that an agency may not waive the statutory definition of “food” that Congress laid down; that nutrition-focused demonstrations are governed by a different provision, section 2026(k), which requires publicly available criteria and rigorous outcome evaluation that the department had bypassed by proceeding under section 2026(b); and that the department had failed to publish a required thirty-day notice in the Federal Register. The government appealed to the D.C. Circuit in August. Nebraska resubmitted its request and holds the December 1 start filed above.

On November 18, 2026, recipients in eighteen states—Arkansas, Florida, Hawaii, Idaho, Indiana, Kansas, Louisiana, Missouri, Montana, Nevada, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Texas, Utah, Virginia, and Wyoming—filed a second suit, joined by the South Carolina Convenience & Petroleum Marketers Association, asking the court to vacate the remaining waivers and to certify nationwide classes of recipients. The complaint cites Aragon, the 2018 rejections, and the industry’s compliance-cost study filed below. The four November 1 states proceeded, in the interim, under the same section 17(b) authority the June ruling held insufficient; the department’s stated position is that it has broad authority to approve projects that test program changes. Both cases are pending. The restrictions remain in force in the twelve active states.

The Other Contraction

The beverage rules govern what the card can buy. The reconciliation law governs who holds a card. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025, expanded the work requirements for able-bodied adults without dependents from ages 18 to 54 up to age 64, narrowed the exemption for parents and caregivers from a child under 18 to a child under 14, and removed the exemptions for veterans, people experiencing homelessness, and former foster youth. A non-exempt adult must work or train twenty hours a week to keep benefits past three months. The changes were effective on enactment and reach recipients as states apply them at application and recertification; the law also raises the state share of administrative costs to 75 percent in federal fiscal year 2027 and, from October 2027, ties a state share of benefit costs to its payment error rate. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities estimates that more than 5.5 million people, including about two million children, have left SNAP since the law’s enactment—an estimate the desk files as its author’s, not as a count.

The proposed rule of November 10, 2026, “Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program: Reforming Categorical Eligibility,” would narrow broad-based categorical eligibility—the policy, used by forty-six states, under which households receiving TANF-funded benefits or services qualify for SNAP without a separate income and asset test—to households receiving TANF cash assistance. The department’s regulatory impact analysis projects that 6.3 million individuals in 3.2 million households, 17 percent of SNAP households, would lose benefits entirely; that benefits would be cut by $50.82 billion, with federal savings of $44.65 billion over fiscal years 2027–2031; that administrative costs would rise by $1.24 billion; and that 1.78 million children would lose automatic eligibility for free school meals. The rule is a proposal: comments run thirty days from publication, and the department projects full effect in fiscal year 2029 if it is finalized. On November 19, more than two hundred House members wrote the Secretary urging rescission; the department has not answered in the record filed here.

The Retail Ledger

The costs of the patchwork land on the stores. A 2025 survey of members by the National Grocers Association, FMI—The Food Industry Association, and the National Association of Convenience Stores, conducted before the restrictions began, estimated compliance at $1.558 billion up front and $759.1 million a year ongoing, with 65.8 percent of the up-front cost falling on convenience stores; the associations count more than 650,000 products requiring review against lists that differ by state. The penalty behind the arithmetic is authorization itself: a retailer that fails compliance can be barred from accepting SNAP. Casey’s General Stores, the third-largest convenience chain in the country, wrote the department that the loss of SNAP authorization “can have significant consequences for communities that rely on a local retailer like Casey’s as an accessible source of food.” On October 30, the department notified grocers in the four November states that the restrictions took effect the next day. The department’s public statement of the policy’s purpose stands on its waivers page: the waivers ensure “that taxpayer dollars provide nutritious options that improve health outcomes within SNAP.”

Where the Record Stands

On November 27, 2026, twenty-seven days into the November wave: restrictions are in force in twelve states and scheduled in seven more; four states’ approvals stand vacated by a court whose reasoning the government is appealing; a second suit against the remaining waivers is nine days old; the comment period on the categorical-eligibility rule has about two weeks to run; Nebraska’s re-approved restrictions begin December 1. No state has yet published evaluation data from the November demonstrations—Virginia’s approval notice states the department will review results using the state’s evaluation data—and the three measurements on file cover the first wave only, in states whose lists were drawn differently. The desk files the record as it stands and draws no conclusion from it.
