The Intermediaries, Filed From the Record

Objective

The Intermediaries: The Paid Economy Between Americans and Their Benefits

Filed from the record: the paid layer between Americans and their benefits—the disability fee the government withholds and mails itself, the firms and lead sellers it supports, the fraud that ran on it at $550 million, the marketplace’s commission door and grant door, the 1985 telephone program and its 2009 nickname, and the kitchen in Washington where breakfast is the front end of an application. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1500 ET, Friday, November 27, 2026.

Between an American and a federal benefit there usually stands a paid intermediary. The disability claimant hires a representative whose fee the Social Security Administration itself withholds from back pay and mails to the representative. The health insurance shopper is enrolled, in most cases, by a broker whose commission the insurer pays. The homeless man who walks into a church kitchen for breakfast meets a case manager whose salary a federal grant pays, whose work is to connect him to the benefits. Its prices are set in statutes and regulations, its largest payment rail is operated by the government itself, and its scale is printed in the government’s own reports: in the five calendar years 2018 through 2022, the Social Security Administration paid claimant representatives about $6.5 billion out of past-due benefits. This report files the record of the layer in its parts: the fee, the firms, the market in claimants, the fraud case, the marketplace’s two doors, the telephone program, and the kitchen. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

The Fee, Set by Statute

The disability representative’s price is fixed by law. Under the fee agreement process in the Social Security Administration’s fee agreement rules, a representative who wins a claim is paid the lesser of 25 percent of the claimant’s past-due benefits or a dollar cap set by the Commissioner. The cap stood at $6,000 from 2009 through November 29, 2022, rose to $7,200 on November 30, 2022, and rose again to $9,200 on November 30, 2024, under a Federal Register notice of May 10, 2024. A representative may instead file a fee petition and ask the agency to approve a larger amount; if the claim fails, the agreement pays nothing.

The government is the collection mechanism: on a win, the agency withholds the fee from past-due benefits and pays the representative directly. The agency charges the representative a user fee for the service—6.3 percent of each payment, subject to a cap. For fiscal years 2015 and 2016, its inspector general counted about 1.1 million direct fee payments totaling about $2.6 billion for 761,039 claims; the user fees recovered $60 million of the $264 million the processing cost—23 percent. For calendar years 2018 through 2022, the inspector general’s summary counts approximately $5.4 billion paid to representatives from past-due retirement and disability benefits—between about $924 million and $1.2 billion a year—and approximately $1.1 billion more from past-due SSI.

Representation is the norm, not the exception: a study of the agency’s administrative data by National Bureau of Economic Research researchers found that 82 percent of hearing-stage claimants had a representative in 2007 to 2014, that fees totaled $1.2 billion in 2019, and that representation raised the probability of an initial-stage award by an estimated 23 percentage points. The same study records the door the non-attorney firms entered through: in 2010, Congress allowed qualified non-lawyers to be paid through the same fee withholding.

The Firms

The fee built national firms. Binder and Binder, founded by brothers Harry and Charles Binder and advertised on television as one of the nation’s largest disability practices, filed for Chapter 11 on December 18, 2014, in the Southern District of New York. The Wall Street Journal’s report counted 966 employees and nearly 58,000 active cases, with assets and liabilities each between $10 million and $50 million.

The non-attorney companies work the same rail. Allsup, headquartered in Belleville, Illinois, was founded in 1984 by Jim Allsup, a former Social Security Administration employee, as the first nationwide non-attorney provider of disability representation; the company reports more than 425,000 customers approved across its history. Its fact sheet records a second customer besides the claimant: long-term disability insurers and self-insured companies refer their policyholders and former employees to Allsup, because a government award offsets what the insurer must pay. Citizens Disability, a Waltham, Massachusetts advocacy firm, states the statutory terms in its own brochure—nothing up front, and on a win 25 percent of the back pay up to the government maximum—and, according to a federal complaint filed in 2025, marketed through a subsidiary operating under names including National Disability, American Disability, and Disability Referral.

The Leads

Above the firms sits a market in the claimants themselves. Lead generation companies advertise the product openly: exclusive Social Security disability leads—web inquiries and live call transfers from people seeking help with a claim—screened on work history, medical treatment, and prior denial, and sold at a fixed cost per lead priced by state and volume. The firm buys the inquiry; the fee, if the claim succeeds, comes later, out of the back pay.

The market’s documented edge is in the complaint the United States filed on September 30, 2025, in the District of Massachusetts against Citizens Disability and its marketing subsidiary, CD Media, on notification from the Federal Trade Commission. The complaint alleges that the defendants sold their advocacy services through telemarketing campaigns that initiated millions of unlawful robocalls and millions of calls to Do Not Call Registry numbers, and misrepresented that the calls responded to consumers’ own inquiries about disability benefits; it alleges that CD Media contracted with third-party lead generators and call centers to run the campaigns. The allegations are unproven as filed; the case is pending.

The Fraud Exhibit

The representative system’s largest criminal case ran on the same rail. Eric Christopher Conn, a Pikeville, Kentucky disability lawyer who advertised himself as “Mr. Social Security,” pleaded guilty on March 24, 2017, to theft of government money and paying illegal gratuities. By the admissions in his later plea, from October 2004 he ran a scheme with David Black Daugherty, a Social Security administrative law judge, clinical psychologist Alfred Bradley Adkins, and others, submitting thousands of falsified medical documents to obtain disability benefits for thousands of individuals. The Justice Department’s sentencing release put the benefits obtained at more than $550 million; its fraud section’s annual review put the lifetime value above $600 million, and recorded more than $7.5 million in representative fees paid to Conn himself and more than $70 million paid to claimants the government deemed ineligible.

The exposure is also in the record. Two employees of the agency’s Huntington, West Virginia hearing office, Sarah Carver and Jennifer Griffith, reported the pattern—Conn’s cases funneled to Judge Daugherty, who approved 90 to 99 percent of them in 2005 to 2010, on the Association of Certified Fraud Examiners’ account—and, according to the Justice Department, Conn conspired with a second judge, Charlie Paul Andrus, to retaliate against the employee who informed. Andrus pleaded guilty to conspiracy to retaliate against an informant and received six months. Daugherty pleaded guilty in May 2017 to receiving illegal gratuities and was sentenced that August to four years; Adkins was convicted at trial and sentenced to 25 years.

Conn’s own sentence came in two parts. On June 2, 2017, after his guilty plea and before sentencing, he severed the electronic monitor from his ankle and fled across the Mexican border. On July 14, 2017, he was sentenced in absentia to 12 years. Apprehended in Honduras, he was returned to the United States on December 5, 2017. In September 2018, on convictions for conspiracy to defraud the United States, conspiracy to escape, and conspiracy to retaliate against an informant, the court added 15 consecutive years—27 in all—and ordered $72,574,609 in restitution, in what the department called the largest fraud scheme in the history of the Social Security program. His former clients, including claimants accused of nothing, faced agency reviews of the awards he had won them.

The Marketplace’s Two Doors

The health insurance marketplaces built two doors and pay for them differently. Through the first door stand agents and brokers: licensed by the states, permitted to recommend a specific plan, and paid by the health insurers—commissions the Department of Health and Human Services’ planning office puts at $5 to $30 per member per month. Through the second stand navigators and other assisters: grant-funded organizations whose staff the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services’ guidance bars from receiving consideration, directly or indirectly, from health insurers in connection with an enrollment, and who may not recommend one plan over another.

The commission door carries the traffic. Brokers facilitated 43 percent of HealthCare.gov enrollments in 2016, the first year for which the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services published the figure; by 2024, according to the department’s planning office, agents and brokers had facilitated 78 percent of active plan selections, up from 55 percent in 2021. Since 2018, web-brokers have enrolled consumers through Enhanced Direct Enrollment platforms plugged directly into the federal system. The channel’s integrity record is also published: from January through August 2024 the agency received 90,863 complaints of plans changed without consent and 183,553 of enrollment without consent, and suspended 850 agents and brokers; in 2025 it canceled coverage for 250,000 people enrolled without consent.

The grant door is measured by its funder. In its announcement of February 14, 2025, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services reported that navigators had received $98 million for the 2024 plan year and had enrolled 92,000 consumers—0.6 percent of plan selections through the federally facilitated exchanges—at a cost of $1,061 per enrollment. The same announcement reduced navigator funding to $10 million a year for the rest of the grant period running to August 2029, stating that the reduction would save $360 million over four years in exchange user fees, toward lower premiums. Navigator funding had stood at $100 million for the 2025 plan year, the largest amount in the program’s history to that point.

The Phone

The Federal Communications Commission created the Lifeline program in 1985, during the Reagan administration, after the AT&T breakup, to discount basic telephone service for low-income households. It is funded not from income taxes but from the Universal Service Fund, which carriers pay into and recover in part through a line item on customers’ bills. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 codified the program. In 2005 the commission made wireless carriers and resellers eligible; in 2008 it approved TracFone, whose SafeLink brand became the first large marketer of a free phone bundled with Lifeline minutes. The discount stands at $9.25 a month. Eligibility runs to households at or below 135 percent of the federal poverty guidelines or participating in programs including SNAP, Medicaid, and SSI, and is checked through the Universal Service Administrative Company’s National Verifier, rolled out between 2018 and 2020; subscribers enroll through participating providers.

The program’s popular name arrived a generation late. The label “Obama phone” spread from about 2009—twenty-four years after the program’s creation, and a year after the reseller approval that put free phones in the market, under the preceding administration.

The Kitchen Door

The nonprofit layer is funded by grants, and the largest federal grant stream for homeless services is the Department of Housing and Urban Development’s Continuum of Care program. On January 17, 2025, the department announced nearly $3.6 billion in awards for fiscal 2024—the largest annual amount in the program’s history—covering nearly 7,000 projects of nonprofit providers, states, tribes, and local governments. The mechanics are printed in the department’s funding notices: for fiscal 2024 and 2025 a single two-year competition; communities apply to renew existing projects, fund new ones, and reallocate money from lower-performing projects; each project’s renewal amount is inventoried in advance; and fiscal 2024 added a cost-of-living adjustment to project budgets for the first time.

Miriam’s Kitchen, in the Foggy Bottom neighborhood of Washington, was founded in October 1983 by Western Presbyterian Church, Unity Church, and the George Washington University Hillel, and served breakfast only. Its programs page records the growth: breakfast and dinner every weekday, nearly 300 meals a day and about 75,000 a year, street outreach, and housing support for more than 215 people. The organization’s stated philosophy, recorded in a 2011 profile, is that “food brings the guests in, but once they’re with us we can begin to help them beyond meeting their basic needs.” The help is case management, offered whenever the doors are open: assistance obtaining identification cards—the document without which benefit applications cannot proceed—mental health care, and links to housing. The organization’s own summary of its direct services is that they help guests “increase their income, improve their health, and obtain housing.” In 2012 the organization changed its mission from responding to homelessness to ending chronic homelessness in the District.

Where the Record Stands

On November 27, 2026, the record stands as follows. The disability fee cap stands at $9,200, with representative fees running at about $1.3 billion a year across both programs on the last five-year summary. The government’s telemarketing case against Citizens Disability and CD Media is pending in the District of Massachusetts. Navigator funding stands at $10 million a year under a grant period running to August 2029, while insurer-paid brokers facilitated 78 percent of the federal marketplace’s active plan selections at the last published count. The Continuum of Care competition for fiscal 2026, offering more than $4 billion, is the subject of litigation noted on the department’s own competition page. The Lifeline discount stands at $9.25 a month, forty-one years after the program’s creation and seventeen after its nickname. The desk files the record as it stands and draws no conclusion from it.
