The Case File—Allegations, Findings, Lawsuit, and the Reopened Investigation

Objective

The Cornell 7 Case: The Chi Phi Allegations, the Signed Statement and the Transcript, Cornell’s Findings, the Civil Lawsuit, and the Special Prosecutor — Special Report

On the night of October 19–20, 2024, a 20-year-old Cornell undergraduate went to the Chi Phi fraternity house at 107 Edgemoor Lane in Ithaca. What happened in that house is the contested center of this file. She alleges, in a civil complaint filed in September 2026, that she was pressured to take ketamine, incapacitated, and sexually assaulted over hours by seven members of the fraternity while a group chat summoned more men upstairs. The district attorney who declined to charge in 2024 states that the sworn statement she signed that November described her participation as voluntary, conscious, and consensual, and did not allege that she was drugged against her will or gang raped. The transcript of her police interview, excerpted in 2026 reporting, contains words the signed summary does not: “I can say with 100% confidence I was raped.” Cornell’s disciplinary panel, judging under a preponderance standard, expelled two of the men, suspended two, barred one from campus, and imposed lesser sanctions on two, and found none of them responsible for every allegation against him. No court has heard evidence on what occurred in the room, and no criminal charge has been filed against any of the seven as of November 9, 2026. Around that open center the institutions have moved: a civil lawsuit against Cornell, three Chi Phi entities, two Tri Delta entities, a bar, and the seven men; a reopened criminal case from which the governor removed the local prosecutor and installed the attorney general as special prosecutor; a university president who kept his board’s confidence and lost his faculty’s, 75 to 50; and a statute whose definition of incapacitation the whole criminal question turns on. The desk files the whole record, attributed and graded, in that order.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1405 ET, Monday, November 9, 2026.

The File in Brief

The case has produced three proceedings, two irreconcilable documents, and no criminal charge. Jane Doe—the name under which the complainant proceeds in every filing, her anonymity formally granted by the court on October 23, 2026—reported to the Cornell University Police Department on November 8, 2024, about three weeks after the night at issue. Campus police interviewed her over two days, November 14–15, 2024, producing a transcript reported at approximately 120 pages and an officer’s six-page written summary, which she read, edited in more than twenty handwritten notations, and signed under the false-statement penalty of Penal Law §210.45. The Tompkins County district attorney, Matthew Van Houten, decided within days not to pursue charges, and stated his reasons in full on September 28, 2026: the signed statement “did not allege that she was drugged against her will or gang raped” and “described her participation in drug use and sexual conduct as voluntary, conscious, and consensual”; applying Penal Law §130.05, “the alleged facts as set forth in the statement do not constitute a crime.” The interview transcript, as reported by CBS News, The New York Times, and CNN from the same document, contains her statements that she was “completely and totally incapacitated,” that she was “physically hit very, very hard,” and the sentence the summary omits: “I can say with 100% confidence I was raped.” Whether the transcript reached the district attorney in 2024 is disputed by the university, the prosecutor, and the governor, and the public record does not settle it. That dispute, printed in Section 4 at its full length, is the hinge on which every later event swings.

The university’s proceeding ran to completion in 2025, under a different standard and a different record. A three-member Cornell panel heard the cases over hearings running May 2–29, 2025, under Policy 6.4’s preponderance-of-the-evidence standard, and concluded that Doe was too intoxicated to affirmatively consent that night, as the panel summaries are reported. Its decision is dated August 18, 2025. Of the seven men: Matthew Ingalls and Johnathan Newell were expelled, each found responsible for sexual assault; Winston Lee received a two-year suspension; Gillio Lopes a three-semester suspension; Diego Sarabia, already graduated, was made persona non grata and barred from campus for three years; Scott Norris received a no-contact order and education modules; and Scott Kretzschmar, found not responsible for sexual assault or sexual exploitation as charged and responsible only for attempted sexual exploitation, received a no-contact order, education, and a reflection paper. Cornell states that published accounts of individual punishments are inaccurate and that federal privacy law bars it from confirming individual records; the outcomes above are as reported by The New York Times from Cornell’s disciplinary summaries and corroborated by the Cornell Daily Sun and other outlets, and they are printed under Cornell’s caveat. The Chi Phi chapter was closed in 2024 and remains barred from campus; the national fraternity states the chapter voted to remove the accused members from its rolls three days after Doe’s police report, and that it permanently expelled the chapter. One accused man, Kretzschmar, sued Cornell to overturn his record; a state judge dismissed his petition on September 23, 2026, and his appeal is pending.

The civil lawsuit, filed September 16, 2026, is in its first motion practice, and no court has ruled on its merits. Jane Doe v. Cornell University, Inc., et al., Index No. 161704/2026 in New York County, pleads ten causes of action in 101 pages: sexual assault, sexual battery, and intentional infliction of emotional distress against the seven men; negligence and negligent supervision against Cornell, the Greek organizations, and the bar; breach of contract against Cornell and Tri Delta; claims against Cornell under the New York Human Rights Law and Education Law §6440; and a Dram Shop Act claim against Zareen Enterprise LLC, doing business as Moonies Bar & Nightclub. No entity connected to Lot 10, the second bar in the night’s narrative, is a defendant. One man originally named, Orazio Petito, was discontinued with prejudice on October 20, 2026, after reporting established he had been misidentified as chapter president; he was never accused of participating in the alleged assault. Diego Sarabia’s motion to dismiss, returnable November 16, 2026, and Moonies’ motion to dismiss are pending. The court declined on October 16 to sign a proposed order restraining counsel’s public statements, and ordered the filings containing addresses sealed on October 9, after an uninvolved man who shares a defendant’s name was served by mistake and his family threatened.

The criminal case reopened on September 28, 2026, changed hands on October 1, and is under investigation with no deadline. Van Houten announced the reopening in the same statement that defended the 2024 declination, assigning a senior sex-crimes prosecutor and planning a grand jury presentation. Governor Kathy Hochul signed Executive Order No. 64 on October 1, 2026, under Executive Law §63(2), its operative words to “displace and supersede” the district attorney, and appointed Attorney General Letitia James as special prosecutor. James announced an active criminal investigation on October 2 and on November 4 appointed Jacqueline Kelly, a partner at Boies Schiller Flexner and a former federal prosecutor who oversaw the prosecution of Sean Combs, to lead it. Five of the seven men are parties to a proceeding, Lopes v. Hochul and James, asking a court to remove James for statements they say show prejudgment; it was heard October 29, 2026, and no ruling has issued. A separate motion in Tompkins County seeks to bar James from any grand jury proceeding in the case; a decision is expected November 13, 2026. The two-year limitations period for misdemeanors arising from October 2024 has run; New York sets no limitations period for the most serious sex offenses.

The university’s leadership has been judged twice in one week, with opposite results. On November 6, 2026, Cornell’s Faculty Senate passed the first no-confidence resolution in its history, 75 to 50 with 5 abstentions and 5 non-votes, resolving that it “has no confidence in the ability of the central administration to effectively address the ongoing crisis of sexual assault at Cornell University.” The Board of Trustees answered within hours that it “remains confident that President Kotlikoff and his administration are the right leaders” and “fully supports” him; President Michael Kotlikoff has told The New York Times he has no intention of resigning. A Board committee has retained Sally Yates, the former deputy attorney general, to review Cornell’s handling of the case and its systems entire; the review has no announced timeline and no findings. The City of Ithaca adopted a permit moratorium against Cornell on October 7, 2026, and let it lapse on November 5, on a single local source’s reporting, with three demands outstanding and revival threatened. Cornell has suspended all Interfraternity Council house parties through the end of the 2026–27 academic year and signed an agreement under which a survivor pursuing criminal charges may choose the New York State Police or the Cornell police as the investigating agency.

What the record does not establish is as much a part of this file as what it does. It does not establish what occurred in the room, in what sequence, or each man’s individual conduct; no court has heard that evidence. It does not establish whether ketamine was administered to Doe without her consent, the element New York’s definition of mental incapacitation turns on and the point the complaint and the district attorney’s reading of her signed statement divide over. It publishes no toxicology or rape-kit results: the only documented medical fact of that kind is that a rape kit was collected at an Ithaca emergency room on November 7, 2024. It does not support the circulated claim that the men’s only punishment was writing essays; reflection papers were components of packages that included two expulsions. And it contains claims this report corrects rather than repeats: no Lot 10 entity is sued; the New York Times text-messages presentation carries no verified November 7 date; the BBC’s explainer is dated September 30, 2026; a separately reported 2023 lawsuit against Cornell was not unsealed in 2026 and drew no response from any SUNY authority, Cornell being a private university. The grades used throughout are the file’s own: VERIFIED, established by a primary document or multiple independent reputable reports; DOCUMENTED, established by a single reputable report or a reputable report of a document; ALLEGED or CLAIMED, an assertion by a party, counsel, or a filing, which is not a finding. Where accounts conflict, this report prints the conflict and does not reconcile it. The final section is the first section’s restatement, as the form requires: three proceedings, two irreconcilable documents, no criminal charge, and an investigation now in a special prosecutor’s hands.

1. The State of the Case: No Charges, and the Standing Cautions of the File

No criminal charges have been filed against any of the seven men as of November 9, 2026, and that fact governs every line that follows. It is verified across all reporting the desk read. Each of the seven is presumed innocent in the criminal matter. The only adjudications to date are a Cornell disciplinary panel’s findings under a preponderance standard, which are not criminal verdicts, and procedural rulings in the civil case, none of which reaches the merits. All seven men deny wrongdoing. The complainant is identified only as Jane Doe, as in the filings; this report makes no attempt to identify her and reproduces no purported identification, and the documented efforts by others to name her are filed in Section 11 as events, because they caused documented harm to uninvolved people. The seven men are named only as the public record names them, in the civil complaint’s caption and in reputable reporting, with their denials and their individual disciplinary outcomes attached to their names wherever the names appear. The desk’s research file for this report rests on primary documents read in full or in part—the district attorney’s September 28, 2026 statement, the civil summons and complaint, Executive Order No. 64 and Executive Law §63(2), Penal Law §§130.00 and 130.05, the Ithaca Common Council’s moratorium resolution, Cornell’s statements, and the governor’s October 2 news-conference transcript—and on reporting by The New York Times, The New Yorker, CNN, CBS News, ABC News, the BBC, the Associated Press, Reuters, USA Today, NBC News, NPR, The Washington Post, the Cornell Daily Sun, New York Focus, Le Monde, and 607 News Now.

2. The Night as the Complaint Alleges It: October 19–20, 2024

The complaint’s account is a pleading, not evidence, and it is printed here in its own terms, at the grade ALLEGED. Doe was a 20-year-old Cornell undergraduate, a member of the Alpha Beta chapter of Delta Delta Delta, living in the sorority house. On October 19, 2024, the complaint states, she drank at the sorority house—it says she had “binged” vodka, and that on arrival at the fraternity house she “was visibly intoxicated as she had consumed about 10 standard drinks within the past 3 hours”—then attended an event at Moonies Bar & Nightclub, where she alleges she was served alcohol despite markings on her hand indicating she was under 21, and then went to a second bar, Lot 10. At about 11 p.m. she left and walked alone, roughly a mile, to the Chi Phi house at 107 Edgemoor Lane to see Matthew Ingalls, a friend, on CNN’s timeline drawn from the complaint. The complaint alleges that Ingalls proposed a threesome with Johnathan Newell, the proposition pleaded as having been made when Doe was already “incapable of consenting”; that she was pressured to snort a substance described as ketamine; that she became incapacitated; and that over the following hours multiple fraternity members sexually assaulted her, snorted ketamine off her body, took photographs of her, and posted them to a fraternity group chat. A message in the “Chi Phi Actives” Snapchat group announced “free pussy” upstairs; the complaint reproduces a screenshot of the message and says it was meant to draw other members to the room. A man identified in the complaint’s room sequence as Alex Vega is named in the narrative and is not a defendant. Newell is alleged to have performed a ketamine-on-body act. The final alleged assault is placed in a smaller room, and the complaint alleges the assaults continued until approximately 5:45 a.m.

Against the institutions, the complaint pleads a failure before the night and a failure after it. It alleges Cornell knew or should have known of drug use at the fraternity and of risks of sexual violence in its Greek system, failed to supervise or warn, failed to discipline adequately afterward, “shelter[ed]” the accused students “from speaking to law enforcement,” and allowed suspended students “the opportunity to mitigate their conduct by submitting essays to Cornell”—the essay-mitigation claim Cornell expressly denies, at Section 5. It describes Cornell’s conduct as “so outrageous, shocking, despicable, and contemptible that it exceeds the reasonable bounds of decency.” The complaint states that Doe reported to the Cornell University Police Department on November 8, 2024, about three weeks after the night, and that Cornell suspended the Chi Phi chapter the same day. Both of those dated facts are verified independently of the pleading.

3. The Two Documents: The Signed Statement and the Interview Transcript

The central evidentiary conflict of the case is documentary, and both documents are in the record. The first is the six-page sworn statement Doe signed for the campus police in November 2024: single-spaced, typewritten, carrying more than twenty handwritten notations by Doe, signed under Penal Law §210.45. Its full text is not public. What is public is District Attorney Van Houten’s account of it in his September 28, 2026 statement, verified as his account: the statement describes Doe texting Ingalls and planning to meet at the house; it rates her own intoxication “5 out of 10” on arrival; it describes an agreed threesome; her refusal of a proposed foursome—“Hell No”—which was honored; a man’s hand swatted away; and her decision to take a second use of ketamine. Per Van Houten, it states that when she said stop, the conduct stopped, and that events ended at about 5:45 a.m. His conclusion, in his words: the statement “did not allege that she was drugged against her will or gang raped” and “described her participation in drug use and sexual conduct as voluntary, conscious, and consensual.”

The signed statement was not devoid of non-consent content, and the file prints that precision because it narrows the dispute to its true size. Per NPR and ABC reporting on the statement’s contents, the signed statement retained language that the encounter “felt like coercion,” that Doe described herself as “pretty incapacitated,” that she was physically hit, and references to “eightsome” bragging. The documented dispute is therefore not whether the statement contained any non-consent element; it is whether the summary omitted the explicit rape characterization that the interview record carries. Printed so, neither side’s framing is overstated.

The second document is the transcript of the campus police interview of November 14–15, 2024, reported at approximately 120 pages and obtained by CBS News in 2026. In the transcript, per the reporting of CBS, The New York Times, and CNN from the same document, Doe said: “I can say with 100% confidence I was raped.” She said she was “completely and totally incapacitated.” She said she was “physically hit very, very hard.” She said the encounter “felt like coercion.” The six-page signed summary does not contain the “100 percent” statement; that is verified as a fact of the two documents’ comparison, per the same reporting and the governor’s October 2 remarks. Per documents ABC News reviewed, Doe’s initial intake report to the campus police recorded that she reported she “was recently raped by 8-10 men at the Chi Phi fraternity house”—a phrase that likewise does not appear in the six-page statement sent to prosecutors. The full transcript is not public. The conflict is printed, not reconciled: the complaint alleges coerced ketamine use, incapacitation, and gang rape; the district attorney’s summary of the signed 2024 statement describes voluntary, conscious, consensual participation with refusals honored. Both documents exist. No public record reconciles them. Doe’s counsel’s position is that the summary omitted what she told interviewers orally; the dispute over who held the transcript in 2024 is filed at Section 4.

The messages of the night and the weeks after are documented in the Title IX record, the complaint, and the reporting, and they are filed here with their senders only where the record fixes them. The complaint reproduces a screenshot from the “Chi Phi Actives” Snapchat group, which the complaint’s image indicates had 57 members. At about 1:42 a.m. a message announced there was “free pussy” upstairs; the Times’ interactive rendering reads “Free pussy in alum pu[—],” apparently a room designation, and a photograph of Doe was posted in the thread. Further messages, as reproduced in the Times interactive and verified against multiple reproductions: a member asked whether the “shop” was still open and was told “Yeah”; another wrote “Boys come whip it out”; members joked about a named member being with her. ABC News separately verified small-group-chat messages among members. Attribution, as documented: the complaint attributes the “free pussy” message to Newell; CBS News and Wikipedia’s synthesis attribute “Boys come whip it out” to Ingalls; and Kretzschmar and Norris each acknowledged sending a message in the thread, without the thread’s worst content being attributed to either by those acknowledgments. Beyond those items, the record does not fix senders, and this report does not. A dating caveat attaches to the Times’ presentation of the thread, the interactive titled “Read the Text Messages at the Center of the Cornell Rape Inquiry”: no Times Cornell text-messages piece bearing a November 7 dateline was located; the interactive surfaces in a push notification logged November 8, 2026, at 22:54 UTC, and the substantive Times text corpus is its October 1, 2026 article. 4. The First Criminal Decision: The Declination of November 2024 and the Dispute Over the File

The local handling ran on a short clock. Doe reported on November 8, 2024; Cornell issued a public-safety alert and temporarily suspended the Chi Phi Xi chapter the same day, both verified. The alert, per Wesley Lowery’s account, said a student reported she had been coerced into consuming ketamine and marijuana and sexually assaulted “by multiple males” at a fraternity house in the 100 block of Edgemoor Lane. A date conflict attaches to the alert and is printed unresolved: the alert as reproduced in the research record gives the incident date as October 25, against October 19–20 in every other account, including the complaint and the district attorney’s statement; no source reconciles the discrepancy, and this file does not assume it an error. The campus police interviewed Doe on November 14–15; she read, edited, and signed the officer’s six-page written statement on November 15. On November 15 the campus police also drafted letters to Verizon and T-Mobile seeking phone records in a sexual-assault investigation, drafts obtained by CNN in 2026; whether the district attorney’s office knew of the records requests is not established. In November 2024 the campus police asked the Tompkins County district attorney’s office to review Doe’s statement for criminality. Van Houten has said he decided within days of the interviews not to pursue charges. His office received, on his account, the six-page signed statement and a Snapchat screenshot; he told CNN his office “never received more than the Snapchat messages and a six-page investigative report.” Times-obtained records show the district attorney’s office declined to review additional evidence the campus police offered after the decision, an investigator’s note recording: “Criminal charges would not be pursued in this case.” Van Houten later characterized the additional material offered around November 25, 2024 as “not pertinent,” on the ground that his office already had “Jane Doe’s comprehensive account,” and stated the extra interviews were of fraternity members declining to speak on counsel’s advice; he told NBC News the same of the file’s interviews. His characterization sits beside, and does not settle, the dispute below. The Ithaca Police Department, the municipal force, says it never received a report and learned of the allegations from the media; the civil complaint initially stated, incorrectly per the department, that Ithaca police were investigating.

The district attorney’s stated reasons are a primary document, and they are printed in his words. Van Houten’s September 28, 2026 press statement, issued when he reopened the case, is the fullest statement of the 2024 reasoning. “Jane Doe’s sworn statement in November of 2024 did not allege that she was drugged against her will or gang raped. On the contrary, Jane Doe’s statement described her participation in drug use and sexual conduct as voluntary, conscious, and consensual.” Applying Penal Law §130.05, he wrote that the statement established none of the routes to lack of consent: no forcible compulsion; no mental incapacitation, a term the statute defines around a substance “administered to him without his consent”; no physical helplessness; and “no evidence… indicating that she clearly expressed that she did not consent”—noting that, per the statement, each time she asked that conduct stop, it stopped. “The alleged facts as set forth in the statement do not constitute a crime,” and the November 2024 decision “was legally correct.” Of the men’s conduct he wrote in the same period, as reported by ABC: “She never says that she was drugged without her knowledge. She admits to taking drugs and alcohol voluntarily and with knowledge”; the behavior of some fraternity members was “without question disgusting and morally reprehensible,” but “criminal prosecutions must be based upon evidence and not emotion.” He stated his office “obviously did not and could not have based our November 2024 decision on the allegations in a civil lawsuit that would not be filed until nearly two years later.” He has said he never personally told Doe or her lawyer of the decision and asked the campus police to convey it. Her counsel, Thomas Giuffra, says Doe was left believing a criminal investigation was continuing while Cornell’s internal review ran: “I think she was misled. She was told it was still—they weren’t going to prosecute, but they were still investigating.” The parties’ accounts compete; the record does not establish what the campus police told Doe.

Three positions on what the prosecutor was sent are on the record, and the record does not settle between them. First, Van Houten: his office received the six-page signed statement and the Snapchat material and never received the interview transcript. When CBS put the “100 percent” line to him, around September 30, 2026, he called it “news to me”: “If she had said those words, we certainly would want to have explored that more… that wasn’t provided to me.” After the governor’s intervention he said, on October 2: “I support the appointment of Attorney General James and I trust that she will examine the facts and apply the law. It has become clear that we were not provided the full picture by the University. The focus has been and should continue to be on the victims. My office will cooperate fully with the Attorney General.” Second, Cornell and its police: the university initially said campus police shared “all investigatory information” with the district attorney’s office in 2024; in later formulations reported by CNN, Cornell said police shared the victim’s sworn statement and a Snapchat screenshot. Cornell has not specified publicly whether the transcript was transmitted. The conflict sharpens on the image itself: Cornell’s FAQ states the statement and a Snapchat screenshot were shared with the district attorney, while Van Houten told ABC News, as reported by the Cornell Daily Sun, that his office “was never provided with an image of a Snapchat group titled ‘Chi Phi Actives.’” The two statements may concern different images—a screenshot, and the group image as such—but no source establishes that, and the conflict is printed unresolved. Third, Governor Hochul, on October 2, 2026: the words “never made it into the account that the Cornell police sent to prosecutors”; the district attorney “then declined to pursue charges after reviewing that incomplete account and never interviewing her… In what world does the District Attorney not even interview her or anyone else involved, or even request the full transcript?” She also noted Doe “sat for hours and told them exactly what happened to her.” Whether the transcript was offered and declined, or never transmitted, is unknown on the public record. The Times documents show the district attorney’s office declined additional materials after deciding; they do not settle whether the transcript itself was among what was available before the decision.

5. The University’s Proceeding: Title IX, the Panel, and the Seven Outcomes

Cornell’s proceeding ran from a formal complaint in November 2024 to a panel decision in August 2025, and two of its dates stand unharmonized in the record. From the court record in the Kretzschmar litigation, Cornell issued its formal Title IX complaint on November 22, 2024, revised in January and March 2025; Doe’s civil complaint and CNN’s timeline use a January 14, 2025 investigation-opening date beside it. Both dates are documented in their respective records; no source harmonizes them, and this report prints both rather than choosing. Doe filed her complaint with Cornell’s Title IX coordinator in January 2025, the same month the 2024 federal Title IX rule was vacated nationwide. Cornell’s Office of Civil Rights states that the 2024 regulations “were never implemented at Cornell due to legal challenges” and that Cornell “continues to utilize and comply with” the 2020 regulations; on Cornell’s own account, the 2020 framework governed, and the standard of proof was Cornell’s Policy 6.4 choice within it: preponderance of the evidence. The investigation ran January to May 2025 and encompassed roughly 50 interviews and 32 witnesses on the litigation record; reported counts vary—one account counts 48 interviews across December to February, CNN’s reading of the redacted panel summary says more than 50, including all of the accused men except one who declined—and the range is printed because the exact count is not fixed in the public record. Six of the seven men were interviewed in the Title IX process; all who were interviewed denied wrongdoing. A three-member panel of trained faculty and staff heard the cases over hearings running May 2–29, 2025, conducted by Zoom; the panel’s report runs 99 pages in redacted form and the hearing transcript 1,732 pages. 10. The Standards: Three Forums, Three Burdens, and the Statute the Case Turns On

Three forums have handled this case or will, under three standards, and the divergence of their outcomes is a function of those standards and their different records. In the criminal forum, a grand jury decides probable cause to indict and a trial jury decides guilt beyond a reasonable doubt; no charges have been filed, and the special prosecutor’s investigation is open. In Cornell’s discipline, a three-member panel decided under Policy 6.4 by a preponderance of the evidence—more likely than not; that proceeding completed in 2025, its outcomes are at Section 5, and one respondent’s court challenge was dismissed with an appeal pending. In the civil forum, a jury, demanded, would decide by a preponderance at trial; at the current CPLR 3211 stage, the complaint’s allegations are taken as true for motion purposes, and the case is in early motion practice. Every serious account of the case makes this point, the Associated Press’s October 14, 2026 Title IX explainer among them. This report states the divergence; it does not resolve it.

The criminal question turns on a definition, and the definition is printed from the statute. Penal Law §130.00(6): “mentally incapacitated” means rendered temporarily incapable of appraising or controlling one’s conduct by a narcotic or intoxicating substance “administered to him without his consent,” or by another act committed without consent. Section 130.00(7): “physically helpless” means unconscious or otherwise physically unable to communicate unwillingness to act. Section 130.05(2)–(3): lack of consent results from, among other things, forcible compulsion, or incapacity to consent, including being mentally incapacitated or physically helpless; for specified offenses it also results from clearly expressed non-consent that a reasonable person in the actor’s position would understand. The consequence, as documented in legal commentary and the governor’s October 2 statement: voluntary intoxication, without more, does not satisfy the “mentally incapacitated” definition, because the substance must have been administered without consent. That is the analytical core of Van Houten’s 2024 decision and of the reform debate. A bill first introduced by then-Senator Biaggi in 2019 would add voluntary-intoxication incapacity with a knowledge element; it passed the New York Senate in 2020, 2021, 2023, 2024, and 2025 and never received an Assembly vote. The current vehicle is S10632 (Fernandez) and A101 (Dinowitz): it passed the Senate on June 4, 2026, no Assembly floor vote is recorded, and it is not law as of November 9, 2026; the governor’s October 2 pledge stands behind it. Two further provisions belong to the landscape. The “Rape Is Rape” Act, effective September 1, 2024—six weeks before the night at issue—brought vaginal, oral, and anal contact under the rape rubric, repealing and folding in the former criminal-sexual-act provisions, so any charging analysis of October 2024 conduct proceeds under the post-September 2024 numbering. And §130.10(1) provides an affirmative defense in prosecutions premised on incapacity: that the defendant did not know of the facts or conditions responsible for the victim’s incapacity. The defense is statutory and would be for the defence to prove; no defendant here has been charged, so it is an element of the legal landscape, not a live pleading.

The limitations record is printed with its open question left open. On the civil side, CPLR 213-c gives claims premised on specified sex offenses a twenty-year limitations window and expressly provides that no criminal charge or conviction is required; it is the provision Sarabia’s motion argues does not reach the narrower conduct alleged against him. CPLR 215(8)’s extension, by contrast, is contingent on a criminal action having been commenced, which has not occurred as of November 9, 2026; it is not established here. The Adult Survivors Act, CPLR 214-j, revived only claims already time-barred as of its 2022 effective date; it is not clearly applicable to October 2024 conduct, and Doe’s suit does not appear to rest on it. On the criminal side, under CPL 30.10, first-degree rape carries no limitations period, other felonies default to five years, and misdemeanors to two years—the two-year misdemeanor period for October 2024 conduct has run. Whether §30.10(2)(a-1)’s twenty-year period or the five-year default governs §130.30(4)–(6) conduct after the 2024 renumbering was not found on the sources the desk retrieved; this report does not assert an answer. The procedural instrument of the reopening, Executive Law §63(2), and its precedent use in Executive Order 147, are at Section 7.
