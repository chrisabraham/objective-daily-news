Aphantasia: The Naming, the Numbers, and the Effort to Measure an Absence

Ask a room of people to picture an apple, and most report that something appeared; a small number report that nothing appeared at all—no shape, no color, only the knowledge that they were thinking of an apple. The trait was documented by Francis Galton in 1880, set aside for a century, and named aphantasia by Adam Zeman and his colleagues in 2015. This report sets out the documented record in the order the evidence stands: what the trait is, how it was first described and then forgotten, how it was named, how the public found it—in a programmer’s essay and in the disclosures of Pixar’s Ed Catmull and Disney’s Glen Keane—how common the competing studies say it is and why their figures disagree, what the laboratories have measured with priming, pupils and high-field scanners, and what the record shows it touches in memory, faces and the other senses. By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 2330 ET, Monday, November 23, 2026.

Ask a room of people to picture an apple, and most of them will report that something appeared: a red shape, a shine on the skin, a particular apple from a particular kitchen. A small number will report that nothing appeared at all—no shape, no color, no image of any kind, only the knowledge that they were thinking about an apple. The second group is not guessing wrong. Its members can describe an apple accurately, draw one from knowledge, and recognize one on sight. What they cannot do is summon one voluntarily in the mind. The trait has a name, aphantasia, given to it in 2015 by the British neurologist Adam Zeman and his colleagues at the University of Exeter, from the Greek phantasia, imagination, with the prefix that means without.

The name is eleven years old. The record is older. Francis Galton documented the variation in 1880 and the science of the following century largely set it aside. Since 2015 the trait has acquired a measurement literature, a prevalence dispute conducted in competing percentages, a public record that includes the co-founder of Pixar and the animator who drew Ariel, and a set of laboratory instruments—pupil measurements, priming tasks, high-field brain scans—built to detect from outside an experience that occurs, or fails to occur, entirely inside. This report sets out the documented record in the order the evidence stands: what the trait is, how it was first described and then forgotten, how it was named, how the public found it, how common the competing studies say it is, what the laboratories have measured, and what the record shows it touches in memory and the other senses. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

What the trait is

Aphantasia is the reduced or absent ability to generate visual imagery voluntarily—the definition Zeman, Michaela Dewar and Sergio Della Sala proposed in the 2015 paper that named it: “the use of the term ‘aphantasia’ to refer to a condition of reduced or absent voluntary imagery.” The word voluntary carries the definition. The trait concerns imagery summoned on purpose: picturing a friend’s face, a rising sun, a familiar shop front, the standard scenes of the questionnaire described below. Perception is intact. Recognition is intact. Knowledge of what things look like is intact. What is absent is the experience of seeing the thing in its absence.

At the far end of the same scale stands the opposite trait, hyperphantasia, in which imagery is reported as vivid as perception itself, or near it. Between the two poles the research literature treats imagery vividness as a spectrum along which every person sits somewhere, most of them unaware that the spectrum exists. That unawareness is a documented feature of the subject. People at each position tend to assume that their own experience is the universal one, and the standard phrases of ordinary language—“picture this,” “see what I mean,” “counting sheep”—are used by speakers at every position as if they described the same event. Zeman’s group records that people usually discover their position in their teens or twenties, “often when sharing memories or trying to follow instructions to visualise.” The discovery is characteristically conversational: a remark meant as a metaphor turns out to have been a description.

The trait as defined is not a diagnosis. It appears in no diagnostic manual, it carries no treatment, and Zeman’s own summary of the first decade of research states the point plainly: “It was clear from the start that, while intriguing, aphantasia was not disabling.” The research questions are what the absence does to the faculties that imagery ordinarily serves—memory, dreaming, the simulation of future events, recognition of faces—and whether the absence in one sense is accompanied by absences in others. Those questions are taken up in their sections below.

The first description, and the century after

The variation enters the written record in 1880, when Francis Galton published his survey of mental imagery, asking respondents to bring to mind a scene and report what they saw. Galton recorded, to his stated surprise, that some of his respondents—men of science among them—reported no imagery at all: they knew the facts of the scene and could reason about it, but no picture presented itself. The finding stood in the literature as a curiosity. Psychology’s attention moved elsewhere, and for most of the following century the distribution of imagery vividness across the population was not a working subject.

The instrument that later made the subject measurable predates the modern research program by four decades. In 1973 the psychologist David Marks published the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire, the VVIQ: sixteen scenes—a relative or friend, a rising sun, the front of a familiar shop, a country scene with trees and a lake—each rated on a five-point scale running from “no image at all, you only know that you are thinking of the object” to “perfectly clear and as vivid as normal vision.” The scores sum to a range from 16, the floor, at which no item produced any image, to 80, the ceiling. The questionnaire remains the field’s standard instrument, and its conventions govern nearly every number in this report: a score of 16 marks the extreme of no imagery; scores in the high seventies mark hyperphantasia; and the line between aphantasia and merely dim imagery is drawn by each research group at a cutoff of its own choosing, a fact that matters to the prevalence record below.

One survey stands between Galton and the modern program. In 2009, Bill Faw of Brewton-Parker College in Georgia reported that about 2 percent of the 2,500 people he queried reported having no visual imagination. The figure was published and, like Galton’s, was not taken up. The subject waited for a patient.

The naming

The modern record opens in a clinic. In 2010 Zeman published the case of a 65-year-old man, identified in the literature as MX, who had lost his visual imagery following a cardiac procedure. MX could perform tasks that appeared to require imagery—he knew what things looked like and could reason about their appearance—but the experience of picturing was gone, and he knew it was gone, because he had possessed it for sixty-five years. Zeman’s group described the case under the term “blind imagination.”

A popular account of the MX case then produced the research program. More than twenty people contacted Zeman after reading of MX, each recognizing the description with one difference: their inability to visualize had been lifelong. They had never possessed the faculty MX had lost, and most had learned of its existence in others only from the article. Zeman and his colleagues studied twenty-one of these respondents with questionnaires including the VVIQ, and in 2015 published the findings in the journal Cortex, proposing the name aphantasia for the lifelong condition. The acquired form MX represented and the congenital form the twenty-one represented have been studied together since, as the two doors into the same room.

The decade after the naming produced a literature where none had stood. Zeman, reviewing the field in 2024, counted well over fifty scientific papers on the subject in the first ten years, and a special issue of the journal Neuropsychologia devoted to aphantasia has since appeared. The research groups are international—Exeter, Edinburgh, Sydney, Paris, Toronto among them—and the questions they pursue are the ones this report takes in order: prevalence, measurement, mechanism, and consequence.

The public discovery

The trait’s public history runs through two documents and one interview, all matters of record.

In April 2016, Blake Ross, a co-creator of the Firefox web browser, published an essay describing his own discovery that other people’s talk of picturing things was literal. Ross reported that he had never pictured anything in his life, had assumed the vocabulary of visualization was metaphorical, and had learned otherwise in adulthood, in conversation. His account of what occupies his mind instead is the most quoted passage in the public record of the trait: “All narration, all the time. An infinite script of milk voice dialogue. When you read a sarcastic essay from me, it is a transcript of this voice.” The essay circulated widely, and Zeman’s own retrospectives name Ross among the early contacts who carried the subject to a general audience; thousands of readers recognized their own experience in it and contacted researchers, as the original twenty-one had contacted Zeman.

In April 2019, the BBC reported the disclosures of two men whose professions are the manufacture of images. Ed Catmull, co-founder of Pixar and then president of Pixar and Walt Disney Animation Studios, told the BBC that his mind’s eye is blind: he had discovered it attempting a visualization exercise in meditation, asked to picture a sphere, and finding nothing. Glen Keane, the Disney animator who drew Ariel in The Little Mermaid and the Beast in Beauty and the Beast, reported the same absence. Catmull had surveyed 540 of his Pixar colleagues on imagery vividness, and the survey’s result is part of the record: the production managers tended to report stronger visualization than the artists. Catmull’s stated conclusion, as the BBC reported it, addresses the assumption the survey contradicts: “People had conflated visualisation with creativity and imagination and one of the messages is, ‘they’re not the same thing’.”

The two disclosures fixed the public shape of the subject. The trait’s best-known carriers include a man who built the studio that defined computer animation, an animator responsible for two of the most recognized characters in film, and a programmer who co-created a browser used by hundreds of millions. The record contains no evidence that the trait barred any of them from the work. What the record contains instead is the recurring mechanism of discovery—an exercise, a conversation, an essay—by which people who had assumed their inner experience was standard learned that it was not.

How common: the numbers in dispute

The published prevalence figures do not agree, and the disagreement is itself documented and explained within the literature: the figures measure different things, because each research group draws the aphantasia line at a different point on the same questionnaire.

At the strictest definition—the floor score of 16 on the VVIQ, no image on any of the sixteen items—the figures cluster near one percent. Dance, Ipser and Simner, in a 2022 prevalence study across two samples of roughly one thousand people each, found 0.8 percent at the floor. Wright and colleagues, in a 2024 study of 9,063 participants, reported 0.9 to 1.2 percent with complete absence of visual imagery.

At the broader definition—scores up to 32 on the VVIQ, capturing dim and vague imagery as well as none—the figures rise toward four percent. The same Dance study reported 3.9 percent at its cutoff of below 33. Wright’s group separately reported about 3.3 percent with significantly reduced imagery, which they term hypophantasia, bringing the combined weak-or-absent figure to roughly 4 percent. Zeman’s own working estimate, from the first several hundred surveys returned to his group, stood at about 2 percent, in line with Faw’s 2009 figure.

The literature states the methodological position without embarrassment: the cutoffs are conventions, not natural boundaries. Zeman’s 2020 survey drew the line at scores of 16 to 23; Dance’s at 16 to 32. The same set of answers therefore counts as aphantasia in one study and as low imagery in another, and every prevalence figure in print is a figure for a definition. The desk prints the figures as their studies state them, with their definitions attached: near one percent for the complete absence, near four percent for absence and marked dimness together, and the boundary between the two unsettled in the literature itself.

At the spectrum’s other end, hyperphantasia—imagery rated as vivid as real seeing, VVIQ scores of 75 to 80—is reported at roughly 2.6 percent in the same literature, a figure subject to the same convention at the opposite cutoff.

The measurement problem

Every figure above rests on self-report, and the field’s central methodological problem is stated in its own papers: the VVIQ measures what people say their imagery is like, and the discovery of aphantasia is precisely that people had been using the same words—vivid, clear, picture—to describe different experiences. A science of a private event required instruments that did not ask the subject. Three such instruments stand in the record.

The first is priming. In the binocular-rivalry paradigm developed in Joel Pearson’s Sydney laboratory, a person who imagines a pattern beforehand is measurably more likely to see that pattern dominate when the two eyes are then shown competing images. The effect is an established behavioral signature of imagery at work. Keogh and Pearson reported in 2018 that people with aphantasia show almost no such priming. The result addresses the standing alternative explanation of the trait—that aphantasic people possess imagery but lack awareness of it—and weighs against it: on the priming measure, the imagery itself, and not only the report of it, is absent or near-absent.

The second instrument is the pupil. Kay, Keogh, Andrillon and Pearson reported in eLife in 2022 that the pupil responds to imagined light as it responds to real light: in people with typical imagery, pupils constricted when the person imagined a bright shape and dilated when the person imagined a dark one, the response growing with self-reported vividness. In eighteen participants with aphantasia, the pupils responded normally to shapes actually shown and showed no significant difference between imagined bright and imagined dark. The study included a control for effort: asked to imagine four objects instead of one, aphantasic participants’ pupils did respond to the increased load, establishing that the absent light response was not an absence of trying. Pearson, the senior author, stated the claim for the method: “This really is the first biological, objective test for imagery vividness,” and the paper presents the result as the first physiological evidence of aphantasia.

The third instrument is the scanner. Liu and colleagues, publishing in Cortex in 2025, used ultra-high-field 7-Tesla fMRI on ten people with congenital aphantasia and ten typical imagers performing imagery and perception tasks across five domains—object shape, object color, written words, faces, spatial relationships. In typical imagers, imagery activated left-hemisphere frontoparietal areas, the relevant domain areas of the ventral temporal cortex, and a domain-general area in the left fusiform gyrus the authors name the Fusiform Imagery Node. In the aphantasic participants, imagery activated similar visual areas; what differed was the coupling—functional connectivity between the Fusiform Imagery Node and the frontoparietal areas was reduced. The study’s stated interpretation is that conscious visual experience, perceived or imagined, depends on the integrated activity of the high-level visual cortex and the frontoparietal networks, and that in aphantasia the parts are present and active while the integration is diminished. The record therefore holds two findings that stand in apparent tension and are printed here together: on the priming and pupil measures, the imagery signal is absent; on the scanner, imagery-related visual areas activate while their connection to the frontal systems is reduced. The literature has not yet joined these into a single account.

What the record shows it touches

The research on consequences clusters in four places: memory, faces, the future, and the other senses.

On memory, Dawes and colleagues (2020, 2022) and Milton and colleagues (2021) report that people with aphantasia describe their autobiographical memories as less vivid and less rich in sensory detail than controls describe theirs, while their factual knowledge of their own pasts—the events, their order, their participants—is retained. The same studies report greater difficulty imagining future events, the simulation of what has not yet happened drawing on the same faculty as the recollection of what has. A related condition stands in association in the literature: severely deficient autobiographical memory, SDAM, described by Brian Levine’s group in Toronto, in which people cannot re-experience their pasts as episodes at all. Aphantasia and SDAM are frequently reported together, and the relationship between them—whether one produces the other, or both arise from a common difference—is recorded as unresolved.

On faces, the same research groups report elevated rates of face-recognition difficulty among people with aphantasia, including developmental prosopagnosia, the lifelong inability to recognize faces that is itself a documented trait. The association is reported as a rate, not a rule: many aphantasic people recognize faces without difficulty.

On the other senses, Dawes and colleagues reported in 2020 that people with aphantasia report reduced imagery in domains beyond the visual—sound, smell, taste, touch—at higher rates than controls, though not universally: for some, the absence is visual only. The literature has begun naming the parallel absences separately; the absence of inner hearing, of an imagined voice or melody, is termed anauralia. Whether aphantasia is one trait with variable reach or a family of modality-specific traits is an open question in the record.

On performance, the record runs against deficit. Cavazos and colleagues, in Frontiers in Psychology in October 2025, studied college students with aphantasia and found no significant differences in learning approaches and academic performance on par with peers; the differences the study documents are of method—extensive externalization through lists and organizational systems, systematic verbal strategies, anchoring new material to familiar references. In a 2026 study in the Neuropsychologia special issue, Suggate and colleagues found aphantasic participants generally slower but more accurate than controls on mental comparison tasks across visual, auditory and tactile items, with an advantage on the tactile items once speed and accuracy were combined. The trait’s documented costs, in the current record, are costs of vividness—in memory’s sensory richness, in the felt presence of the past and the imagined future—rather than costs of capability.

The state of the question

The record as it stands supports a short list of agreements. The trait is real and was documented as early as 1880. It was named in 2015, and the name is standard. It is defined on voluntary imagery, with perception and knowledge intact. Its complete form is reported by near one percent of studied populations, and its broader form, including markedly dim imagery, by near four percent, with the boundary between the forms a matter of convention. Objective measures—priming, pupil response, connectivity—corroborate the self-reports in the groups studied. Its best-known carriers discovered it by accident, in conversation or in exercises, in adolescence or adulthood.

The record leaves a matching list open. The prevalence dispute is a definition dispute and continues. The mechanism is unsettled between an absence of the imagery signal and a disconnection of the systems that generate it. The trait’s reach across the senses varies by person and is not yet mapped. Its relationship to autobiographical memory deficits is documented as association, not as cause. And the oldest fact in the file remains unexplained by any party: that a variation in inner experience, present in every population studied and described accurately in 1880, could pass unexamined for a hundred and thirty-five years—hidden, the record suggests, inside the ordinary assumption that everyone’s mind works the way one’s own does. The desk prints the record. The question stands where the record leaves it.
