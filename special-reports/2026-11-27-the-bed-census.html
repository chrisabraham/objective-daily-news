The Bed Census, Filed From the Record

Objective

Mental Illness in America: The Hospital Census, the Prison Census, and a Century of Suicide Rates

Filed from the record: the state hospital bed census from its 1955 peak of 558,922 to 37,679 beds in 2016; the instruments of the asylum era; the prison census and the Penrose hypothesis; a century of suicide rates; and the point where the record splits, between the severe disorders that measure flat and the young who measure worse. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0600 ET, Friday, November 27, 2026.

The United States keeps four long counts that bear on a question its public argument asks every year: whether mental illness is more common now than it was. The counts are the census of state psychiatric hospital beds, kept since the nineteenth century; the census of prisoners, kept in federal series since 1925; the vital statistics of suicide, kept since 1900; and the survey record of the present century. This report files the four counts in the order they run, with the instruments the asylum era used and the point where the counts disagree. The record is printed whole.

The Bed Census

In 1955 the state and county psychiatric hospitals of the United States held 558,922 patients, according to the Treatment Advocacy Center’s survey of the bed record, Going, Going, Gone. That is 337 beds for every 100,000 Americans, one hospital bed for every 300 people in the country. The census had climbed for a century, through the building of the great state asylums, and 1955 is its peak.

The fall from the peak is the largest single movement in the record. The American Psychiatric Association’s presidential task force on the bed crisis counts about 370,000 state hospital residents in 1970 and about 40,000 in 2014. The Treatment Advocacy Center’s census puts the 2016 figure at 37,679 beds, 11.7 for every 100,000 Americans—3.5 percent of the 1955 rate, a decline of 96.5 percent per capita. The National Mental Health Services Survey counted about 35,000 state hospital beds in 2019, about 11 per 100,000. A large share of what remains is forensic: beds held for defendants found incompetent to stand trial and for persons acquitted by reason of insanity.

The drivers are on the record. Chlorpromazine, marketed as Thorazine, was approved in 1954—the first drug that quieted psychosis enough for some patients to live outside locked wards. President Kennedy signed the Community Mental Health Act on October 31, 1963, the last bill he signed, stating the aim of cutting the institutional population in half within a decade or two; the act planned 1,500 community mental health centers, about half were built, and those were underfunded. Medicaid, created in 1965, barred federal payment for adults in state psychiatric hospitals—the Institutions for Mental Diseases exclusion—which gave the states a standing financial reason to move patients out of their own hospitals and into settings the federal government would help pay for. The federal courts narrowed the states’ power to commit in the same decades. The beds went; the centers that were to receive the patients largely did not arrive.

The Instruments of the Asylum Era

The 1955 census was not a census of volunteers. Commitment required no crime and, in most states, no courtroom contest: a family’s petition and physicians’ certificates were enough to hold a person indefinitely. The population inside was broader than the population of a psychiatric hospital today—the psychiatric association’s task force notes that the old hospitals also held people with medical conditions, older adults with neurocognitive disorders, and persons with disabilities, all under the same roofs and the same legal authority.

The era’s other instruments are matters of statute and case law. Indiana passed the first eugenic sterilization law in 1907, and more than thirty states followed. In Buck v. Bell, 274 U.S. 200 (1927), the Supreme Court upheld Virginia’s law, Justice Holmes writing for the Court: “Three generations of imbeciles are enough.” About 60,000 Americans had been sterilized under the state laws by 1960, about a third of them in California. The decision has never been formally overruled.

The somatic therapies of the period are also record. Lobotomy, introduced from Portugal in 1935, was performed in the United States from 1936; Walter Freeman’s transorbital procedure followed in 1946, and the operation peaked about 1949 at roughly 5,000 procedures a year. Estimates of the American total run from 40,000 to 50,000 operations. Egas Moniz, the operation’s originator, received the Nobel Prize in 1949. Insulin coma therapy and convulsive therapy belonged to the same era’s practice. The antipsychotic drugs of the 1950s ended the surgical era as they emptied the wards.

The Prison Census and the Penrose Hypothesis

In 1939 the British psychiatrist Lionel Penrose published a comparative study of eighteen European countries and reported an inverse relationship between the population of mental hospitals and the population of prisons: where one was large, the other was small. The relationship held most strongly against the number of deaths by murder. Penrose’s paper, “Mental disease and crime,” appeared in the British Journal of Medical Psychology.

The American baseline makes the pattern legible. The federal imprisonment series runs at about 110 prisoners per 100,000 residents, essentially flat, from 1925 into the early 1970s—criminologists Blumstein and Cohen measured an average of 110.2 for 1930 to 1970 and called the stability itself the finding. In the asylum era the prison was not the instrument of removal; the hospital was. The imprisonment rate began its climb in 1972 and 1973 and reached about five times its historic level by 2007, in the same decades the hospital census completed its fall.

The modern census is filed by E. Fuller Torrey and colleagues in More Mentally Ill Persons Are in Jails and Prisons Than Hospitals (2010). Using 2004 and 2005 data, the report finds more than three times as many seriously mentally ill persons in the nation’s jails and prisons as in its hospitals, and estimates that about 16 percent of inmates are seriously mentally ill, against 6.4 percent in 1983. In 1955, the report notes, there was one psychiatric bed for every 300 Americans; in 2005 there was one for every 3,000. Cook County Jail, Rikers Island, and the Los Angeles County Jail are described in the literature of the field as, in effect, the three largest mental health facilities in the United States; about one in three prisoners in Cook County was estimated mentally ill in 2015, on what its own sheriff’s office called a conservative estimate.

The hypothesis has been tested since, and the tests are filed with it. A longitudinal study of Norway from 1930 to 2004 found the inverse pattern across the century and called it remarkably robust. An English study covering 1960 to 2018–19 found psychiatric beds down 93 percent and the prison population up 208 percent, correlated at −0.96, with about 36 additional prisoners a decade later for every 100 beds closed. The counter-bearing is printed with them: after eighty years the literature describes the hypothesis as neither rejected nor confirmed, and a 2004 re-examination across 158 countries found patient and prisoner numbers positively related in low- and middle-income countries and unrelated in high-income ones. The desk files both bearings.

A Century of Suicide

The vital statistics run from 1900. The highest rate of the century is 17.4 deaths per 100,000 in 1932, in the Depression; age-standardized, the 1932 peak stands higher, at 21.9. The lowest is 9.8 in 1957. The rate stood at 11.4 in 1950, 10.6 in 1960, and 10.8 in 1964, and it had not exceeded 12.0 since 1941 when the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention published its mid-century review. The series moves in long swings across the century, rising with economic collapse and falling in the prosperous postwar decades; it does not move in a straight line in either direction.

The recent figures stand at the top of the modern range: 14.3 per 100,000 in 2022, the highest since 1941, and 14.7 in 2023. The composition of the total has changed across the century even where the total has not. Rates among older men—the highest of any group in the early century—fell across it; rates among the young climbed from the 1950s onward. The century’s suicide record is therefore two records moving in opposite directions inside one number.

One caveat is part of the record and is printed as a caveat, not a number. The historical figures are floors. Suicide was understated in the cause-of-death statistics of its own time—the federal review of 1964 concedes the rates were suspected of being understated even then—and families and coroners concealed deaths under the religious and social stigma the act carried. How large the understatement was cannot be recovered from the record, and the desk does not convert the caveat into an estimate.

Where the Record Splits

The four counts do not tell one story, and the split is located precisely: it runs between the severe disorders and the young.

Where measurement is comparable across decades, the severe end measures flat. The Global Burden of Disease study, analyzed by Solmi and colleagues in Molecular Psychiatry in 2023, finds the age-standardized prevalence, incidence, and burden of schizophrenia essentially stable worldwide from 1990 to 2019; the raw count of cases rose 65 percent because the population rose. The disorder that filled the 1955 census shows no comparable rise where the instrument is held constant.

The adolescent series is not flat. The Youth Risk Behavior Survey, which asks American high school students the same questions in the same way, records the share reporting persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness rising from 28 percent in 2011 to 42 percent in 2021 and 40 percent in 2023; the share who seriously considered suicide rose from 16 percent to 22 percent between 2011 and 2021. Suicide rates among Americans aged 10 to 24 rose 62 percent between 2007 and 2021. These measurements rise in ways that broader diagnosis and readier disclosure do not by themselves account for; the literature argues the causes and has not settled them.

The Record as It Stands

In 1955 the United States kept 558,922 people in its state psychiatric hospitals and about 110 per 100,000 of its people in prison. It had sterilized some 60,000 of its citizens under law its Supreme Court had upheld, and it operated on the brains of 40,000 to 50,000 more. Its suicide rate, across the century, moved between 9.8 and 17.4 per 100,000. The beds are gone: 11.7 per 100,000 remain. The prisons filled as the hospitals emptied, in the pattern Penrose described in 1939 and the literature has neither confirmed nor dismissed since. The suicide rate stands at its highest since 1941. The severe disorders measure flat where the measurement is comparable; the young measure worse, and that part of the record is not explained by the rest. The desk draws no conclusion from it. The record is printed whole.
