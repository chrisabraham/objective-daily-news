The Bundle of Sticks, Filed From the Record

Objective

The Fasces: The Bundle of Sticks in Rome, in the Fable, in American Symbols, in the Five Arrows, and in the English Word

A bundle of rods bound around an axe stands behind the Speaker’s rostrum in the House of Representatives, under Abraham Lincoln’s hands in his memorial, and on the back of every American dime struck between 1916 and 1945. The same bundle gave its name to fascism. This report sets out the record of the object and the word, in the order the record runs: what the bundle was in Rome, the fable that later attached to it, the republics that carved it, the five arrows of the Haudenosaunee, the Italian movement that took its name, the definitions the scholars and the movement’s own Doctrine left on file, and the English word that still means a bundle of sticks in Britain. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1200 ET, Tuesday, November 24, 2026.

The Object

A bundle of wooden rods bound around an axe stands behind the Speaker’s rostrum in the United States House of Representatives. Fasces stand on the armrests of Abraham Lincoln’s chair in his memorial in Washington, on the reverse of every American dime struck between 1916 and 1945, on the seal of the United States Senate, and on the coat of arms of Ecuador. The same bundle gave its name, in Italian, to fascism. This report sets out the record of the object and the word, in the order the record runs: what the bundle was in Rome, the fable that later attached to it, the republics that carved it, the five arrows of the Haudenosaunee, the Italian movement that took its name, the definitions the movement’s own Doctrine and the scholars of fascism left on file, the unions that carried the fable’s image, and the English word that still means a bundle of sticks in Britain. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

Rome: The Lictor’s Bundle

The Latin word is fasces, a plural form from fascis, a bundle. The Oxford Classical Dictionary defines the object as wooden rods bound with red leather thongs in the form of a cylindrical bundle around a single-headed axe, the blade emerging from the bundle. The rods were of birch or elm and about a metre and a half long. They were carried on the left shoulder by attendants called lictors, who walked before a Roman magistrate in public.

The earliest remains are Etruscan. A miniature fasces in iron was recovered from a tomb of the late seventh century BC at Vetulonia, in Etruria, excavated by Isidoro Falchi in 1897 and dated to about 630 to 625 BC. The ancient literary tradition assigns the object the same origin: Silius Italicus and Dionysius of Halicarnassus both give the fasces to the Etruscans, from whom Rome took them.

The number of lictors, and so of bundles, followed the magistrate’s rank: twenty-four before a dictator, twelve before a consul, six before a praetor. The bundle was the visible form of imperium, the magistrate’s power to command and to punish. The Dictionary records that the fasces grouped together all the equipment needed to inflict corporal or capital punishment: the rods for beating, the axe for beheading. The National Park Service, in its account of the fasces carved at the Lincoln Memorial, prints the same reading: the rods suggest punishment by beating, the axe suggests beheading.

Inside Rome itself the axe came out. Within the pomerium, the city’s sacred boundary, the fasces of a magistrate carried no axe, dictators and triumphing generals excepted. The Dictionary records what the absence meant: it symbolized the citizen’s right of appeal, the provocatio, against a capital sentence. The bundle that announced the power to kill announced, inside the city, a limit on that power.

The Fable

The bundle’s second career begins in a fable. Aesop’s story of the old man and his sons is number 53 in the Perry Index of the fables, and it was included by Babrius in his collection. In the Library of Congress’s telling, a father whose sons are always quarreling asks them to bring in a bundle of sticks. He places the bundle into the hands of each of them in turn and bids them break it; they try with all their strength and are not able to do it. He next unties the bundle and gives his sons the sticks to break one by one; this they do with the greatest ease. The moral the Library of Congress prints under the fable is four words: “In unity is strength.” The moral Babrius himself drew from it was brotherly love.

The fable’s bundle is sticks. It is not Roman, and in its earliest forms it has nothing to do with magistrates, axes, or punishment. Its lesson is about a family.

The Fable’s Travels

The same lesson was taught with arrows and spears across Eurasia, and the teachers are on record. Plutarch’s Sayings of Kings and Commanders preserves the Scythian king Scilurus, who lived in the second century BC. On his deathbed, the account runs, Scilurus offered a bundle of darts to each of his eighty sons in turn and bade them break it; when no one could, he drew the darts out one by one and broke them easily himself, and told them that if they held together they would continue strong, but if they fell out and were divided, they would become weak.

The Secret History of the Mongols, the Mongolian chronicle of the house of Genghis Khan, tells the same lesson of Alan Gua, an ancestress of the Khan standing eleven generations before him in the chronicle’s genealogy. She seated her five sons, gave each a single arrow-shaft, and told them to break it; each did, easily. She then bound five arrow-shafts together and passed the bundle to each son in turn; none could break it. Her words, in the standard translation, are recorded in the chronicle: “All five of you were born of this same belly. Alone, you can be broken easily by anyone. Together and of one mind, like bound arrow-shafts, none can easily vanquish you.”

Scholars of Inner Asia file the two tellings as one long-lived pattern. A study in the Eurasian Studies Journal in 2014, on the longevity of the fable of bundled arrows in Inner Asian cultures, traces the story from Scilurus to Alan Gua and beyond: it was told also of the Bulgar khan Kubrat and of Svatopluk, and it travelled to Japan, where the old man’s role is taken by the daimyo Mōri Motonari. Spain carved a version of it into a royal badge at the end of the fifteenth century: the yoke and arrows of Ferdinand and Isabella, a bundle of five arrows bound together, which alluded to the proverb that arrows are broken easily one by one and not at all when tied. The badge was taken up in the twentieth century by the Falange and stood as an emblem of the Franco regime; it was removed in Spain’s transition to democracy and dropped from the royal arms in 2014.

The Merger

For most of its Roman and medieval life, the fasces meant authority and punishment, and the fable meant a family holding together. The two were joined late. A history of the symbol published by Oxford University Press records that in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, European authors and artists conflated the Roman fasces with Aesop’s fable of the bundle of sticks, although the fable had nothing to do with Rome. That learned conflation, the history records, produced the emphatically non-Roman idea that the fasces represented unity and good government, and it was this merged meaning, unity read into a badge of punishment, that afterward informed the political imagery of the young United States and of revolutionary France.

The Republics’ Bundle

The American instances are documented one by one. In the chamber of the House of Representatives, in use since December 16, 1857, the American flag behind the Speaker’s rostrum is flanked by two bronze fasces; the Architect of the Capitol’s account of the chamber lists them among its fixtures, and the House’s own historians record that the present bronze bundles replaced gilded-iron ones in the remodeling of 1950. The House’s other instrument of authority is built to the same pattern: the Mace of the House, in its present form since 1841, is thirteen ebony rods bound together in silver and topped by an eagle.

At the Lincoln Memorial, dedicated in 1922, the National Park Service has published a full account of the symbol’s presence. Fasces are carved on the fronts of the armrests of Daniel Chester French’s seated Lincoln, without axes inside the chamber. Outside, at the base of the steps, a carving more than ten feet tall shows thirteen rods bound together with an axe and an eagle’s head on top. The Service reads the exterior bundle as the states bound by the Constitution, under the memorial’s own motto, E Pluribus Unum, out of many, one. The same Park Service account lists fasces in the Oval Office, at the base of the Statue of Freedom on the Capitol dome, on the seal of the United States Senate, which carries a pair of crossed fasces, and in Houdon’s statue of Washington inside the Washington Monument. A frieze on the facade of the Supreme Court building depicts a Roman centurion holding a fasces, representing Order.

The dime carried the bundle furthest. The Mercury dime, struck from late 1916 to 1945 and designed by Adolph A. Weinman, bears a fasces with an olive branch on its reverse. Weinman’s own explanation of his design is on the numismatic record: he chose the fasces, he wrote, “to symbolize the strength which lies in unity, while the battle-ax stands for preparedness to defend the Union. The branch of olive is symbolical of our love of peace.” The design was struck in 1916, three years before any Italian movement adopted the bundle. The record also preserves a letter of 1936, written to the House Coinage Committee, whose author worried that future historians might conclude from the coin that the United States, and not Italy, had been the birthplace of fascism.

France’s republican bundle is older than its fascist namesake and outlasted it. The Great Seal of France, fixed by an ordinance of September 8, 1848 and still in use, shows Liberty seated, holding a lictor’s fasces. The Republic’s diplomatic emblem, adopted by the Foreign Ministry in 1913 and carried on French passport covers, sets a fasces at its center; the emblem’s own documentation associates the bundle with the exercise of justice and notes that this use predates its adoption by Benito Mussolini. Ecuador’s coat of arms, in its current form since October 31, 1900, rests its shield, in the words of the official description, on “a bundle of consular beams, a symbol of the republican dignity”; the Ecuadorian embassy’s account calls the consular fasces the insignia of the consuls of Rome, a symbol of authority and dignity.

The Five Arrows

North America has its own bundle teaching, older than the republic that later carved bundles of its own, and it is carried in the Haudenosaunee’s own record. The Onondaga Nation, keepers of the Confederacy’s council fire, state the founding in their published history. Before the union, the five nations, Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, warred with one another. The Peacemaker, Dekanawida, with the spokesman Hiawatha, carried the Creator’s message of peace from nation to nation. His demonstration is the bundle: he took one arrow and broke it easily, in the Nation’s words, showing that “one nation standing alone can be easily broken, like a single arrow.” He then bound five arrows together, and they could not be broken. The Nation’s history states the conclusion he drew: five united nations, “bound together with one heart, one mind, and one law,” would be very powerful. The union he founded is governed by the Great Law of Peace, preserved orally for centuries, whose emblem is the great white pine; its needles grow in clusters of five, the Nation records, to remind the Haudenosaunee of the five nations coming together. Scholars date the Confederacy’s founding variously between the twelfth and the sixteenth centuries.

No document in the record carries this teaching from Rome. The Haudenosaunee bundle is arrows, not rods; it belongs to a constitution preserved by the nations themselves; and the documented transmissions in the record run in the other direction, from the Haudenosaunee toward the English colonies. At the Treaty of Lancaster in 1744, the Onondaga speaker Canassatego advised the assembled colonies to unite as the Five Nations had. Benjamin Franklin printed the speech the same year. Its words stand in the printed treaty record: “Our wise Forefathers established Union and Amity between the Five Nations; this has made us formidable; this has given us great Weight and Authority with our neighboring Nations. We are a powerful Confederacy; and, by your observing the same Methods our wise Forefathers have taken, you will acquire fresh Strength and Power.” The Encyclopedia of Virginia’s account of the occasion records that Canassatego made the point with the arrows themselves, demonstrating how one arrow was easily broken and a bundle of arrows was not. Franklin returned to the example in a letter of 1751, and his Albany Plan of 1754 proposed a union of the colonies. In 1775, commissioners of the Continental Congress quoted Canassatego’s advice back to the Six Nations at Albany, telling them their forefathers’ words had sunk deep, and that the colonies had tied twelve arrows together into a strong confederacy.

The United States put a bundle of arrows into its own arms seven years later. The Great Seal, adopted on June 20, 1782, shows the American eagle holding an olive branch in one talon and, in the words of the adopting blazon, “a bundle of thirteen arrows” in the other, thirteen for the thirteen states. Charles Thomson, who presented the design, explained the pair in his official remarks: “The Olive branch and arrows denote the power of peace & war which is exclusively vested in Congress.”

How much of the American union descends from the Haudenosaunee example is a question the scholarly record argues in the open. The strong form of the claim, that the Great Law of Peace influenced the framing of the United States Constitution, was advanced from the 1970s by the historians Donald A. Grinde Jr. and Bruce Johansen. Critics, among them the anthropologist Elisabeth Tooker, answer that direct borrowings from the Great Law into the constitutional text are difficult to document. A weaker form of the claim, that the Iroquois example contributed to colonial thinking about federation alongside the Magna Carta, English common law, the Dutch Republic, and Enlightenment theory, is accepted by a wider range of historians. The Congress of the United States put its own position on the record in 1988. House Concurrent Resolution 331, agreed to by the House on October 4, 1988, by a vote of 408 to 8, and by the Senate on October 21, states in its preamble that George Washington and Benjamin Franklin “are known to have greatly admired the concepts of the Six Nations of the Iroquois Confederacy,” and that “the confederation of the original Thirteen Colonies into one republic was influenced by the political system developed by the Iroquois Confederacy.” Its resolving clause acknowledges “the contribution made by the Iroquois Confederacy and other Indian Nations to the formation and development of the United States.” In 2010 the United States Mint struck the teaching itself into a coin: the reverse of that year’s Native American one-dollar piece shows the Hiawatha Belt with five arrows bound together, inscribed, in the Mint’s official description, “Haudenosaunee” and “Great Law of Peace.”

The Unions’ Bundle

The fable’s image, detached from Rome entirely, did its own work in the labor movement. A man kneeling over a bundle of sticks, under the motto “Unity is Strength,” appeared on the badge of the Nottinghamshire Miners’ Association, on a Durham trade union banner, and on a trade token of the Worcestershire Co-operative Society. The emblem of the Amalgamated Society of Engineers, designed in 1851 by James Sharples, a founder member of the society, sets two kneeling men to the same illustration of Aesop’s fable; the keyed description of the emblem reads the pair as showing that “Union is Strength,” and the scroll beneath them reads “Be united and industrious.” The Australian National University’s guide to the symbolism of union banners keys the device in one line: “bundles of sticks—strength in unity.” One account of the banners, written from the People’s History Museum’s collection in Manchester, records that after the First World War British unions let the twig bundle go, as the Roman bundle’s new association took hold in Italy.

The Name Taken

The Italian word fascio means a bundle, a sheaf, and by extension a league or group, and it was ordinary political vocabulary long before it was a party name. Sicilian revolutionary democrats were using it in the political sense in the 1870s. The Fasci Siciliani of 1891 to 1894 were leagues of peasants and workers; the historian Robert O. Paxton records that the Sicilian peasants rose in 1893 and 1894. Before 1914, Paxton also records, the symbolism of the fasces was usually appropriated by the Left, and in nineteenth-century France the figure of Marianne was often portrayed carrying fasces, to represent the force of republican solidarity.

Mussolini organized his first fascio in November 1914, the Autonomous Fasci of Revolutionary Action, an interventionist group agitating for Italy’s entry into the war. The Roman emblem came after the war. On March 23, 1919, in the Piazza San Sepolcro in Milan, the movement was relaunched as the Fasci di Combattimento, and it was this fascio, the Oxford history of the symbol records, that took the old Roman fasces as its emblem; in the campaign of October 1919 the movement’s line was that “the Fascist emblem signifies unity, force and justice.” The movement became the National Fascist Party in 1921, and the regime it built, from 1922 to 1943, carried the fascio littorio, the lictor’s bundle, on its buildings, its insignia, and its coinage. The name of the ideology is the name of the bundle: fascismo, from fascio.

The Doctrine and the Scholars

The movement defined itself in print. The Doctrine of Fascism, published in the Enciclopedia Italiana in 1932 over Mussolini’s name, its first part written by the philosopher Giovanni Gentile, states the conception in its own words: “The Fascist conception of the State is all-embracing; outside of it no human or spiritual values can exist, much less have value. Thus understood, Fascism is totalitarian.” The same text denies the premise of the republics whose emblem it shared: “Fascism denies that the majority, by the simple fact that it is a majority, can direct human society; it denies that numbers alone can govern by means of a periodical consultation.” On the unions whose banners carried the fable, the Doctrine is equally plain: it declares fascism opposed to trade unionism as a class weapon, and assigns the unions their place inside the corporative State.

The scholars who have made the definition of fascism their subject define it without reference to the emblem. Paxton’s Anatomy of Fascism, published in 2004, defines it as behavior: “a form of political behavior marked by obsessive preoccupation with community decline, humiliation or victimhood and by compensatory cults of unity, energy and purity,” pursued, in his fuller account, by a mass party of nationalist militants working with traditional elites, abandoning democratic liberties. Umberto Eco, in his essay “Ur-Fascism” in The New York Review of Books of June 22, 1995, listed fourteen features of the phenomenon and warned against treating them as a system: they “cannot be organized into a system; many of them contradict each other, and are also typical of other kinds of despotism or fanaticism. But it is enough that one of them be present to allow fascism to coagulate around it.” Roger Griffin, in The Nature of Fascism, published in 1991, reduced the definition to what he calls the fascist minimum: “Fascism is a political ideology whose mythic core in its various permutations is a palingenetic form of populist ultra-nationalism,” the myth of a nation reborn from decadence. None of the three definitions turns on a symbol. Each defines the ideology by what it does, what it exhibits, or what it believes, and the bundle appears in none of them.

The English Word

Chris’s question, whether a bundle of sticks in England is called a faggot, is answered by the dictionaries. It is. The English word faggot came into Middle English from the Old French fagot, a bundle of sticks; its remoter origin is uncertain, and the Oxford University Press’s etymological record, following the Oxford English Dictionary, prints the French descent and leaves the root beyond it open. The Italian fascio descends from the Latin fascis, a bundle; the English word’s documented road runs through French, and the two words meet at the meaning, a bound bundle of wood. Older English translations of Aesop’s fable use the word for the fable’s own bundle: one widely reprinted translation has the father call for “a faggot of sticks” and then bid his sons untie the faggots.

The bundle sense survives in Britain as living usage. Dictionaries record it as chiefly British and Irish: a bundle of sticks or brushwood bound together for fuel. The word also names a traditional food: faggots are meatballs of minced pork offcuts and offal, heart, liver, and fatty belly meat, wrapped in caul fat, traditional in South and Mid Wales and in the English Midlands, carried into industrial South Wales by migrating workers in the middle of the nineteenth century, and sold under the name in British shops to this day.

The word’s later senses are a matter of dated record, and the desk prints the dates without commentary. The Oxford English Dictionary’s citations, as set out in the Oxford University Press’s etymological series, show faggot in use as a term of reproach for a woman from the end of the sixteenth century. Its first recorded use against homosexual men in print is American and twentieth-century: Jackson and Hellyer’s A Vocabulary of Criminal Slang, published in 1914, prints the word in that sense, and the shortened form fag in the same sense is first recorded in 1923, in Nels Anderson’s The Hobo. A claim in wide circulation, that the slang derives from the burning of men at the stake with bundles of sticks, is recorded by etymologists as an urban legend: Douglas R. Harper, of the Online Etymology Dictionary, calls it an “oft-reprinted assertion,” no evidence supports it, and the punishment in England was hanging, not burning.

The League’s Entry

The Anti-Defamation League keeps an entry for the fasces in its database of hate symbols, and the entry is headed by the database’s standing instruction, printed for every symbol it lists: “All the symbols depicted in the hate symbols database must be evaluated in the context in which they appear. Few symbols represent just one idea or are used exclusively by one group.” The entry itself, first published on August 28, 2019 and updated on November 18, 2026, records the symbol’s career in sequence: its Roman origin, its survival in heraldry and as a symbol of governmental authority, its use by the United States, in the military, in government agencies, in the House of Representatives, and on the Mercury dime, and then Mussolini’s adoption of it, which made it, in the League’s words, “the most well-known symbol of fascist Italy, and thus of fascism itself.” The entry closes with the symbol’s third career: from the late 2000s, it records, some American white supremacists took up the fasces, possibly because it lacked the swastika’s connotations and because its place in the symbology of the United States government could be pointed to in its defense; the entry names the groups that have used it, among them Youth for Western Civilization, the Southern White Alliance, Vanguard America, Patriot Front, and the National Socialist Movement. The League does not file the fasces as a hate symbol in itself. Its entry treats the meaning of the bundle as a question of the context in which it appears.

The Record as It Stands

The record, assembled, is this. A bundle of rods and an axe was the badge of a Roman magistrate’s power to punish, Etruscan in its earliest surviving form, and inside Rome its missing axe marked the citizen’s right of appeal. A fable about a father and his sons taught, with the same object, that what breaks singly holds together; the lesson was taught again with darts in Scythia and with arrows on the Mongolian steppe. Renaissance readers joined the fable to the Roman badge, and the merged emblem, unity in a bundle, was carved by republics that understood themselves to be bound by law: behind the Speaker’s chair, under Lincoln’s hands, on the republic’s dime, on the seals of France and the arms of Ecuador. The Haudenosaunee taught the lesson with five arrows and built a confederacy on it, advised the English colonies to do the same in words Franklin printed, and were acknowledged by the Congress of the United States, in 1988, for their contribution to the American founding. Unions carried the fable’s bundle on their banners as voluntary solidarity. An Italian movement took the bundle’s name and the Roman emblem in 1919, and defined itself, in its own Doctrine, as a totalitarian state in which no human or spiritual values exist outside the whole. The scholars who define that ideology define it by behavior, by features, and by myth, and not by its emblem. The English word for the bundle survives in Britain on firewood and on a plate of faggots, and its American slang sense is a twentieth-century record with dates. That is what the record says, and the desk draws no conclusion from it.
