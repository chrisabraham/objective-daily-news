Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena: What the Record Shows, From the Nuremberg Sky Battle of 1561 to the PURSUE Releases of 2026

The United States government has investigated reports of objects in the sky since 1947, and its standing finding, restated in every official report through October 2026, is that no verifiable evidence of extraterrestrial technology has been found. Around that finding stands a documented record of real reports, classified programs concealed under the reports, a minority of cases unresolved for lack of data, and a folklore of Grays, Nordics, reptilians, and a Galactic Federation whose every figure can be traced to a named book, broadcast, or claimant. This report prints the whole field in its separate ledgers: what is documented, what is unresolved, what is folklore, and what is forged, with the evidence for each printed beside the claim.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1630 ET, Friday, November 13, 2026.

The File in Brief

People have reported strange objects and beings in the sky for centuries, the United States has formally investigated such reports since 1947, and through November 13, 2026, no government investigation, archive release, or congressional proceeding has produced a verified non-human artifact, craft, or body. The Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), in its March 2024 historical review and its fiscal 2025 annual report, found no verifiable evidence that the U.S. government or any private company has recovered or reverse-engineered extraterrestrial technology; NASA's independent study of September 2023 found no evidence that unidentified anomalous phenomena are extraterrestrial in origin. Against those findings stands a genuine residue: of 12,618 reports collected by Project Blue Book between 1947 and 1969, 701 were left unidentified, and AARO's current caseload holds cases it cannot resolve for insufficient data, including a June 2026 report by two federal agents of a silent black triangle at a sensitive western site, an incident AARO says warrants further investigation. The year 2026 brought the largest release program in the subject's history: PURSUE, the Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters, which published 478 government files in seven releases between May 8 and October 23, 2026. No file in those releases makes a finding of non-human origin. The folklore that surrounds the record is a separate and traceable literature: the Gray alien was standardized by a 1987 book cover, the Tall Whites rest on one man's uncorroborated books, the reptilian bloodline theory was published in the 1990s by an author whose framework recycles an antisemitic forgery, and the Galactic Federation entered the news in 2020 through a retired Israeli official who offered no evidence. This report keeps the four piles separate, because the confusion of the subject is the confusion of the piles.

The desk's method is the paper's method. Material was supplied to the desk as a stack of AI-assistant summaries of the whole field. Every checkable claim in that stack was re-verified against the primary record before printing: AARO's reports and case resolutions, the Department of War's PURSUE releases at war.gov, the FBI Vault, the National Archives and the Air Force fact sheets, the congressional hearing record, NASA's study, and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence's 2021 assessment. Three claims in the supplied material failed verification and are corrected in the body of this report where they belong: the PURSUE program was not created by executive order; a speed of roughly 1,800 miles per hour attributed to the 2026 triangle appears in no released document; and the balloon explanation for the first pilot death of the era, in 1948, is a later official revision, not a settled finding. Nothing in this report rests on a source the reader cannot open.

1. The Names, and What Each One Refuses to Carry

Every name the subject has carried was chosen to exclude something, and the exclusions are the history of the subject in miniature. Flying saucer, coined by headline writers in the summer of 1947, names a shape and carries the whole carnival with it. UFO, unidentified flying object, the Air Force's term, narrows the claim to an object in flight and says nothing about what it is. UAP began as unidentified aerial phenomena and now stands in U.S. government usage for unidentified anomalous phenomena, a wording broadened to cover reports in air, space, and water and reports of movement between them, and chosen in part so that military pilots would file reports without the saucer stigma. NHI, non-human intelligence, is the phrase of the recent disclosure legislation; it names an agent rather than an object and is deliberately silent on whether the agent is extraterrestrial, interdimensional, or something else. Disclosure itself names two different projects that share a word: the verifiable project of releasing records, which has happened at scale in 2026, and the anticipated revelation of recovered craft and beings, which has not happened in any public record. Jacques Vallée's older umbrella, the Phenomenon, is the only term in the literature built to hold the physical reports and the folklore in one hand without ruling on either, and this report follows its discipline without adopting its conclusions: the record in one ledger, the lore in another, and no claim promoted from one to the other without evidence.

2. Before 1947: Every Age Saw Its Own Sky

The pre-modern record is a record of reports, and the reports arrive dressed in the cosmology of whoever made them. On April 14, 1561, the Nuremberg printer Hans Glaser published an illustrated broadsheet of globes, crosses, and tubes appearing to fight over the city at dawn and fall to earth smoking; the broadsheet is a real document, preserved in Zurich, and it frames the sight as a divine warning. The leading modern explanation is an atmospheric-optical display, sun dogs and halo effects recast in the era's genre of wonder signs; it is plausible and, from one stylized source, unprovable. At Basel in 1566 a similar broadsheet appeared. What the documents establish is that people reported structured wonders in the sky four centuries before powered flight. What they cannot establish is what was in the sky.

The first modern wave wore the technology of its own decade. From November 1896 to May 1897, American newspapers printed more than a thousand stories of a cigar-shaped mystery airship with searchlights crossing the country, years before the Wright brothers flew; investigators attribute the wave largely to misidentifications, hoaxes, and press sensationalism feeding one another. Its most famous item is its smallest: on April 19, 1897, the Dallas Morning News reported that an airship had crashed into Judge Proctor's windmill at Aurora, Texas, and that the pilot, not of this world, had been buried in the town cemetery. No wreckage, burial record, or official investigation survives, and the modern consensus is that the story was fabricated. It is printed here as what it is: a newspaper tale of 1897, not an event. The pattern the wave establishes recurs in every decade after: the visitors arrive in machines that look like the expected future of the people reporting them.

The war years produced reports by trained crews, and the reports are documented even where their objects are not. From 1942, Allied aircrew over Europe reported balls of fire and moving lights pacing their aircraft; American pilots named them foo fighters, and British and American intelligence collected the reports and found no Axis weapon to explain them. In 1946 and 1947, Sweden and its neighbors logged some two thousand reports of ghost rockets, initially suspected to be Soviet tests of captured German missiles; British intelligence chief R. V. Jones believed the scare was bright meteors read through fear of Soviet expansion, and no rocket or craft was ever established. Both waves are confirmed as waves of reports investigated by governments. Neither carries an established cause.

The indigenous traditions are knowledge systems, not sighting reports, and the modern literature's use of them is documented better than the use is justified. Many Native American nations hold star knowledge and traditions of sky beings and star ancestors; Lakota, Dakota, and Ojibwe star work is published today by community-led projects such as Native Skywatchers. The Hopi Ant People, the Wandjina figures of Aboriginal Australian rock art, and similar traditions are spiritual and relational within their own systems. The claim that the Dogon of Mali possessed ancient knowledge of Sirius's invisible companion star rests on the fieldwork of Marcel Griaule as popularized by Robert Temple in 1976; a 1991 restudy by Walter van Beek found his Dogon informants did not hold the knowledge attributed to them, and the mainstream critique, argued by Ian Ridpath and Carl Sagan, is that the material likely entered through European contact. The transmission chain has never been traced, so the ancient-knowledge claim stands unverified. The ancient-astronaut literature built on such material has a documented verdict of its own: Zecharia Sitchin's readings of the Sumerian Anunnaki as alien colonizers, from The 12th Planet (1976), are rejected by Assyriologists, who record that the word means princely offspring and that no peer-reviewed specialist has endorsed his translations; Erich von Däniken's Chariots of the Gods? (1968) was branded pseudohistory by archaeologists and historians for factual errors. Ezekiel's wheel, the Hindu vimanas, and the Nuremberg tubes all sit in this same ledger: real texts and images, carrying interpretations their sources did not make.

3. 1947 and 1948: The Saucer, the Disc, and the First Death

The modern era has an exact birthday and an exact mechanism. On June 24, 1947, private pilot Kenneth Arnold reported nine bright objects in formation near Mount Rainier, Washington, moving, in the line quoted ever since, like a saucer would if you skipped it across the water. Reporters Bill Bequette and Nolan Skiff put the sighting on the wire; a headline writer supplied flying saucers; and within weeks the country was reporting saucers as a shape, though Arnold's own contemporary accounts used saucer, disk, and pie-pan of the shape as well as the motion. The Smithsonian's account of the summer is the desk's: the term entered the vocabulary from that newsroom chain, and the shape of the reports followed the word.

Roswell runs on two timelines, and they must not be merged. On the 1947 timeline: ranch foreman Mac Brazel found debris on a ranch northwest of Roswell, New Mexico; on July 8, 1947, the Roswell Army Air Field's 509th Bomb Group announced it had come into possession of a flying disc, and the Roswell Daily Record printed the announcement; Eighth Air Force headquarters at Fort Worth retracted it the same day as a weather balloon with a radar reflector. On the second timeline: the story slept for thirty years; in 1978 the physicist and UFO researcher Stanton Friedman interviewed Jesse Marcel, the intelligence officer who had handled the debris in 1947; in 1980 Charles Berlitz and William Moore published The Roswell Incident, and the crash, the bodies, and the secret facilities entered the story from accounts gathered decades after the event. The Air Force's 1994 report, produced under congressional inquiry, concluded the debris came from Project Mogul, a classified train of high-altitude balloons carrying acoustic sensors to detect Soviet nuclear tests. Its 1997 follow-up, Case Closed, attributed the alien-body accounts to anthropomorphic test dummies from 1950s balloon programs combined with two real accidents, a 1956 KC-97 crash and a 1959 balloon mishap, compressed in memory into 1947. What the documents establish: a classified device crashed, the military's first statement was false, and the debris identification in the 1994 report is the Air Force's conclusion, disputed by Roswell proponents to this day. What no document from 1947 establishes is a body.

The era's first death is documented; its explanation is a revision, and the revision is printed as one. On January 7, 1948, Captain Thomas Mantell of the Kentucky Air National Guard climbed his F-51 from Godman Field in pursuit of an unidentified object and died, on the U.S. Army's own history, most likely blacking out from oxygen deprivation near 25,000 feet. The Air Force's initial assessment was the planet Venus. The identification with a Navy Skyhook balloon, a classified high-altitude program, is Captain Edward Ruppelt's 1952 revision from inside Project Blue Book, offered as probable; the Army's history states plainly that no documentation proves or disproves it, and no specific Skyhook launch record for that day has been located. Retellings that print Skyhook as the settled cause go one step past the record.

4. The Investigation Era: 12,618 Reports and a Debunking Policy

The United States investigated the reports for twenty-two years, and its own fact sheet carries the totals. Project Sign opened in January 1948; its analysts' Estimate of the Situation, reported to have leaned toward an extraterrestrial reading of the best cases, was rejected by the Air Force chief of staff and ordered destroyed, an episode known from later accounts rather than a surviving document. Sign became Grudge in February 1949, and Grudge became Blue Book, which ran from 1952 to December 17, 1969. The Air Force fact sheet at the National Archives states the arithmetic: from 1947 to 1969, 12,618 sightings were reported to Project Blue Book, and of these 701 remain unidentified. Unidentified is a classification about the file, not the object: it records that the report could not be resolved on the information collected, and the same fact sheet records that no reported UFO was ever an indication of threat to national security and no evidence ever showed the reports to be extraterrestrial. The project's termination rested on the University of Colorado's Condon Report, completed in 1968 and published in January 1969, together with the National Academy of Sciences' review of it and the Air Force's own experience; accounts that credit Condon alone simplify the stated basis.

The era's most consequential document is not a sighting report but a policy. In January 1953 the CIA convened the Robertson Panel, which recommended that UFOs be stripped of their special status and aura of mystery through a public education program; the surviving Durant memorandum records the program's two aims as training and debunking, conducted through mass media. The policy was aimed at a real operational fear, that report traffic would clog military channels in a crisis, and it is the documented origin of the official ridicule that witnesses complained of for decades after: not an inference by believers, but a recommendation in a government file. The same file holds the era's largest event: on the nights of July 19–20 and July 26–27, 1952, radar operators at Washington National Airport and Andrews Air Force Base tracked returns over the capital, pilots and ground observers reported lights, and F-94 interceptors were scrambled, most clearly on the second weekend; the Air Force attributed the returns at its largest postwar press conference to temperature inversion. And it holds the era's most famous dismissal: on March 25, 1966, Blue Book's scientific consultant J. Allen Hynek, pressed at a Detroit press conference on the Michigan sightings, proposed marsh gas for the Dexter and Hillsdale cases, and swamp gas entered the language as the shorthand for official contempt. Hynek's own trajectory is part of the record: he began as the project's skeptic and left it arguing that a residue of reports described a real anomaly, and his close-encounter classification is still the field's vocabulary.

The era also contains the mechanism that keeps the subject alive: real secrecy, accurately perceived and wrongly attributed. Groom Lake, Area 51, was a classified test site for the U-2 and the A-12 OXCART reconnaissance aircraft, and the CIA has acknowledged that high-altitude reconnaissance flights generated a large share of the era's UFO reports, flown at heights civilians and airline pilots had no reason to expect aircraft. Observers who reported structured craft where no craft should be were in those cases reporting real machines; the government let the wrong public explanation stand because the right one was classified. Roswell's Mogul train is the same mechanism at smaller scale. The record supports the general claim that the government concealed programs under UFO reports. It does not support the specific claim, examined in section 8, that any of the concealed programs was a recovered non-human craft.

5. The Modern Military Cases: What the Sensors Do and Do Not Show

The strongest modern case is the 2004 Nimitz encounter, and its evidence divides into three kinds that retellings habitually merge. On November 14, 2004, off Southern California, Commander David Fravor and his wing pilot, Lieutenant Commander Alex Dietrich, visually encountered a smooth white oblong object, about forty feet long, wingless, without exhaust, hovering over disturbed water; Fravor testified to the encounter under oath before Congress on July 26, 2023, and Dietrich corroborated it on CBS's 60 Minutes in 2021. Fravor testified that the cruiser USS Princeton had tracked objects in the area for over two weeks; no raw Princeton radar data has been publicly released, so the extreme performance figures attached to the tracks, descents from high altitude in seconds, stand in the public record as testimony, not as instrument data a reader can check. The infrared video called FLIR1 was recorded by a later sortie the same day, weapons systems officer Chad Underwood's flight; Fravor himself testified the video was taken by the other airplane, and Underwood did not visually acquire the object. Retellings that put the video on Fravor's jet are false. The Pentagon formally released FLIR1 with two 2015 videos, Gimbal and GoFast, on April 27, 2020, stating that the phenomena in them remain characterized as unidentified. A genuine video is an authentic recording; it is not an authenticated explanation, and the public data set for the Nimitz case does not include the synchronized radar, range, and timing records that a calculation of the object's acceleration would require.

Where full analysis has been possible, it has cut toward the ordinary, and the desk prints those findings at the same weight. GoFast, the 2015 clip that appears to show an object skimming the ocean at high speed, was worked as an example by NASA's study team, which calculated the object at roughly 13,000 feet moving at about 40 miles per hour, a typical wind speed at that altitude; AARO's February 2025 case resolution assessed with high confidence that the object did not move at anomalous speeds and that its apparent velocity is motion parallax from the filming jet, though AARO did not definitively identify the object. The 2013 Aguadilla, Puerto Rico infrared video, long circulated as one object splitting and entering the sea, was resolved by AARO in March 2025: with high confidence the video shows two objects traveling together, in a straight line over land at about eight miles per hour, which never entered the water; the split and the water entry are perspective and thermal effects. AARO's own attribution, at moderate confidence, is a pair of sky lanterns; a partner assessment inside the same report proposed mylar balloons, and summaries that print balloons as AARO's finding misstate the document. The Belgian wave of 1989–90, in which police and civilians reported large triangular craft and Belgian F-16s made nine intercept attempts with brief radar locks on the night of March 30–31, 1990, was later analyzed to the locks being the jets' radars locking onto each other and atmospheric Bragg scattering; neither pilot visually confirmed an object. The wave's one famous photograph, the Petit-Rechain image, was confessed a hoax on Belgian television on July 26, 2011, by Patrick Maréchal, who had built it from painted polystyrene and flashlights. The Phoenix Lights of March 13, 1997, separate into two events that retellings merge: a V-shaped formation crossing Arizona in the evening, and a row of lights over Phoenix near 2200 that is attributed, on the record of a participating pilot in 2007, to illumination flares dropped by Maryland Air National Guard A-10s on a training exercise. The earlier formation's own explanation remains contested between witness accounts and a later skeptical identification of a separate aircraft formation; the flare attribution covers the later lights, and only those.

The institutional assessments of the modern era are unanimous in wording and limited in data, and both halves of that sentence are the finding. The Director of National Intelligence's preliminary assessment of June 25, 2021, covered 144 reports from U.S. government sources since 2004, 80 of them observed with multiple sensors; investigators identified exactly one, a large deflating balloon, and left the rest unexplained, citing limited data, and made no extraterrestrial conclusion. NASA's independent study team, reporting September 14, 2023, found no evidence that UAP are extraterrestrial in origin and stated that the limited number of high-quality observations makes firm scientific conclusions impossible; its recommendation was not a verdict but an instrument: standardized, calibrated data collection, and a NASA director of UAP research, appointed the same day.

6. Disclosure as a Public Record, 2017 to October 2026

The disclosure era opened with journalism, not with a government announcement. In December 2017 the New York Times reported the Pentagon's funded UAP research effort, known through the names AATIP and AAWSAP, and published the Navy videos; the 2020 formal release followed. Congress then built the record in public: the July 26, 2023 hearing of the House Oversight subcommittee, at which Fravor testified firsthand, aviator Ryan Graves testified to reporting stigma and his own squadrons' encounters, and former intelligence officer David Grusch testified under oath that he had been informed, in the course of his official duties, of a multi-decade UAP crash retrieval and reverse engineering program, and that, in his answer to Representative Mace, biologics came with some of these recoveries and were non-human, on the assessment of people with direct knowledge he had spoken to. The hearing record is exact about the basis: Grusch's own opening states that his testimony is based on information he has been given by individuals, and that he is speaking to the facts as he has been told them. He claimed in open session no firsthand sight of a craft or a body; specifics were deferred to classified session. The crash-retrieval program he described remains unverified in the public record, and AARO's historical review, examining the same claims, found no empirical evidence for it. Both of those sentences are the state of the record; neither cancels the other. The transparency hearings continued with the House Task Force on the Declassification of Federal Secrets on September 9, 2025, chaired by Representative Luna, with veteran and witness testimony on reporting and whistleblower protection; testimony at a hearing is testimony, and the 2025 hearing issued no committee finding of non-human technology. No public congressional UAP hearing was held in calendar 2026; a planned September hearing fell through, and the chair of the Oversight Committee said in the autumn that he hoped to revive one, with no topic or witnesses agreed.

AARO's two capstone documents define the official position, and one program in the file defines its limit. The Historical Record Report, Volume I, cleared March 6 and released March 8, 2024, covering U.S. government involvement from 1945 to October 2023, concluded that AARO found no evidence that any U.S. government investigation, sponsored research, or review panel ever confirmed a sighting as extraterrestrial technology, and no empirical evidence that extraterrestrial technology was recovered or reverse-engineered; the Pentagon's summary sentence is that AARO has found no verifiable evidence for claims that the U.S. government and private companies have access to or have been reverse-engineering extraterrestrial technology. Volume II, promised to cover later claims and interviews, had not been published as of October 2026. The report's examination of KONA BLUE is the record's cleanest exhibit of how a retrieval program exists on paper without existing in fact: KONA BLUE was a prospective special access program proposed to the Department of Homeland Security by advocates of the earlier AAWSAP effort who believed the government held off-world technology and biologics; the declassified record states that it was never approved or formally established, that the Deputy Secretary disapproved it roughly six months after its 2011 establishment as a proposal and ordered its immediate termination, and that it never received any materials or funding and no data or material of any kind was ever transferred to it. The fiscal 2025 annual report, published July 20, 2026, on a May 30, 2025 information cutoff, carries the current arithmetic: AARO received 319 reports in the period and held 1,870 reports in total; it resolved 370 cases during the period, of which 114 were of the new reports and 256 were backlog, and every resolved case was attributed to a prosaic object, including 238 resolved as satellite flaring by a new three-dimensional modeling capability; 191 of the new reports were transferred to the active archive for insufficient data, and nine were identified for further analysis by intelligence and science partners. The report states that no resolved case indicated advanced foreign or breakthrough technology, and no evidence that the U.S. government or a private entity has captured or exploited UAP material.

The largest event of the record is the 2026 release program, and its trigger and its contents are both routinely misstated. PURSUE, the Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters, is a Department of War program housing declassified and historical UAP files at war.gov/UFO, launched May 8, 2026, which drew, on the Department's own statement, over one billion visits. It was not created by executive order; no executive order, memorandum, or other presidential document on UAP appears in the Federal Register. The trigger was the President's February 19, 2026 social media post directing the Secretary of War and other agencies to begin identifying and releasing government files on alien life, UAP, and UFOs. Seven releases followed on a rolling basis, May 8, May 22, June 12, July 10, August 7, September 18, and October 23, 2026, totaling 478 files on the portal's own list and independent tallies of the Department's announcements. A September 14, 2026 Department legal waiver allowed current and former personnel and contractors to disclose UAP-related national defense information to designated PURSUE representatives notwithstanding their nondisclosure agreements; it authorizes disclosure to the program, not to the public. The releases are records, and their evidentiary weight is the weight of records: a released file establishes that the document exists and can be examined, not that its contents are true. No release makes a finding of non-human or extraterrestrial origin, and each arrives under AARO's standing position unchanged.

The newest solid fact in the disclosure record is the seventh release, and it is also the discipline's best current lesson in reading one. Release 07, published October 23, 2026, contains 28 documents and no video, and its lead case is a June 2026 incident at a sensitive national security site in the western United States, the exact site and date redacted, in which two federal special agents reported a silent, matte-black triangular object, with a corroborating account from a third official at a separate post. AARO's released index states that it considers the incident to warrant further investigation due to the close alignment of the phenomena's reported morphological features and kinematic profiles with those of other reported incidents in the western United States region. The file contains two primary records, an AARO memorandum and an FBI FD-302, and they do not agree with each other: the FBI statement puts the sighting at approximately 1923 hours and approximately three seconds in total and describes the object as most resembling a delta wing drone; the AARO memorandum puts it at 2023 local and approximately thirty-two seconds, divided among a ten-second ascent, a twenty-second hover, and a two-to-three-second departure, with a wingspan estimate of thirteen to fifteen feet. Neither document contains an instrumented speed measurement or a photograph; the file's nine images are FBI forensic renderings. A speed of roughly 1,800 miles per hour circulating in coverage of the case appears in neither document and is arithmetic performed on the FBI's distance and the FBI's three seconds by commentators; it is not a finding, and this report does not print it as one. A search of the canyon below the site the following morning found no wreckage or ground damage, and neither document reaches a conclusion on what the object was. The former AARO director Sean Kirkpatrick told Fox News Digital that the case rests on testimony, with no photographs and no instrument readings. It is, as of November 13, 2026, the record's frontier: a real report, by named-category officials, flagged by the government's own office for further work, unresolved.

7. The Lore's Cast, With Origins

The beings of the literature form a taxonomy with publication dates, and the dates are the finding. No physical evidence supports any entry in the table below; what the record documents is where each figure came from, who published it, and what happened to the claim afterward. The figures also change with the culture that reports them: benevolent space brothers in the atomic 1950s, clinical examiners in the abduction decades, hidden rulers in the conspiracy 1990s, and a managed federation in the channeling literature throughout.

Two conclusions about the cast are documentary, and they are the only two the desk draws. 8. The Far Side of the Moon, and the Forgery File

The Moon's far side is a place, not a darkness, and it has been photographed, landed on, and sampled. The far side is the hemisphere turned away from Earth by tidal locking; it receives as much sunlight as the near side and passes through the same phases. The Soviet Luna 3 returned the first photographs of it in October 1959. China's Chang'e 4 made the first landing there on January 3, 2019, and Chang'e 6 returned the first far-side samples to Earth on June 25, 2024; NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has mapped the hemisphere at meter scale and photographed the Chinese landers in place. Against that survey record, the literature's claims of alien bases, mining operations, glass domes, and astronaut warnings of watching spacecraft have produced no verified location, image, or artifact in sixty-five years of orbital photography. The far side's communications blackout is real and is engineering, not concealment: the Moon blocks direct radio links, which is why the Chinese missions fly relay satellites. A claim that the far side is permanently dark is false, and it is the load-bearing error of the shelf of books built on it.

The forgery file's centerpiece is Majestic 12, and the archive that holds it is the lesson. In 1988, two FBI offices received versions of a memorandum titled Operation Majestic-12, purporting to be a 1952 briefing for President Eisenhower on a secret committee exploiting a recovered extraterrestrial aircraft. The FBI's file, open today in the FBI Vault, records that an Air Force investigation determined the document to be a fake, and the Bureau's released copies are stamped, in the file's own word, BOGUS. No corroborating government record of an MJ-12 committee has been found. The documents live in a genuine federal archive because the government investigated them, and their presence in the archive is routinely cited as their endorsement; it is the opposite. The same mechanism runs through the whole subject: the PURSUE releases of 2026 are an archive of records, some of which contain allegations, hoaxes, and unresolved reports, and release is examination, not authentication. A reader who holds that distinction can use every archive in this report. A reader who drops it will be owned by all of them.

The desk's cocked hat, stated as method rather than verdict, since the record carries no verdict past the one its custodians have printed. What would move the record is known exactly, because the modern cases show its absence: one case documented with synchronized radar, optical, and infrared records, with calibration, range, and timing sufficient for independent teams to compute what the object did, and a custodian willing to release the raw data. The Nimitz case has the witnesses and lacks the public data. The 2026 triangle has the official flag and lacks the instruments. Blue Book has the volume and lacks the resolution. Until a case arrives with its measurements attached, the documented record stands where AARO and NASA have left it, and this report's last paragraph is the summary the first one promised: people report wonders in the sky, in every century and in the idiom of the century; governments have investigated the reports since 1947 and have concealed real machines among them; a residue of reports is unresolved for want of data, and none is resolved as non-human; the beings and federations of the literature are a datable folklore with named authors and no artifacts; and the largest release of records in the subject's history, the 478 files of 2026, changed the size of the archive and did not change the finding.
