The Lucia Prologue, Filed From the Record

Objective

The Lucia Prologue in Grand Theft Auto VI: Payday Loans, Eviction Notices, Deactivated Accounts, and the Call That Follows

The Prologue of Grand Theft Auto VI sends Lucia Caminos from a café table—in the accounts of the scene in circulation, an eviction notice in front of her and her driver account suspended, her infant son beside her—to a payday-loan storefront, a police chase built to fail, and a child-welfare referral, inside twenty minutes. Clips of the sequence, and above all of its coda in the café, have circulated since the game’s release on November 19, 2026. This report sets out the record of the American machinery the sequence passes through, in the order the afternoon passes through it: the loan, the account, the notice, the referral, and the receiving end of the call. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1200 ET, Tuesday, November 24, 2026.

The Scene as It Stands in the Record

Grand Theft Auto VI was released on Thursday, November 19, 2026. Its first mission, the Prologue, is the occasion of this report. The walkthroughs published on release day and cross-checked against one another—the guides of PowerPyx and GTABase among them—record the sequence beat by beat, and clips of it have been in wide circulation since the day the game went on sale.

The Prologue opens inside the Hamlet Correctional Facility, where Lucia Caminos, one of the game’s two protagonists, is serving a sentence. A counselor, Stefanie Gates, interviews her for a supervised release program—home detention with electronic monitoring, offered, as the counselor explains, because the county needs beds—and asks how she came to be inside. The question starts a flashback, and the player plays the crime.

In the flashback, Lucia is sitting in a coffee shop with her infant son and no money. In the accounts of the scene in circulation, a final notice of eviction sits on the table and her rideshare driver account stands suspended. She tells the staff she will be back in two minutes. She leaves the baby in the café, crosses the mall to a storefront signed Payday Loan, and robs it at gunpoint. A customer seizes her; she fights free. The police chase that follows cannot be won—the guides are explicit that the escape is scripted to fail—and when she makes her way back toward the coffee shop, the police are waiting. The staff had already reported the child abandoned. The counselor, back in the present, tells her what the game’s later account confirms: custody is a separate matter for child services. The boy, Tomás, called Tommy, was taken into child welfare on the day of the arrest and lives, in the game’s account, with his aunt.

The fragment of the sequence that has traveled furthest is its coda, in the café after the arrest. The transcripts of the clips in circulation record a café worker addressing the officers: “Oye, we’ve been calling you. She just walked out and left it here. We don’t know what to do with this, you know? I got frijoles burning back there!” An officer answers by radio: “Yeah, we have the baby. I’m gonna need a CPS officer here right away.” The worker’s beans, burning in a kitchen behind the worst afternoon of a customer’s life, are the line the clips repeat. The desk files the scene as it stands in the record and takes up, in order, the four pieces of American machinery the sequence passes through on its way to that radio call, and then the receiving end of the call itself.

The Storefront Across the Mall

The store Lucia robs is a payday lender, a business whose documented economics are a matter of federal record. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s study of the industry, drawn from more than 12 million storefront payday loans over a twelve-month period, found that four out of five payday loans are rolled over or renewed within two weeks, and that roughly half of all loans are made to borrowers in loan sequences lasting ten or more loans in a row. More than one in five initial loans results in a sequence of seven or more loans; at the typical fee the Bureau recorded, 15 percent of the amount borrowed, a borrower who renews seven times has paid more in fees than she borrowed. The Bureau’s director, releasing the study, put the number of American adults borrowing through payday loans at about 12 million, on industry analysts’ figures. Of borrowers paid monthly—many of them elderly or receiving disability benefits—one in five borrowed in every month of the study year.

The storefront itself is a fixed feature of the landscape the game reproduces. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, surveying the industry in 2014, counted approximately 20,000 storefront lenders in the United States, an average of 6.3 stores for every 100,000 people, and recorded that the stores tend to concentrate in locations with higher-than-average poverty rates and more single parents. The Bureau noted in the same period that the loans are also offered online. The game’s geography—a payday storefront across a mall from a café, close enough to reach inside two minutes—is the industry’s geography as the federal record describes it.

The Suspended Account

The second prop in the accounts of the scene is an income already cut off: a rideshare driver account suspended before the afternoon begins. Deactivation—the platforms’ word for the closing of a driver’s account—is documented in the survey record. In October 2025 the Asian American Legal Defense and Education Fund published its analysis of surveys, conducted by the New York Taxi Workers Alliance, of nearly 350 deactivated Uber and Lyft drivers in New York City. Seventy percent of the drivers deactivated by Uber and 76 percent of those deactivated by Lyft reported that they had received no prior notice. Nearly all of them appealed. More than 90 percent remained permanently locked out of their accounts.

The fund’s report records the procedure as the drivers described it: penalties imposed on vague allegations, without disclosure of dates, details or evidence, and without an opportunity to review or rebut the allegations before the income stopped. The drivers surveyed were, in the main, long-time drivers with high ratings. The desk records the mechanism as it stands in the survey: for a worker whose income runs through an application, the account is the job, and its suspension is a dismissal delivered by notice, effective on receipt, with the appeal heard afterward and, in more than nine cases out of ten in the New York survey, denied in effect by silence or refusal.

The Notice on the Table

The third prop is the paper on the café table: a final notice of eviction. An eviction notice is the front end of a court process whose volume is tracked, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, by the Eviction Lab at Princeton University. Through its Eviction Tracking System, the Lab monitors filings in ten states and more than thirty cities, jurisdictions that are home to roughly one in every three renter households in the country. In those jurisdictions alone, landlords filed just over one million eviction cases in 2024. The overall filing rate, including the full-state data, was 7.3 filings for every hundred renter households.

The Lab’s city figures give the rate a clock. In Phoenix, Arizona, landlords filed 86,946 evictions in 2024—a record for the city, at a rate of 14.3 filings per hundred renter households—which the Lab renders as one filing every six minutes. In 2024, 58 percent of the people named on eviction filings in the tracked jurisdictions were women, against 53 percent of the renting population. A filing is not a removal; it is a case opened, one of a million and more opened in the tracked third of the country in a single year, each beginning, as the game’s prop begins, with a notice.

The Referral

The radio call in the coda—an officer asking for a child protective services officer “right away”—sets in motion the fourth piece of machinery, and it is the one whose paperwork the scene’s counselor names: custody is a separate matter. What the call produces, in the American system as the federal government reports it, is a referral. The Children’s Bureau’s Child Maltreatment 2023, the annual report of the National Child Abuse and Neglect Data System, records that in federal fiscal year 2023 child protective services agencies received an estimated 4,399,000 referrals alleging maltreatment, involving approximately 7,782,000 children. Among the 46 states that report both figures, 47.5 percent of referrals were screened in for a response. In all, 3,081,715 children received an investigation or an alternative response, and 546,159 children were determined to be victims of maltreatment, the largest category of it neglect.

The reports come, in the main, from the professions that stand where the café staff stood. Professionals submitted 70.9 percent of the reports in fiscal 2023, and legal and law enforcement personnel were the largest single professional source, at 21.4 percent—the game’s officer with his radio is, statistically, the system’s most common reporter. Where a child is removed, the placement that follows has a modal form. The Department of Health and Human Services’ research arm, analyzing the federal foster-care data, records that on September 30, 2024, 126,944 children in foster care—39 percent of all children in care that day—were living with relatives or kin. The game’s disposition of Tomás, taken from his mother on the day of her arrest and placed with his aunt while a separate custody case runs, is the largest single disposition in the federal figures: the 39 percent placed with kin stands ahead of the 28 percent placed with non-relative foster families in the same count.

The Receiving End

The last fact of the scene is the one the clips repeat without noticing it: nobody in the café, the storefront, or the police car treats the afternoon as unusual. The documented volumes at the receiving end of such calls describe the working condition that produces the manner. The Government Accountability Office, citing the National Emergency Number Association, records that Americans make about 240 million calls to 911 each year, answered through approximately 5,300 call centers. The National Fire Protection Association’s research records that United States fire departments responded to 42.4 million calls in 2023, up from 10.8 million in 1980; fires accounted for about 1.4 million of them, and the U.S. Fire Administration records that nearly 67 percent of fire department response calls in 2023 were for emergency medical service. The emergency, in the receiving system’s ledger, is the minority entry. The remainder is the routine the system exists to absorb.

The literature measures what the absorption costs the people who perform it. A meta-analysis of the emergency medical services literature, pooling 35 studies of 32,841 personnel, estimated the prevalence of burnout among them at 40 percent. A cohort study of EMS workers in Minnesota, published in the Western Journal of Emergency Medicine in 2018, found burnout in 18 percent of respondents overall and in 32 percent of the dispatchers among them. The same study records the limit of the routine: the seven pediatric incident types in its survey accounted for seven of the eight incident types respondents rated most difficult to cope with. A call involving a child is, in the measured record, the call that does not become routine. The officer in the café asks for the CPS officer right away, and the kitchen worker goes back to his beans; the record shows both responses, at national scale, in the same figures.

The desk prints the record as it stands: a twenty-minute fictional afternoon, documented beat by beat in the guides and clips of a game five days on sale, passing through a loan market measured by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, a dismissal procedure measured by survey in New York, a court docket counted past a million filings a year in the tracked third of the country, a referral system that fields 4.4 million allegations a year, and a receiving end that answers 240 million calls a year with a measured burnout rate of two workers in five. The systems did not stop for the afternoon. That is what their figures say, and the desk draws no conclusion from them.
