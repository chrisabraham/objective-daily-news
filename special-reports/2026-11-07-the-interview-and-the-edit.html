The Media File — A Broadcast and Its Record

Objective

The Interview and the Edit: What the Record Shows About a 60 Minutes Segment

On October 25, CBS aired thirteen minutes on third-country deportations. Within two weeks the segment had produced a fraud allegation the broadcast never mentioned, a longer interview released the next day, a standards file six decades deep, and a newsroom dispute reported in three outlets. The desk files the record whole: the segment, the policy underneath it, the dispute, and what could not be verified. Every claim is attributed.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0905 ET, Saturday, November 7, 2026.

The File in Brief

The segment is documented. The dispute about it is documented. The completeness of the record CBS released is not. The facts the desk can file with confidence are these. On Sunday, October 25, 2026, 60 Minutes aired “Destination: Nowhere,” reported by contributor Gianna Toboni, on the administration’s practice of deporting people to countries not their own. It reported about 25,000 such deportations, and it profiled Moussa Diawara, a Malian man of 57 with no criminal record, whom an immigration judge in 2012 ordered removed from the United States but protected from return to Mali. The broadcast said Ghana, which received him on July 31, forced him to Mali within five days. On the night of the broadcast, the Department of Homeland Security’s general counsel, James Percival, who had sat for the program’s interview, posted that the program had selectively edited him and that Diawara’s original asylum application had claimed persecution for his homosexuality, a fact the broadcast omitted. Fox News reported that DHS had emailed CBS about the claim on October 6, nineteen days before the broadcast. CBS News declined to comment. On October 26, CBS posted a 43-minute “Extended Interview” with Percival. Whether those 43 minutes are the whole interview is not established: Percival said the interview ran nearly an hour, and CBS has published no transcript and given no reason for the release. The policy underneath the segment is real and litigated: by the count of Human Rights Watch, from Immigration and Customs Enforcement data, 27,943 people were deported to third countries between January 20, 2025, and August 5, 2026, and the Supreme Court hears the governing case on December 9. What no source in the file establishes is the segment’s intent, the completeness of the released interview, and any adjudicated finding on the allegation at the dispute’s center. The report files the record in that order.

The Segment: What Was Broadcast

The program reported a practice, a flight, and a man. “Destination: Nowhere” opened with the scale of the second Trump administration’s removals: “Nearly one million people were deported from the U.S. during the first year and a half of President Trump’s second term… About 25,000, though, were sent somewhere else… They’re called third countries.” The broadcast said deportations of this kind “can be legal and past administrations have carried them out, but not at this scale,” and that receiving countries get “millions of dollars in aid or other benefits from the U.S.” in return. Its five-month investigation, it said, “uncovered cases in which people ended up in places where U.S. immigration judges said they would likely be persecuted or tortured.” The program obtained the names of all 63 people on the flight that left Texas on July 30, 2026, and reached 19 of those sent to Ghana; 17 of the 19 said Ghana quickly transported them against their will to countries that judges had ruled they should not be returned to. It aired video it described, through an eyewitness, as Ghanaian officials dragging a woman, Rabbiatu Kuyateh, described as a nurse who had lived in the United States for 30 years, to a flight home to a country where she held protection from return. An immigration attorney, Meredyth Yoon, said on camera that the United States had been warned of Ghana’s conduct in a lawsuit the prior year and sent ten more flights afterward. Ghana’s government declined to comment to the program. The segment’s own count of the 63 people on the flight found 19 percent had a criminal charge or conviction, for theft, assault, drugs, or weapons offenses; the rest had traffic violations or none.

The case at the segment’s center was Diawara’s, and the broadcast told it in full except for one part of his file. The program said he entered the United States 25 years earlier, overstayed a tourist visa, and “applied for refugee protection, arguing he would be persecuted if he were returned to Mali”; that in 2012 a judge ruled he could be deported but not to Mali, after which he held a work permit; that ICE agents waited at his Houston-suburb home after his Uber night shift on July 11, 2026; that after three weeks in detention in Texas, without speaking to his lawyer or a judge, he was flown out and landed in Ghana on July 31. There, he said, he and others were held in a hotel by armed guards and not allowed calls. “Within five days, Diawara says, Ghanaian officials forced him onto a flight to… Mali.” In Mali he was, in the broadcast’s words, “homeless and in hiding”; part of the reason he did not feel safe, he told the program, is that he had converted to Christianity, in a Muslim-majority nation with an Islamist insurgency. He said he was dropped with no money or identification, and that after three weeks, with the help of local truckers, he escaped to another African country he asked the program not to name. He showed a DHS medication document and baggage tags listing the destination as Mali; Percival, shown them on camera, answered, “Well, he wasn’t flown to Mali. He was flown to Ghana, but I can’t speak to why that piece of paper says that.” After the interview, DHS told the program the tags were made by contractors not involved in determining destinations and the medication form was a “clerical error”. The broadcast said his attorney had asked a federal judge in Washington to order his return. His wife Barbara and his children, Corey, 16, and Chloe, 12, appeared. The broadcast made no mention of any sexuality-based claim in his asylum history. It did say, in narration immediately before an interview clip: “DHS also questioned the credibility of Diawara’s arguments for fearing persecution in Mali.” The episode drew 5.64 million viewers.

The Interview: What Aired, What Did Not

In the broadcast, Percival defended the policy and declined its central number. His interview material is intercut through the thirteen-minute segment, in five short passages among field reporting and family scenes. He said: “I absolutely believe with all of my heart that these third country removals have stopped crimes from happening and kept Americans safer. And that is our fundamental obligation of the Department of Homeland Security.” He explained the practice’s two uses: a serious criminal whose country “just won’t take them back,” and a person granted country-specific relief, ordered to leave but not to the home country. Asked about Ghana sending deportees onward, he said, “I’m not aware of any such cases,” and offered that deportees might have consented or that the foreign government had applied its own procedures consistent with international law. Asked what percentage of the detained and deported are violent offenders, he said, “I don’t have that figure off the top of my head.” The broadcast’s closing narration carried his position that the administration is “following the law that Congress has passed.”

The longer interview CBS posted the next day contains four exchanges the broadcast did not carry. First, a challenge on the onward returns: Percival asked, “Are you suggesting that when these people went back to their home countries that they were persecuted or tortured?” Toboni cited the judges’ standard and said some deportees had faced violence, but that actual persecution was “not her claim”; Percival answered that the ultimate question is whether anyone is being sent somewhere they will actually be persecuted or tortured, “and it doesn’t sound like you are making that claim.” Second, a statistics exchange: Toboni cited an internal DHS document under which, in the administration’s first year, 0.5 percent of the deported were accused or convicted of homicide and 1.4 percent of sexual assault. Percival answered: “Isn’t it wonderful that we are getting those people out of the country, should that be the limit of who has to follow the law? Just rapist and murderers have to follow the law?” Third, on compassion for nonviolent people separated from families, he said the people he has “the most compassion for is the American victims who we’re protecting,” and that “justice is about doing things that might be unpleasant to protect people”. Fourth, asked again for the violent-offender percentage, he gave the explanation the broadcast omitted: “there’s a very specific reason why this population is disproportionately criminal. Literally two reasons. Number one, when countries refuse to accept their nationals back, they disproportionately do that for violent criminals. People don’t really want to take their violent criminals back.” His second reason concerned withholding of removal, the country-specific protection Diawara held. In the circulated clips of that exchange, he cited no agency figure, and repeated that he did not have the percentage.

The release itself is part of the record, and its most repeated description is wrong. CBS titled the October 26 posting an “Extended Interview” and published it under its 60 Minutes Overtime brand, on CBS.com and YouTube; the podcast listing runs 43 minutes 12 seconds. CBS did not call it full, raw, or unedited in any text the desk found. The words “FULL, uncut” are the Department of Homeland Security’s, from its October 26 post: “WATCH WHAT @60Minutes DIDN’T SHOW YOU. Here’s the FULL, uncut interview.” Percival, on the night of the broadcast, had said of CBS that “they did promise they would publish the entire interview, so I look forward to my comments being posted in full this week,” and that he had spoken with the program for “nearly an hour.” His own excerpt clips followed on October 27, after the posting. No statement by CBS giving a reason for the release was found in any source. Whether the 43 minutes are the complete interview cannot be confirmed from the public record, and no transcript of the extended interview has been published.

The Omission: What Each Side Has Put on the Record

The administration’s allegation was made in public, on the night of the broadcast. Percival posted on October 25 that CBS had made Diawara the “poster child for what it claims are wrongful deportations,” and added: “His claimed reason for fearing Mali was that he is ‘gay’, just in case you’re wondering how bad the immigration fraud problem is.” DHS posted that Diawara “claimed on an asylum application that he would be persecuted for his homosexuality if he was returned to his home country, despite being married to a woman he has two children with,” and that 60 Minutes “left this absurd bit of fraud out of their ‘Texas dad’ segment”. ICE posted on October 26: “Fake & Gay!” Fox News reported on October 26 and 29 that it had obtained Diawara’s asylum application, filed in 2009, in which he wrote that he had “experienced extreme punishment and persecution based on the ground of my sexual orientation”. Some secondary accounts give the application’s year as 2012; 2012 is the year of the removal order and the grant of protection against return to Mali, which DHS’s own posts date to February 2, 2012. No adjudicated finding that the application was fraudulent appears in any source the desk reviewed. Asylum applications are confidential under federal regulation, 8 C.F.R. §208.6; an immigration-law commentary flagged that the application’s disclosure itself raises a confidentiality question. No statement by Diawara or his counsel responding to the allegation was found.

The warning, on the reported evidence, was an email dated October 6. A DHS spokesperson told the New York Post on October 27 that CBS had been informed “two weeks before” the segment aired. Fox News and the Post reported that they obtained a DHS email to CBS dated October 6, 2026, nineteen days before the broadcast, stating that Diawara, “who is married to a woman he shares two children with, oddly claimed he would be persecuted for homosexuality if returned home to Mali”. A network source told the Post the email did not explicitly allege fraud. CBS has neither confirmed nor denied receiving it; the network declined to comment to Fox News on October 29 and to the Post on October 30. The only defense of the broadcast on the record is anonymous: a “60 Minutes insider” told Fox News Digital that the broadcast had acknowledged DHS questioning the credibility of Diawara’s fear of persecution, which the transcript confirms it did, in the narration quoted above.

Why the material was omitted is the subject of two attributed accounts that do not fully agree. The Post reported on October 30 that, according to its sources, the segment intentionally left out the claim “to protect him”; that producer Andy Court had made an agreement with Diawara’s lawyer not to mention it and had not briefed 60 Minutes executive producer Nick Bilton or CBS News editor-in-chief Bari Weiss, who “went ballistic” on learning of it; that Bilton met with Toboni and Court on October 29; and that the network was weighing disciplinary action against Court, up to firing. Status News reported on November 6 that Toboni’s team made a deliberate decision to exclude the claim, fearing that publicizing it could expose Diawara to further persecution in Mali, where homosexuality is criminalized, and that Bilton and a senior lieutenant were at least partially informed, which partly contradicts the Post’s account; Status also reported staff accusing network leadership of throwing its journalists “under the bus”. CBS declined to comment for both stories. At the Information’s WTF Summit on October 29, Weiss asked critics, per the Post, to “judge us by our journalism.” No on-the-record statement by Weiss about this segment was found.

The Policy Underneath: Third Countries, in the Record

The practice rests on a statute, two guidance documents, and a case the Supreme Court hears in December. The Immigration and Nationality Act, §241(b), codified at 8 U.S.C. §1231(b), permits removal to a country that will accept the person where removal to the designated country is impracticable, inadvisable, or impossible; withholding of removal and protection under the Convention Against Torture bar removal to the specified country and do not by their terms bar removal to a third country. ICE guidance of February 18, 2025, directed review of withholding and torture-protection cases to determine the viability of third-country removal. Secretary Kristi Noem’s guidance of March 30, 2025, reaffirmed July 9, 2025, provides that where the receiving country has given diplomatic assurances, judged credible by the State Department, that removed people will not be persecuted or tortured, the person “may be removed without the need for further procedures”; otherwise the government must give notice of the country and, if the person states a fear, screen the claim as to that country.

The documented scale exceeds the broadcast’s figure, and most of it is not Africa. Human Rights Watch, analyzing ICE data obtained by the Deportation Data Project under the Freedom of Information Act, counted 27,943 third-country deportations from January 20, 2025, to August 5, 2026, against 8,563 in the comparable prior period, and 46,563 third-country removal orders through August 31, 2026. A tracker count put roughly 22,000 people sent to at least 26 countries since January 2025, about 19,000 of them to Mexico. The documented receiving countries include Panama, Costa Rica, Guatemala, Honduras, Rwanda, Uganda, Liberia, Cabo Verde, Cameroon, the Central African Republic, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Equatorial Guinea, and Sierra Leone. El Salvador’s CECOT arrangement of March 2025 proceeded under the Alien Enemies Act proclamation, a distinct authority from §241(b). In Africa: South Sudan received a first cohort of eight men in July 2025; Eswatini had received about 30 people by mid-2026 under a deal whose cap and payment figures conflict between sources; Rwanda agreed to take up to 250 people for $7.5 million, the first seven arriving in August 2025. Ghana’s agreement, announced by President John Mahama on September 10–11, 2025, accepted West African nationals only, under the ECOWAS free-movement protocol; Mahama said the first 14 arrivals, mostly Nigerians and one Gambian, were facilitated onward, the Nigerians by bus to Nigeria, and that no money was exchanged. Foreign Minister Samuel Okudzeto Ablakwa defended the arrangement as humanitarian and pan-African; the parliamentary minority argued it required ratification under Article 75 of Ghana’s constitution. Human Rights Watch reported that Ghana later returned several of the September 2025 group to their home countries. For the July 2026 flight at the segment’s center, Ghana’s government declined to comment, and no Ghanaian statement about it was found.

The courts have ruled in both directions, and the last word is scheduled. In D.V.D. v. DHS, Judge Brian Murphy of the District of Massachusetts issued a preliminary injunction on April 18, 2025, requiring written notice in a language the person understands, a meaningful opportunity to raise a fear claim, and, if no reasonable fear is found, a minimum of 15 days to move to reopen, before any third-country removal; he found in May 2025 that the department had violated the injunction in an attempted removal to South Sudan on about 24 hours’ notice. The Supreme Court stayed the injunction on June 23, 2025, over a dissent by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson, allowing the removals to proceed while the case continued. On September 18, 2026, the First Circuit affirmed in large part a judgment requiring the government to seek removal first to designated or home countries, to give effective notice of the specified third country, and to allow a meaningful opportunity to contest on reasonable-fear grounds, and vacating the guidance; the Supreme Court stayed that judgment as well and granted review in DHS v. D.V.D., No. 26-426, with argument set for December 9, 2026. The broadcast’s summary of this posture, that an appeals court had found the policy unlawful and the Supreme Court had allowed the deportations to continue until it hears the case in December, is consistent with the record.

The Argument: Who Said What, and When

The administration’s case against the segment was made by its principals, in sequence. Percival’s posts of October 25–27 charged selective editing and the omitted asylum claim, and promoted the exchanges in §2 above: “You can kind of see why @60Minutes left stuff like this on the cutting room floor.” White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis told the Daily Caller on October 27 that the program had engaged in “selective editing” and “failed to include key information about how third country removals are essential to the safety of the American people”; that “these removals allow us to remove some of the most barbaric criminals, including child rapists and murderers whose home countries refuse to take them back”; and that the program chose to “peddle false sob stories of illegal aliens.” Larry O’Connor, on his Townhall program on October 28, played the omitted disproportionately-criminal explanation, called it “clear, concise, cohesive,” and argued the program cut it because it was a logical defense of the policy. The Federation for American Immigration Reform argued on October 28 that the segment minimized the deportees’ criminal records, noted the broadcast’s own 19 percent figure, and asserted that every person on the flights had been offered the chance to self-deport, with payment, to a country of their own choosing, and declined. Rich Lowry of National Review published a video analysis on November 2, “What 60 Minutes Didn’t Tell Viewers About Trump’s Deportations,” examining the full interview and the Diawara case; its description says Lowry questions whether CBS gave viewers the complete story. No transcript of the video was retrievable, and the desk asserts nothing of Lowry’s spoken argument beyond its published description and chapter titles.

The calls for consequences, and the defenses, are both thinner than the volume suggests. No public call by a named official or major outlet to cancel or end 60 Minutes over this segment was found. The documented prescriptions are these. Stephen Miller, the White House deputy chief of staff, said on Fox News on December 23, 2025, of the producers of the earlier, shelved CECOT segment: “Every one of those producers at ‘60 Minutes’ engaged in this revolt, fire them. Clean house.” The Post reported the network weighing discipline or firing for the Diawara segment’s producer. Aaron Ackerman, writing October 29, prescribed process: “Release the interviews. Explain consequential edits. Correct errors prominently.” Two process rules from earlier disputes are already in force at the network: release of transcripts of interviews with presidential candidates, adopted in the 2025 settlement described below, and the live or live-to-tape-only rule Face the Nation adopted in September 2025. On the other side, no named press critic was found on the record calling this segment sound journalism in full, and none calling for its retraction; the documented defense is the anonymous insider’s point that the broadcast acknowledged the credibility challenge, the safety rationale in the Post and Status accounts, and Sharyn Alfonsi’s December 2025 defense of her own segment, in an internal email, as a vetted story that should not be held for government non-participation.

The Standards: The Yardsticks on the Record

CBS has stated its editing standard twice in two years, in its own defense. On October 20, 2024, answering the charge that it had deceitfully edited an interview with Vice President Kamala Harris, 60 Minutes said the accusation “is false”: “Same question. Same answer. But a different portion of the response… When we edit any interview, whether a politician, an athlete, or movie star, we strive to be clear, accurate and on point.” On February 5, 2025, publishing the Harris transcripts and video it had given the Federal Communications Commission, it said the material showed the broadcast “was not doctored or deceitful. In reporting the news, journalists regularly edit interviews, for time, space, or clarity. In making these edits, 60 Minutes is always guided by the truth and what we believe will be most informative to the viewing public.” CBS News has maintained written standards since they were first compiled under Richard Salant in 1976; the 1999 edition’s introduction states that most of them “come down to two essential principles: accuracy and fairness.” The current handbook is not published. In September 2025, after Secretary Noem accused CBS of editing her Face the Nation interview, the network first said the edit “met all CBS News standards,” then, on September 5, changed the program’s policy to live or live-to-tape interviews only, citing audience feedback and “greater transparency in our interviews.”

The profession’s codes state the compression rule in one sentence each. The Society of Professional Journalists’ Code of Ethics, revised September 6, 2014, instructs journalists to “provide context”, to “take special care not to misrepresent or oversimplify in promoting, previewing or summarizing a story,” and to “never deliberately distort facts or context, including visual information”; the code states of itself that it is not legally enforceable. The Radio Television Digital News Association’s code holds that journalism “verifies, provides relevant context, tells the rest of the story and acknowledges the absence of important additional information,” and that responsible reporting “is clear about what it omits, as well as what it includes”. PBS’s editorial standards, a comparator broadcast standard, state that “editing should never alter the meaning of a person’s remarks,” permit condensing that fairly and accurately represents the remarks and their context, and prohibit placing a question next to a different answer. Joe Peyronnin, a television news executive of forty years at CBS, Fox, and Telemundo, stated the practitioner’s test on November 17, 2025: “whatever you edit has to fairly and accurately represent what she said, the point she was making”, and an editor may not join material in a way that can “change the meaning, or potentially change the meaning, or mislead the viewer.”

The adjudicated precedents cut in more than one direction. In the Harris matter, the Center for American Rights filed an FCC news-distortion complaint on October 16, 2024. Chair Jessica Rosenworcel dismissed it on January 16, 2025, as failing to allege intentional falsification as against protected editorial judgment; Chair Brendan Carr reinstated it on January 22, 2025, demanded the full unedited transcript and camera feeds, and the FCC published them on February 5, 2025. The full record showed that both aired excerpts came from the same single answer, the first half in a preview and the second half on the broadcast; no words were fabricated. No FCC finding of news distortion against CBS was issued. President Trump’s civil suit over the interview was settled by Paramount on July 1–2, 2025, for $16 million, paid to his future presidential library and to fees and costs, with no apology, and with 60 Minutes agreeing to release transcripts of future interviews with presidential candidates. The FCC approved the Skydance–Paramount merger on July 24, 2025. In the network’s older precedent, the Benjamin Report of July 1982, CBS’s own internal review of the documentary “The Uncounted Enemy,” found violations of CBS News standards, including editing in which interviewees appeared to answer different questions than those asked, and concluded the execution was seriously flawed on fairness, accuracy, and balance; the related libel suit was withdrawn in 1985. In November 2025, Peyronnin distinguished the BBC’s splicing of two parts of a Trump speech recorded some fifty minutes apart, which he called misleading, from the CBS Harris edit, which he treated as within the fair-representation rule.

The Institution: What CBS and 60 Minutes Say They Are

The network’s stated mission is empowerment through understanding, under standards it says it pioneered. CBS News’s publishing principles state: “At CBS News and Stations, we are dedicated to providing the highest quality journalism under standards we pioneered and continue to set today. Our mission is to empower Americans across all ages and demographics to understand the stories and issues that shape the world around us.” On politics, the same page promises to “hold your elected officials accountable through fair, unbiased and fact-based reporting.” The CBS Evening News announced five principles of its own on January 2, 2026, including “We work for you… Not our advertisers. Not politicians. Not corporate interests, including the corporate owners of CBS News,” and “We report on the world as it is… We’ll admit when we get it wrong.” The two other networks state their missions in their own documents. Disney’s journalistic-integrity statement commits ABC News to independence of editorial decision-making from the company’s commercial and political interests, to fairness and integrity, and to approaching the work “with rigor, to question thoroughly and skeptically, to analyze deeply.” ABC News president Almin Karamehmedovic told his staff in March 2025, in a memo reported by Deadline: “our mission is simple: straightforward journalism.” No standalone public NBC News handbook was found; its president, Noah Oppenheim, told Poynter in 2022: “Our mission is to illuminate and not advocate.”

The program’s founding premise, in its founder’s words, was storytelling. Don Hewitt created 60 Minutes, which premiered on September 24, 1968, as a CBS News production; it remains one. Hewitt’s documented framing of the secret was four words: “Tell me a story.” His 2001 memoir carries the title. He sought a program that “packaged reality as attractively as Hollywood packages fiction,” a television version of Life magazine mixing hard and soft news: “We could look into Marilyn Monroe’s closet so long as we looked into Robert Oppenheimer’s laboratory, too.” The first episode framed it as “a kind of a magazine for television.” Jeff Fager, a later executive producer, described the format in 2018 as combining Murrow’s See It Now and Person to Person, “hard-hitting journalism and entertaining profiles”, adding: “You start with fairness.” Hewitt’s own pitch criteria, as summarized in reference works, required that stories tell the audience something new, have a national dimension, “run their course in thirteen minutes or less, and have an up-front punch to garner an emotional response.” CBS’s current boilerplate calls the program “the most successful television broadcast in history,” offering “hard-hitting investigative reports, interviews, feature segments and profiles of people in the news,” regularly in Nielsen’s Top 10. Its published awards count, from its 2019–20 boilerplate, is 150 Emmy Awards, 25 Peabody Awards, and 20 duPont-Columbia awards, the most won by any single news program; no updated official count was found. Retired correspondent Steve Kroft said in June 2026: “It’s the highest-rated news program on television, and it has been that way for more than 50 years.”

The tension between the two halves of that founding formula has a documented history inside the institution. In his 1998 Gannett Lecture, Hewitt criticized the imitator newsmagazines his program’s success had spawned: “60 Minutes showed [executives] that a television newsmagazine can be worthwhile and profitable at the same time. The trouble is, they ignored the first half of that equation and zeroed in on the second”; and: “News is news and entertainment is entertainment, and crossing the line between them is often dishonest and always bad broadcasting.” The Wigand episode is the documented case of commercial pressure on the program. In November 1995, CBS aired its tobacco segment without the key interview with Brown & Williamson research chief Jeffrey Wigand, citing lawyers’ fear of a multibillion-dollar suit for inducing breach of his confidentiality agreement, while CBS’s owner was completing the $5.4 billion sale of the network to Westinghouse. The full interview aired on February 4, 1996. Fager, in 2016, called the decision to withhold it “a low point in our history,” and the story “probably the most important story that was ever reported by ‘60 Minutes.’” The program’s sibling in format, CBS Sunday Morning, is likewise a CBS News production, a newsmagazine aired since January 28, 1979, and conceived, per its reference record, as a broadcast version of a Sunday newspaper magazine supplement.

The Earlier Episode: “Inside CECOT,” December 2025

Eleven months before the Diawara segment, the same newsroom pulled a deportation segment hours before air, and the dispute became public in the correspondent’s own email. Sharyn Alfonsi’s report on Venezuelan men deported to El Salvador’s CECOT prison, alleging torture and abuse, was scheduled for December 21, 2025. CBS announced some two to three hours before broadcast that it would air in a future broadcast. Weiss, editor-in-chief since October 2025, said the decision was hers; at the December 22 editorial call she said the story did not “advance the ball,” that the New York Times and others had done similar work, and that it lacked a sufficient on-camera administration response. Her statement: “Holding stories that aren’t ready for whatever reason, that they lack sufficient context, say, or that they are missing critical voices, happens every day in every newsroom. I look forward to airing this important piece when it’s ready.” Alfonsi’s internal email, which leaked, said the story “was screened five times and cleared by both CBS attorneys and Standards and Practices. It is factually correct”; that interview requests had gone to the White House, the State Department, and DHS; that “government silence is a statement, not a VETO”; and that pulling it after every internal check had been met “is not an editorial decision, it is a political one.” On December 22, the episode as fed to the Canadian broadcaster Global TV appeared online and spread; CBS issued takedown notices. The segment aired on January 18, 2026, with its body unchanged and a new introduction and conclusion by Alfonsi incorporating written White House and DHS statements; Los Angeles Times reporting put the added material at about three and a half minutes, with no new interviews. Alfonsi’s contract later expired and was not renewed, in May 2026. In May and June 2026, Weiss replaced executive producer Tanya Simon with Bilton, and CBS parted with correspondents including Scott Pelley, who told the New York Times in June that CBS had bent to Trump and that Weiss “must go”; retired correspondent Kroft told Variety the same month that of Weiss, “everything she’s touched has turned to s***,” and that “60 Minutes, as the audience has known it, no longer exists.”

The Family Question: What the Law and the Studies Show

One premise of the broadcast’s framing, that deportation separates this father from his family, was not examined by any participant in the segment, the interview, or the dispute, and the desk found no participant or commentator in the record who raised the alternative. The documented law is as follows. A removal order runs against the removed individual only; removal proceedings are civil, not criminal, under INS v. Lopez-Mendoza, 468 U.S. 1032 (1984). United States citizens cannot themselves be deported; if citizen family members travel with a removed parent, they are in law leaving voluntarily, and parents may instead arrange care for them in the United States. The department’s own stated position in 2025 citizen-child cases is the same choice framing: Assistant Secretary Tricia McLaughlin said ICE asks parents whether they want their children removed with them or placed with a person the parent designates. A citizen departs and enters on a valid United States passport under 8 U.S.C. §1185(b), a documentation rule; relocation abroad does not strip citizenship, and the right to return is unaffected. No provision of United States law barring Diawara’s wife or children from leaving, or from later returning, was found. The broadcast stated that his wife and children are United States citizens; secondary coverage names his wife as Barbara Johnson, a registered nurse. No court filing stating the wife’s citizenship was found.

The other side of the question is also documented, in scholarship, in advocacy, and in dissent. Scholars use the term “de facto deportation” for emigration to accompany a deported family member. Hamilton, Masferrer, and co-authors, in the Russell Sage Foundation Journal of the Social Sciences in November 2025, estimated from Mexican census data that more than 11,000 people were de facto deported from the United States to Mexico between 2015 and 2020, including more than 6,000 United States citizens, almost 90 percent of them children. In survey data from 2015 to 2019, of deported Mexican parents separated from minor children, 39 percent planned to remain separated, 20 percent planned to reunify in the United States, and 11 percent planned to reunify in Mexico. Texas Civil Rights Project attorney Daniel Hatoum described the choice as presented to families: “Either take them with you or we’re going to separate them quickly from you”. Columbia Law professor Elora Mukherjee called the use of separation threats “calculated cruelty,” in Reuters reporting of December 11, 2025. In the case law, every federal appellate court to decide the question has rejected the claim that removing a parent unconstitutionally deports the citizen child: Perdido v. INS (5th Cir. 1969); Acosta v. Gaffney (3d Cir. 1977), reversing a district court that had held the infant’s de facto deportation “repugnant to the Constitution”, and holding that the parents make “the real choice of family residence” and that removal “merely postpone[s]” the child’s residence here; Ayala-Flores v. INS (6th Cir. 1981); and Oforji v. Ashcroft (7th Cir. 2003). The contrary position survives in Judge Harry Pregerson’s dissent in Memije v. Gonzales (9th Cir. 2007), speaking of “de facto expulsion from their native land,” and in Judge Richard Posner’s concurrence in Oforji, calling the children’s options “unappetizing choices… more excruciating the longer they remain here.” Congress provided a statutory channel instead of a constitutional bar: cancellation of removal under INA §240A(b) for “exceptional and extremely unusual hardship” to a citizen family member, under the standard of Matter of Monreal-Aguinaga (BIA 2001), which weighs schooling, health, and country conditions under both the stay and relocate scenarios, and holds adverse country conditions alone insufficient.

For this family, the destination’s documented record runs in two directions. Mali’s Act No. 04-058 of November 25, 2004, as summarized in Mali’s own report to the United Nations Human Rights Office, provides at Article 13 that a temporary residence permit must be issued automatically to a foreigner married at least three months to a Malian national, on production of the marriage certificate, and to a foreign child under 18, or a dependent, of a Malian national; a visa of up to one year, renewable, accompanies the authorization. On that text, Diawara’s wife and both minor children fall within the automatic-permit categories once in Mali. Against that text stand two documented facts of 2026. The State Department’s Mali advisory, issued July 2, 2026, is Level 4: “Do not travel to Mali for any reason. Due to safety risks, family members cannot join U.S. government employees who work in Mali.” And as of January 1, 2026, Mali suspended visa issuance to United States citizens, with limited exceptions, in reciprocity for United States restrictions on Malians; whether spouses and children of Malian nationals are within the exceptions is not documented in any source the desk reviewed, and whether the children hold or could claim Malian nationality by descent is likewise not documented. The advisory is a safety assessment, not a legal bar; Article 13 governs residence after entry, not entry itself. The file carries both, and no participant in the dispute addressed either.

The Numbers in Their Past: Deportation by Administration

The comparison most often drawn rests on three different counts, and the desk prints the counts separately. Under the Department of Homeland Security’s usage, a removal is a compulsory movement out of the country under an order of removal; a return is a confirmed departure without such an order; an expulsion is the separate Title 42 public-health category used from March 2020 to May 11, 2023. The broad totals in circulation are counts of events, not of unique people; repeat crossers are counted repeatedly, particularly under Title 42. On the department’s Yearbook Table 39, the Obama administration’s fiscal years 2009–2016 sum to 3,062,466 removals and 2,186,427 returns, a combined 5,248,893. The label applied to that record was coined by an advocate: Janet Murguía, president of the National Council of La Raza, said on March 4, 2014, “For us, this president has been the deporter-in-chief.” For the Biden administration, the Migration Policy Institute counted about 1.1 million removals and returns from fiscal 2021 through February 2024, plus most of roughly 3 million Title 42 expulsions, for “nearly 4.4 million” repatriations by early 2024; summing Table 39 across the full term, fiscal 2021–2024, gives 2,032,300 removals and returns plus 2,754,120 expulsions, about 4.79 million. For the first Trump administration, Table 39 sums to about 1,196,000 removals and 599,000 returns across fiscal 2017–2020, about 1.795 million, rising to about 2.0 million with fiscal 2020’s 206,770 Title 42 expulsions; the Migration Policy Institute’s like-for-like count was about 1.5 million, and Syracuse University’s TRAC put ICE deportations below 932,000. Three definitions produce three numbers, all documented. For the current term, the Institute estimated about 980,000 deportations, removals and returns, from January 20, 2025, to July 31, 2026, consistent with the broadcast’s “nearly one million,” and noted that levels remain well below those routinely observed in the early 2000s; the department’s higher public claims, of three million or more departures including claimed self-deportations, are not adopted by the Institute.

The earlier episodes of mass removal carry their own definitional warnings. The Palmer Raids of November 7, 1919, and January 2, 1920, produced arrest estimates from 3,000 to 10,000 by source; 556 resident aliens were ultimately deported, after Acting Labor Secretary Louis Post cancelled some 2,000 warrants. For the Mexican Repatriation of the 1930s, Balderrama and Rodríguez, in Decade of Betrayal, estimated about one million people of Mexican descent driven out and “approximately 60 percent of those leaving were legal American citizens”; no official count exists, estimates in the literature run to two million, and California apologized by statute in 2005. For Operation Wetback in 1954, the Immigration and Naturalization Service claimed 1.1 to 1.3 million departures; Britannica records the claimed 1,100,000 against most estimates of about 300,000; the claimed figure covered the entire fiscal year ending June 30, 1954, although the operation began on June 10, included repeat arrests, and consisted, at more than 97 percent, of returns without removal orders. The enforcement-priority question has its own dated record: Secretary Jeh Johnson’s memo of November 20, 2014, set three priority tiers; Secretary Alejandro Mayorkas’s memo of September 30, 2021, re-imposed priorities; Executive Order 14159 of January 20, 2025, revoked the Biden-era guidance and, per the Heritage Foundation’s analysis of November 21, 2025, “mandated enforcement against all inadmissible and removable aliens.” The same Heritage analysis notes that ICE’s fiscal 2026 budget justification reintroduces priority language nearly identical to the rescinded Mayorkas memo.
