Thanksgiving Prep: The Six Families, Filed From the Record

Objective

Which Six Families Run America, According to Conspiracy Theories

The question presupposes a fixed six. This report files what the record actually holds: the versions of the claim in circulation, which name five families, seven, eight, or thirteen, and agree on neither the number nor the full set of names; the documented history of the names in the common pool, kept separate from what the theories allege; the published wealth rankings; the state of the evidence, stated once; and, against the secret lists, the open ones—the published rosters and invitation lists of the country’s documented networks, whose concentration is a matter of record and whose influence the record shows to be emergent. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1400 ET, Wednesday, November 25, 2026.

Asked which six families run America according to conspiracy theories, the record gives an answer the question does not expect: there is no list of six. The versions of the claim in circulation name five families, or seven, or eight, or thirteen, and the names rotate between versions. What the record holds is a common pool of names, a set of published lists that disagree with one another, and a documented history—in banking, oil, chemicals, retail, and politics—for every name in the pool. This report files the lists, the pool, and the documented record, in that order.

The Lists in Circulation: Five, Seven, Eight, Thirteen

The five-name version is the most widely republished. The Sarajevo Times, under the headline “Which five Families have been ruling the World for Centuries?”, names the Rothschild dynasty, the Rockefeller dynasty, the Morgan family, the Du Ponts, and the Bush family, and describes the five in its own text as “the most powerful, the wealthiest, and the scapegoats for all theories of conspiracy theorists,” the ones who “tailor the global policy.” A second Sarajevo Times piece asks whether the world “is ruled by Five Families who possess half of the Planet’s Wealth” and names the same five. The FYI vertical at Vocal publishes the same five under the title “The 5 Most Powerful Families Allegedly Controlling the World,” with a caution of its own: “Most of these claims remain unproven and heavily debated.”

The seven-name version circulates on social platforms. A carousel in wide circulation in 2026, titled “7 Families That Secretly Control the World Behind the Scenes for Over 200 Years,” names the Rothschilds, the Rockefellers, the Morgans, the Du Ponts, the Waltons, the Koch family, and the Al Saud family. The eight-name version, in a social-media list circulating in November 2026 under the headline that the world is not run by eight billion people but by eight families, names the Rothschild, Rockefeller, Morgan, Du Pont, Bush, Walton, and Windsor families and the Al Saud family.

The thirteen-name version has a book behind it. Fritz Springmeier’s Bloodlines of the Illuminati, self-published in 1995, names thirteen top bloodlines: Astor, Bundy, Collins, DuPont, Freeman, Kennedy, Li, Onassis, Rockefeller, Rothschild, Russell, and Van Duyn, with the remaining place given in the book’s volumes to a Merovingian line of European royal houses; other versions of the list substitute Reynolds. A Scribd explainer, “Illuminati’s 13 Bloodline Families Explained,” reproduces the thirteen and carries a critical assessment of its own: the theory relies on speculative rather than verifiable claims and lacks substantial documented evidence.

No filed version names six. The number in the question sits between the five of the newspaper lists and the seven of the social lists, and no publisher in the record occupies it.

The Common Pool: The Documented Record, Name by Name

Five names appear in nearly every version. For each, a documented record exists, and it is separable from the allegation. The desk files them separately.

The Rothschild family is a European banking dynasty founded by Mayer Amschel Rothschild (1744–1812) of Frankfurt, who placed his five sons in five financial centers—Frankfurt, Vienna, London, Naples, and Paris. The theories allege that the family controls central banks, the Federal Reserve among them, and global finance. The claims about this family are the oldest in the pool; the fact-check record notes their long association with the antisemitic claim that Jews control the world’s money supply.

John D. Rockefeller founded the Standard Oil Company in Ohio in 1870. At its height the company controlled some 90 percent of the United States’ oil refineries and pipelines; the Supreme Court ordered its breakup in 1911. The family’s philanthropic foundations are a matter of public record and public filing. The theories allege that the family continues to rule through its foundations, its banks, and the successor oil companies.

John Pierpont Morgan, the banker at the head of J.P. Morgan and Company, twice acted in a national financial emergency: in the gold crisis of the 1890s his syndicate supplied gold to the United States Treasury, and in 1907 he organized the New York bankers who stopped the panic of that year. The theories allege that the Morgans forced American entry into the First World War to protect the loans they had made to France and Russia.

Éleuthère Irénée du Pont, a French immigrant, founded a gunpowder works in Delaware in 1802. By the beginning of the First World War the company produced 40 percent of the world’s explosives; in the Second World War it took part in the construction of the American atomic bomb. The company became the DuPont chemical company. The theories allege military and industrial control exercised through it.

The Bush family is a political dynasty that gave the United States two presidents, George H. W. Bush and George W. Bush. The theories allege a hidden hand running forward from Prescott Bush, the first President Bush’s father, through the family’s place in banking and oil.

The remaining places rotate. The Astor family—John Jacob Astor, who arrived from Germany in 1783, made a fortune in the fur trade and in Manhattan real estate, and became America’s first multimillionaire—appears in the thirteen. The Kennedy family, the political dynasty of President John F. Kennedy, appears in the thirteen. The Walton family appears in the seven and the eight, and it is the pool’s one name drawn from the current documented wealth rankings: Sam Walton founded Walmart in 1962, and the family’s fortune, estimated at $432.4 billion in December 2024, is the largest documented family fortune in the world. The Windsor family, the British royal house, appears in the eight and in some versions of the thirteen. The Koch family and the Al Saud family appear in the seven and the eight.

The Documented Wealth Rankings

A documented ranking exists, and it is not the theory pool. The “List of Wealthiest Families,” compiled from Forbes and other business magazines and excluding royal and ruling dynasties, records that as of July 2026 the world’s twelve richest families each hold more than $100 billion and the top twenty-five control approximately $3.4 trillion. The documented list is headed by the Waltons, followed by the Ellison and Arnault families—names the conspiracy lists do not carry. The documented fortunes are corporate and disclosed, held in named companies whose filings are public, and they are measured in hundreds of billions. No name from the theory pool’s five constants stands at the head of the documented list.

The State of the Evidence

The state of the evidence is this. No document, filing, court record, or investigation in the record establishes that any group of families secretly directs the United States government or the world. The lists’ own publishers concede the point in their own pages: the Vocal list carries the note that its claims remain unproven and heavily debated, and the Scribd explainer of the thirteen bloodlines records that the theory relies on speculative rather than verifiable claims and lacks substantial documented evidence. The largest specific claim in the literature—that the Rothschild family holds $500 trillion, or 80 percent of the world’s wealth—was rated false by Snopes, against the benchmark that the world’s total wealth was estimated at $250 trillion in 2015.

The Open Network: The Documented Counterweight

Against the secret lists, the record also holds open ones. Renaissance Weekend, founded in 1981 on Hilton Head Island, South Carolina, by Linda LeSourd Lader and Philip Lader, the former United States ambassador to the Court of St. James’s, is an invitation-only, nonpartisan gathering of leaders in business and finance, government, the media, religion, medicine, science, technology, and the arts, organized by the nonprofit Renaissance Institute. Sixty families attended the first gathering. By the late 1990s, by Philip Lader’s own account at a White House briefing, some 1,100 families took part across five weekends in a year, with about 500 families, some 1,500 people, at the New Year’s gathering alone; more than 185 weekends have been held since. Counting by attendance across the four decades, the families associated with the gathering number roughly 3,000—an estimate, since the gathering publishes no roster; attendance is by invitation and nomination, not membership, and its discussions are off the record. The institution, its founders, and its scale are public record.

The other networks publish their names. The Council on Foreign Relations, founded in 1921, posts its full membership roster on its own website, searchable by any reader; the roster returns 5,476 names. The Rhodes Trust selects more than 100 scholars a year from 24 constituencies and publishes them. The MacArthur Foundation has named 1,195 fellows since 1981 and publishes every one. The Foreign Service fills published postings; the universities keep published alumni registers.

The same names recur across the published lists. A reader who lays the Council’s roster beside the Rhodes classes, the MacArthur fellows, the Foreign Service, the alumni registers of the Ivy League universities, and the attendance of the invitation gatherings finds heavy overlap: the families of the published networks are, in large part, the same families, two to ten members at a time, generation over generation. What the overlap documents is concentration—of money, schooling, credentials, acquaintance, and ambition—in a few thousand families. What it does not document is direction. No filing in the record shows the published networks acting as one body, under one instruction, toward one end. The influence the record supports is emergent: it arises from the overlap itself, without a director.

The Mechanism: Mass, Momentum, and the Rooster

The record also supplies the mechanism by which concentration reads as control. An institution or a family with more mass—more money, more graduates, more members in more rooms—produces larger effects by doing ordinary things: bigger splashes, bigger patterns, bigger blast radii. Stanford University is not directing the world; but the people who pass through it carry its force, momentum, and ambition into every institution they join, and from outside, the pattern of their arrivals and effects looks like a plan. Outcomes that follow concentrated mass and momentum are read backward as intention and control. The inference is the step the record does not support: sequence and scale are not direction.

The mechanism has a folk statement, in a line from the television series The Unit: “The cock crows and the sun comes up, but the crowing of the cock does not make the sun come up.”

Why the Lists Persist

Jesse Walker, author of The United States of Paranoia, put the cultural record in one sentence in a PBS News conversation with Judy Woodruff: “It tells us something true about the anxieties and the experiences of the people who believe it, even if it says absolutely nothing true about subjects of the theory themselves.” The lists’ own instability is part of that record. A claim that cannot settle on five, seven, eight, or thirteen names, or on the same names twice, is not a census of rulers. It is a standing measure of how the culture imagines power—concentrated, hereditary, and hidden—at a moment when the documented networks of influence are published, searchable, and open to inspection.

The Record as It Stands

The record, as filed, is this. The conspiracy lists name five families, seven, eight, or thirteen—never six—and the names rotate between versions; their five constant names are Rothschild, Rockefeller, Morgan, Du Pont, and Bush. Every name in the pool has a documented history, and the documented history is separable from the allegation in every case. The documented wealth rankings are headed by families the lists mostly ignore. No evidence in the record establishes secret control by any group of families. The networks that demonstrably concentrate influence in the United States publish their rosters, and the influence the record supports is emergent—mass and momentum, not direction. The desk draws no conclusion from the record. The record is printed whole.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
