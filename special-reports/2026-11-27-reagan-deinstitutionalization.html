The Emptying, Filed From the Record

Objective

The Emptying of the Asylums and the Two Memories of Ronald Reagan

Filed from the record: the releases from the state hospitals and the two verdicts filed over them—liberation in the civil-liberties record, abandonment in the homelessness record—carried on one chronology: the census peak of 1955, the California law of 1967 and its bipartisan authors, the court holdings of 1975 and 1999, the funding act of 1980 and its repeal in 1981, St. Elizabeths from 8,000 patients to the 1984 transfer, the street counts, the McKinney Act of 1987, and the prison census on the other side of the seesaw. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0900 ET, Friday, November 27, 2026.

The emptying of the American state psychiatric hospitals is filed in the public record under two verdicts, and the verdicts do not share a page. In one filing, the releases were a liberation: the restoration of legal personhood to people held indefinitely without crime, charge, or sentence. In the other, the releases were an abandonment: the discharge of people with severe mental illness into communities whose promised services were never built, from which a share of them reached the shelters, the streets, and the jails. Both filings cite the same statutes, the same court decisions, and the same census. This report files the chronology the two verdicts share—the laws, the cases, the money, and the counts—in the order it ran. A companion report filed today, The Bed Census, prints the bed and prison counts in full; this report is confined to the releases and to what the record says followed them.

The Chronology Before the Name

The name attached to the emptying in public memory is Ronald Reagan’s. The chronology is longer than the name. The state hospital census peaked in 1955 at 558,922 patients, 337 beds per 100,000 Americans. Chlorpromazine, marketed as Thorazine, was approved in 1954—the first drug that quieted psychosis enough for some patients to live outside locked wards—and the census fell from the peak onward. California’s twelve state hospitals held 37,489 people at their 1959 peak; by 1966, under Governor Edmund G. Brown, the census stood at a little more than 26,000. The fall was under way before Reagan held any office.

The federal architecture of the replacement was drawn in the same years. President Kennedy signed the Community Mental Health Act on October 31, 1963, planning 1,500 community mental health centers to receive the discharged; about half were built, and those were underfunded. Medicaid, created in 1965, barred federal payment for adults in state psychiatric hospitals—the Institutions for Mental Diseases exclusion—giving every state a standing financial reason to move patients out of its own hospitals and into settings the federal government would help pay for. The hospitals emptied into a system that existed at half scale.

The California Law

The statute that set the national pattern was California’s. The Lanterman-Petris-Short Act was co-authored by Assemblyman Frank D. Lanterman, a Republican, and Senators Nicholas C. Petris and Alan Short, Democrats, and signed into law in 1967 by Governor Ronald Reagan; it went into full effect on July 1, 1972. Its first stated intent, printed in the statute, is “to end the inappropriate, indefinite, and involuntary commitment of mentally disordered persons” and “to eliminate legal disabilities.” In place of indefinite commitment it set graduated holds—72 hours for evaluation, fourteen days on certification—and a conservatorship capped at one year, renewable only through the courts.

The Act’s authors called it the Magna Carta for state hospital patients. The coalition behind it joined the two ends of the spectrum: civil libertarians, who held that no one should be involuntarily hospitalized, and fiscal conservatives, who counted the cost of the hospitals—a pairing E. Fuller Torrey, later the leading voice of the opposite filing, described as “a very odd coalition.” The sequel is also on the record: Senator Short was the first of the three authors to denounce the legislation that bore his name, and Lanterman and Petris in later years acknowledged its flaws while defending its intent.

The Courts

The federal courts wrote the same principle into constitutional law. In O’Connor v. Donaldson, 422 U.S. 563 (1975), the Supreme Court considered Kenneth Donaldson, held in a Florida state hospital for nearly fifteen years. The Court held that “a State cannot constitutionally confine without more a nondangerous individual who is capable of surviving safely in freedom by himself or with the help of willing and responsible family members or friends.” In Addington v. Texas (1979), the Court required proof by clear and convincing evidence before a person may be committed. In Olmstead v. L.C., 527 U.S. 581 (1999), the Court held that “unjustified isolation” of people with disabilities in institutions is discrimination under the Americans with Disabilities Act, and that states must provide community-based services when their own treatment professionals find community placement appropriate, the person does not oppose it, and the placement can be reasonably accommodated. The liberation filing rests on these texts, and the texts are not in dispute.

The Money

The funding half of the record runs the other way. President Carter created the President’s Commission on Mental Health in February 1977, with Rosalynn Carter as honorary chair; its 1978 report became the basis of the Mental Health Systems Act, which Carter signed on October 7, 1980. The Act authorized federal grants to community mental health centers, services directed at people with chronic mental illness, and a patients’ bill of rights. Congress never appropriated the Act’s funds before the administration changed hands. In 1981 President Reagan signed the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act, which repealed most of the Mental Health Systems Act and consolidated the surviving programs into a single block grant to the states carrying roughly 25 percent less funding. The statute book of 1963 and 1980 promised a community system; the appropriations record of 1981 reduced the federal share of building it.

St. Elizabeths

One hospital carries the whole chronology in a single institution. St. Elizabeths Hospital in Washington, founded in 1855 as the Government Hospital for the Insane, was the first federally operated psychiatric hospital in the United States. At its peak in the 1950s it housed more than 8,000 patients and employed 4,000 people. The psychiatrist E. Fuller Torrey joined its staff in 1976 and later volunteered for sixteen years in Washington’s shelters for homeless women; his books Nowhere to Go (1988) and Out of the Shadows (1997) are the abandonment filing’s central texts. The St. Elizabeths Hospital and District of Columbia Mental Health Services Act was enacted on November 9, 1984, and federal responsibility for the hospital ended with its transfer to the District of Columbia on October 1, 1987. By 1996, 850 patients remained; as of January 31, 2009, the inpatient census was 404.

The Street Count

The abandonment filing rests on what the discharged met. The community centers numbered about half the planned 1,500 and were underfunded. A Department of Health and Human Services study prepared in September 1986 cited the release of mentally ill people from institutions into communities lacking services among the causes of the era’s homelessness. The street count itself was contested from the start: estimates prepared for the National Bureau of Economic Research put the homeless population at 250,000 to 400,000 at any given time, while the National Coalition for the Homeless claimed three million over the course of a year. On July 22, 1987, President Reagan signed the Stewart B. McKinney Homeless Assistance Act, Public Law 100-77—the first significant federal legislative response to homelessness—creating fifteen programs and the Interagency Council on the Homeless. The signature is on both halves of the record: the 1981 repeal that cut the community system’s funding, and the 1987 act that first funded a federal response to the street.

The Prison Side

The releases have a second destination in the record, counted in the companion report. The federal imprisonment rate ran flat at about 110 per 100,000 from 1925 into the early 1970s—the asylum era did its removing through the hospital, not the prison—and then climbed to about five times its historic level by 2007, in the decades the hospital census completed its fall. Torrey and colleagues, in their 2010 survey of the states, found more than three times as many seriously mentally ill persons in jails and prisons as in hospitals, and about 16 percent of inmates seriously mentally ill, against 6.4 percent in 1983. The pattern is the one Lionel Penrose described in 1939, and the literature, after eighty years, describes his hypothesis as neither rejected nor confirmed. The counter-bearing is filed with it: scholars of the carceral state dispute a direct causal line from the hospital closures to mass incarceration, assigning the prison census to sentencing law and the drug war, and answer the proposal to rebuild the asylum—put in print by Sisti, Segal, and Emanuel in the Journal of the American Medical Association in 2015—from the record of what the asylums were.

Where the Record Splits

The two filings do not dispute the chronology. They dispute the unit of account. The liberation filing counts rights: commitments ended, holds capped at days instead of lifetimes, courts reviewing what physicians and families once decided alone, a Supreme Court holding that mental illness by itself is no ground for a locked door. The abandonment filing counts services: beds at 3.5 percent of their 1955 rate, a community system built at half scale, its funding act repealed within a year of signing, and the discharged visible in the shelters, the street counts, and the jail census. Both counts are drawn from the same documents. The intent clauses and the court holdings are on paper; so are the appropriations and the censuses. The desk files the chronology whole, under both verdicts, and draws no conclusion from it.
