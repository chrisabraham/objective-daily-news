Objective

Grand Theft Auto VI: The Launch, the Reviews, the Sick-Day Claim, and the Record of the Franchise

Rockstar Games released Grand Theft Auto VI on Thursday, November 19, 2026, thirteen years after Grand Theft Auto V. The reviews are the highest the aggregators have recorded. The sales figures in circulation are estimates, not company figures. The claim that a million and a half workers took the day off is a projection, not a count. The argument over the game’s politics is being conducted with clips from the same game, cited as proof of opposite conclusions. What the record shows, section by section, follows.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1159 ET, Friday, November 20, 2026.

The Record in Brief

Grand Theft Auto VI went on sale Thursday, November 19, 2026, for PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S, at $79.99 for the standard edition and $99.99 for the ultimate edition. Metacritic records a score of 99 from 151 critic reviews, with every review positive; the score ties the highest the site has recorded. Take-Two Interactive and Rockstar Games have published no sales figure. The totals in circulation are estimates by the analytics firm Sensor Tower, which puts the first day at about 24.58 million copies and about $2.36 billion, pre-orders included. The widely repeated claim that 1.5 million American workers would skip work on launch day traces to a projection published in September 2026; no measured count of American absences for November 19 has been published. In Britain, one absence platform recorded a 56 percent rise in sickness absence on the day against the previous Thursday. Take-Two shares rose 6.22 percent on the day the reviews appeared and fell 3.67 percent on launch day.

Thursday, November 19 | The Launch

The game unlocked in a rolling midnight release by time zone, beginning in New Zealand; in the United States it unlocked at midnight Eastern on November 19. It is a console release only. No personal-computer version has been announced, and no online mode was available at launch; Reuters reported analysts expect both in 2027. Pre-loading opened November 10. Rockstar’s pre-load guidance put the download at up to 212 gigabytes; Microsoft’s catalog listed a maximum near 225 gigabytes. The physical edition is a download code in a box, with no disc. Reuters, the global rollout Rockstar Games, launch announcement

Sony said in advance of the release that the game was the most pre-ordered title in PlayStation Store history; Microsoft said it was the most pre-ordered title on the Microsoft Store. Neither company published a number. Rockstar’s launch announcement stated that the game had arrived “as the Highest Rated Game of All Time,” a claim resting on the review scores covered below. Streams Charts, which measures viewing across streaming platforms, recorded a preliminary peak of 7,886,402 concurrent viewers on launch day and, in its later day-one accounting, a peak of 7,915,475 viewers and 125.47 million hours watched in 24 hours; the Twitch peak was the largest recorded for a gaming category in that service’s ranking. The internet exchange operator DE-CIX recorded throughput of 33.03 terabits per second on November 18, up 25 percent on the start of the year. Reuters, Take-Two and the launch

Comcast, the largest American broadband provider, said in a statement carried by the industry association NCTA that the first 48 hours of pre-loads “drove the largest standalone event in Comcast’s network history and two of its 10 busiest network days ever”; its peak day ranked third-busiest in the company’s history and above this year’s Super Bowl. Charter’s Spectrum service reported gaming download traffic up 40 to 50 percent in the pre-load period. In Florida, the Associated Press reported that Miami-Dade County entered a $3.9 million promotional agreement with Rockstar that included a “Welcome to Vice City” display on the Kaseya Center, and that the Miami Heat wore Vice City jerseys on the eve of release. A festival under the game’s name runs through the post-launch weekend with Cardi B, Metro Boomin and Travis Scott, Reuters reported. NCTA, the network operators’ statements

The Reviews: A 99, and the Spread Beneath It

The review embargo lifted at 11:00 UTC on Wednesday, November 18. Metacritic’s page for the game, as read on Friday, records a Metascore of 99, designated “Universal Acclaim,” from 151 critic reviews on the PlayStation 5 listing, of which 100 percent are positive, none mixed and none negative. The score ties The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, which has held a 99 since 1998 on 22 reviews. OpenCritic recorded a top-critic average of 99 and 99 percent recommending, from 89 reviews, in the early hours of November 19. CNN’s roundup of the first day counted more than 150 critics and described the praise as universal. Metacritic, the game’s page CNN, the reviews roundup

Perfect scores were recorded at, among others, IGN, where Luke Reilly called it “the single most impressive video game I have ever played” after more than 120 hours; GameSpot; The Guardian, where Keza MacDonald called Leonida “the most extraordinarily, indulgently detailed virtual world that we have yet seen”; The Washington Post, where Gene Park awarded four stars on the paper’s four-star scale; Edge; Game Informer; Eurogamer; and Forbes. Prestige outlets that do not score reviews were of the same tenor. The New York Times made the game a Critic’s Pick, in a review by Yussef Cole describing a 70-hour story about “love, relationships and nurturing your partner.” NPR’s review carried the headline “Grand Theft Auto VI is a grounded love story, a gonzo crime thriller and a gaming triumph.” Bloomberg’s Jason Schreier called the game a masterpiece. The Verge, also unscored, concluded that the game “does something impossible—it lives up to the hype.” NPR, the review

The spread beneath the aggregate is narrow but it exists. The lowest score on Metacritic is 80 of 100, from ScreenHub, where Alice Clarke wrote that “at its core, GTA 6 is a pretty good game,” reported being bored for the first dozen hours, and described the opening as an on-rails “Good Boyfriend Simulator” before the game became fun at about 25 hours. CBC’s Jonathan Ore called the game “a spectacular, cynical and sometimes shallow joyride through the American psyche” and “mostly a familiar formula with some smart additions,” an astonishing open world but no revolution in gaming. Slant awarded four and a half of five stars. The same criticisms recur inside the high scores and the unscored reviews: the game runs at 30 frames per second on every console, the PlayStation 5 Pro included, with no performance mode, and Rockstar confirmed to Kotaku that no performance-mode patch is planned; Digital Foundry measured sporadic dips below 30. Reviewers also recorded a slow first chapter, pacing sags in the middle and later chapters, tightly scripted missions, and, in The Verge’s account, clunky combat and a frustrating weapon wheel. Reported lengths differ by what is counted: about 60 hours for the main story at Push Square, 70 hours in The New York Times, and 115 to 120 hours to the credits with detours at IGN.

What reviewers praise in the world’s construction is specific. TheSixthAxis and RPGamer describe witnesses who report what they saw, police who search on the basis of those reports and can recognize the pair by appearance and vehicle, and a corresponding value in changing clothes and cars; NPCs react to visible long arms and to masks. NPR reported that Rockstar assigned a dedicated writing team to the incidental dialogue of the world’s pedestrians. The game is set in the fictional state of Leonida, modeled on Florida, with Vice City at its center and six regions named in coverage; the player starts in the Leonida Keys and, on Polygon’s account, may take up to 20 hours of story to reach Vice City. Rockstar’s Aaron Garbut has said Leonida is about twice the size of the previous game’s map; the company has published no measurement.

The audience scores stand apart from the critics’. Metacritic held user reviews closed until 12:00 p.m. Pacific on Friday, November 20, so no Metacritic user score existed when this report was filed. Ratings posted before release—Men’s Journal recorded a user score of 6.5 of 10 from five ratings, none with written text—were removed by Metacritic, which blocks user rating until after release as a standing practice against rating by people who have not played the title. On the PlayStation Store, the Kazakh news agency Qazinform recorded an average of 4.88 of 5 from nearly 48,000 ratings, about 96 percent of them five stars and 2 percent one star; the breakdown gives no reasons. Windows Report, the removed pre-release ratings Qazinform, the PlayStation Store ratings

The Sick-Day Claim: Predicted, Not Counted

The claim in circulation is that 1.5 million American workers would be absent on November 19, at a cost of about $1 billion in lost output. The desk found no source for the figure of 1.6 million, which has appeared in retellings; the figure in the reporting is 1.5 million. Its provenance is this. In July 2025, the Spanish broadcaster Cadena SER published an article whose first paragraph carried the $1 billion figure with no source or calculation; the article cited José García-Montalvo, an economics professor at the Universitat Pompeu Fabra in Barcelona, on the profile of gamers who might stay home, and it did not attribute either figure to him and did not mention 1.5 million workers. In September 2026 the International Business Times published the pairing—1.5 million absences, about $1 billion, at roughly $70,000 of average annual output per worker—and credited the estimate to García-Montalvo as an academic projection, linking to no study, survey or data. PolitiFact, in a fact-check published November 19, reported that other articles from the same publisher stated that neither figure should be attributed to García-Montalvo, who did not respond to its requests; PolitiFact concluded: “We found no official survey or published study that shows either 1.5 million workers planning time off or $1 billion in lost productivity.” The Guardian, examining the claim before launch, wrote that the figures “appear to correspond to some back-of-the-napkin math, rather than any hard data analysis,” and two outlets that recomputed the stated inputs found that 1.5 million workers at $70,000 a year across about 260 working days comes to roughly $400 million for one day, not $1 billion. NME repeated the 1.5 million figure in October, attributing it to the International Business Times. PolitiFact, the fact-check

Economists quoted after launch divided. Nicholas Bloom of Stanford University told PolitiFact that perhaps 5 percent of full-time American workers play the game and only a fraction of those would take the day off: “I really don’t believe these numbers unless it’s backed up by a population-representative survey.” Nicholas Huntington-Klein of Seattle University told Newsweek he expected about 800,000 to miss work, calling it a “very rough ballpark.” Victor Matheson of the College of the Holy Cross told Newsweek he had no issue with estimates of more than $1 billion in lost productivity, and in the same reporting called stories about the game crashing the economy “deeply flawed as any sort of real economic analysis,” because leave scheduled in advance does not cost employers in the way an unplanned absence does.

What was measured before launch was intentions and advance requests, and it is narrower than the headline figure. BambooHR, an HR software company, reported from anonymized platform data covering more than 3.4 million American employees that paid-time-off requests for November 19 ran nearly 13 percent above comparable Thursdays in 2024 and 2025; in its survey of 1,530 full-time American employees, conducted October 9 to 20, 18 percent of salaried workers said they planned to take launch day off, 32 percent of Gen Z respondents and 27 percent of millennials, and half of those intending to take time off for a game release said they would call in sick rather than schedule leave. UKG reported advance time-off requests among American frontline workers running about 20 percent above comparable pre-Thanksgiving weeks, from a base of about two million workers, and said its data does not show the game alone driving the rise. Gusto, a payroll company, reported time off booked for the day 9 percent above the two preceding Thursdays. A GameSquare and Pollfish survey of 1,000 full-time American employees who identify as gamers found 10 percent planning to skip work and another 30 percent considering it. A YouGov survey for PCMag produced the largest number in the record, an extrapolation to 12.5 million American adults, but its question covered taking time off work or school or rearranging a schedule, a broader measure than absence. BambooHR, the platform data and survey USA Today, the day-off reporting

After launch, one measured count exists, and it is British. BrightHR, whose absence software covers more than a million employees at about 70,000 British companies, recorded a 56 percent increase in sickness absence on November 19 against Thursday, November 12, and estimated 83,000 days booked off across November and December in connection with the game; its users are 3.37 percent of the British workforce. Personnel Today, reporting the figures, noted in the same article that absences also rose with colder weather and respiratory conditions, and that the London Ambulance Service had declared a critical incident that week. The Sun reported that “nearly a million” Britons skipped work and school; its article gives no source or method for that figure. For the United States, no measured count of November 19 absences has been published in the record reviewed; the American evidence remains the pre-launch requests and surveys above. Some employers made the day official: Logitech held its first company-wide “Game Day” with Thursday off as an option, Delivrd gave employees Friday off, and the game studio Pocketpair announced special leave from November 19 to 23. A June 2026 memo from Burger Motorsports announcing a company-wide pause was later said by the company to have been a joke. Personnel Today, the BrightHR figures MarketWatch, the callouts and the company holidays

The claim had an official life as a joke before it had one as a statistic. Take-Two’s chief executive, Strauss Zelnick, said at a Las Vegas conference in April 2026: “I think a lot of people will be calling in sick on November 19.” The British Embassy in Washington posted on launch day: “From our friends in Edinburgh to all American CEOs: we are deeply sorry about your lost productivity today.” Downing Street, asked whether the Prime Minister would play, answered: “His diary is very full. He won’t be playing it.” The Guardian quoted Jacqueline Burgess, a business and game studies researcher at the University of the Sunshine Coast: “Data suggests that there will be a spike in absenteeism.” A spike is what the measured record shows, in requests before the day and in British sickness absence on it. The 1.5 million figure itself was predicted, and in the United States it has not been counted. We Got This Covered, the embassy apology and the Guardian’s reporting

The Culture Argument, in Both Directions

The argument began with the first trailer, on December 4, 2023, which introduced Lucia Caminos, a Latina recently released from prison, as one of the game’s two leads. TheGamer reported in January 2024 that posters on social media called the series “woke” on stated grounds including that Lucia is a woman and that the trailer’s Vice City crowd contained Black and Hispanic people; comments quoted included “Not a single white person in this trailer. Big surprise.” A theory that Lucia is transgender circulated in the same period; the outlets that reported it stated it had no evidence, and Rockstar has announced no such thing. Two pieces of reporting were used as a stated basis for the claims. Bloomberg reported in July 2022 that the game’s developers were being cautious not to “punch down” by making jokes about marginalized groups, and in December 2024 that Rockstar was asking its writers to be “less crude toward transgender people and other minorities”; Rockstar did not comment on the December 2024 report at the time. In 2022, the updated version of Grand Theft Auto V had removed a doll described as having “interchangeable genitalia” and characters described as “drag queens” outside an in-game nightclub; NME reported that the changes followed an open letter from Out Making Games, a British group of LGBTQ+ people working in games, and that Rockstar never addressed the changes. Ned Luke, who voiced a lead in Grand Theft Auto V, answered the claims in an IGN interview reported by TheGamer, noting earlier female protagonists in the series and saying of Lucia: “She looks like a badass to me… she led the way. She kicked open the door, and then Jason was back here.” TheGamer, the early claims and Luke’s answer NME, the Bloomberg reports and the 2022 removals

At launch, the argument settled on what the released game does not contain. Players cannot hire sex workers, a feature present since Grand Theft Auto III in 2001, and the two leads cannot be unfaithful to each other. Rockstar’s own account, given to Shacknews and published November 18, is the anchor in the record: “GTA VI is a game about a couple in a bind, lovers on the run. While we have taken a lot forward from previous GTA titles, everything we have implemented in VI has been done to support the spirit of the story we wanted to tell… Many aspects of previous titles are not present in GTA VI, but the elements we have we believe are all necessary and complementary to the feeling and motivations we wanted to emphasize as you play through Jason and Lucia’s story.” The statement names no pressure and no politics. Claims in circulation that private strip-club dances were also removed are contradicted by review reporting: Dexerto, citing The Guardian’s review, reported that strip clubs are in the game and that either lead can take a private dance, alone or together. No Rockstar statement in the record attributes any content decision to outside pressure, and none refuses a specific requested change. Rob Nelson, Rockstar North’s co-studio head, told The Guardian that the game’s empathy mechanisms are a first for the series—“We’ve tried for the first time in a GTA to provide mechanisms that encourage it, for those who want to”—and that crime remains fundamental and players are not punished for playing as they are used to: “The relationship is yours to nurture or yours to lose.” Shacknews, Rockstar’s statement Dexerto, the removed and retained features

The complaints as posted are specific. The New York Post quoted an X poster: “GTA VI was 100% written by woke liberal white women. First, you can’t hire a hooker, and I am not allowed to cheat on my girlfriend Lucia with my hot neighbor Mandy”; another wrote, “You know it’s gone full woke when the main character is now a cuck”; a third called the game “a humiliation ritual for all male gamers.” WIRED reported that the streamer Zack Hoyt, who broadcasts as Asmongold, called the game a “cuck simulator” after finding that part of it has Jason preparing for Lucia’s return by buying groceries and repairing his house; that Braden Peters, who posts as Clavicular, complained of too much “simping for girls”; that Mark Kern, who posts as Grummz, called the housework “mundane crap”; and that Andrew Tate denounced the game in a newsletter message to subscribers. Polygon’s reporting on the released mechanics qualifies the claim that the game forces the relationship on the player: the preparation activities are ambient and, in its words, “could be disregarded altogether,” and a player can decline to collect Lucia from prison, after which the two argue and do not speak much, while the central story continues around the pair. New York Post, the complaints WIRED, the reaction to the relationship mechanics Polygon, the mechanics as released

A further set of claims concerns limits on violence. Players posting online said they could not attack protesters and could not shoot up certain nightclubs, as summarized by GuruGamer; Newsweek reported, on the accounts of players who began early, that the leads will not aim a weapon at patrons inside the game’s gay bars and nightclubs and that players cannot attack street protesters. One version of the claim, posted by Dom Lucre, held that Rockstar had made it impossible to open fire in a gay nightclub specifically. WIRED reported that the Washington Post’s critic Gene Park pointed out that the club in the clip was not an LGBTQ venue and that the game’s mechanics do not allow shooting anyone in any club. The restriction, on that account, is general to clubs rather than specific to one kind. GuruGamer, the player posts summarized

Clips from the same game were cited in the opposite direction within the same news cycle. Them reported a Vice City billboard, paid for by a fictional group called the American Rifle Membership, reading: “Step 1: They take your guns. Step 2: Forced gender reassignment surgery”; the game’s Leonida has outlawed furry costumes under a “No Paws Clause,” with its governor declaring the state “furry-free,” television advertisements urging citizens to report suspected furries, and an underground group, the Furverts, resisting the law. Them also reported that some transgender readers found the satire not visible enough as satire and feared it could be mistaken for endorsement. Newsweek reported that the game’s radio hosts resemble Alex Jones and Nick Fuentes, and Gizmodo described an in-game news channel’s talk show built on the same resemblances. The New York Post catalogued clips players were celebrating as evidence against political correctness: a radio advertisement for “Brownie Points” addressed to “liberal men suffering from white guilt”; an animal-rights activist mauled by the polar bear she and her group had freed, in a clip viewed more than eight million times; a second activist killed by an orca; a player executing a tiger; and a player shoving an elderly thief out of a motorized wheelchair. An X poster quoted in that article said the bear scene was “resulting in mass backlash from the Left”; the article cited no source for a backlash. A commenter quoted in the same article noted the two readings running at once: the game called woke for what it removed, and praised in the same feeds for the bear. Them, via Out, the billboards and the furry ban New York Post, the clips cited in the other direction Gizmodo, the political reaction

The published assessments of the whole, as distinct from the clips, converge on a narrower description. Newsweek described the game as skewering “both sides of the political aisle” and quoted Coventry University’s Marcus Maloney that the game still lets players fulfill “hyperviolent and hypermasculine fantasies,” and Bournemouth University’s Amy Tatum that the couple’s relationship is “a much more positive way of interacting with women than the previous games.” NPR’s review framed the game as a Bonnie-and-Clyde love story and “a knife-edged satire of modern American culture and politics.” Reviewers’ verdicts on Lucia herself are in the reviews above: TheGamer called her “without a doubt the best protagonist in Grand Theft Auto history”; MobileSyrup called her “easily the more compelling of the two” leads; Ars Technica found Jason the less compelling of the pair. Talk of a boycott was recorded before launch, in the same posts that made the “woke” claims; no source in the record measures a boycott’s size or attributes any sales change to one. The only scale figures in the record are Sensor Tower’s estimates, and Take-Two has published no figure of its own.

The Franchise: 1997 to 2026

The series was created by David Jones and Mike Dailly at DMA Design, later Rockstar North. The first Grand Theft Auto was released on November 28, 1997, under the working title “Race-n-Chase,” and already contained Liberty City, San Andreas and Vice City. Grand Theft Auto III took the series into three dimensions in 2001 and was the best-selling game of that year in the United States. Vice City followed in 2002 and San Andreas in 2004. On July 20, 2005, the Entertainment Software Rating Board revoked San Andreas’s Mature rating after its investigation found fully rendered sexual material programmed by Rockstar on the discs and inaccessible to players in normal play; it assigned an Adults Only rating and directed retailers to stop sales pending correction, and Take-Two ceased manufacture of that version and lowered its financial guidance. Grand Theft Auto IV was released in 2008. GamesIndustry.biz, the franchise history ESRB, the San Andreas determination

Grand Theft Auto V was released on September 17, 2013. Take-Two reported $800 million in its first day and $1 billion in three days, the fastest any entertainment property had then reached $1 billion. Guinness World Records separately counted 11.21 million units and $815.7 million in 24 hours; a Take-Two representative told GameSpot the Guinness figures “didn’t come from our company” and could not be verified by it. The two accounts stand unblended. Grand Theft Auto Online followed on October 1, 2013, and was still receiving content in 2026. Take-Two’s reported totals for the game rose past 220 million units in 2025 and, on the company’s November 9, 2026 earnings call, stood at nearly 235 million. The franchise as a whole passed 470 million units, on the company’s June 2026 count. Take-Two Interactive, the November 9 results

The road to the sixth game ran four and a half years in public. Rockstar confirmed the next entry “well underway” on February 4, 2022. In September 2022 more than 90 clips of development footage leaked; Rockstar confirmed a network intrusion on September 19, 2022. The first trailer was released on December 4, 2023, a day early after it leaked; Guinness counted 90,421,491 YouTube views in 24 hours, a record for a game trailer, while YouTube’s own figure, reported by GameSpot, was more than 93 million. On May 2, 2025, Rockstar delayed the game to May 26, 2026, citing the need to “deliver at the level of quality you expect and deserve.” The second trailer followed on May 6, 2025; Rockstar told The Hollywood Reporter it reached 475 million views in 24 hours across all platforms, a different basis from the first trailer’s YouTube-only count, and the two are not directly comparable. On November 6, 2025, Rockstar delayed the game a second time, to November 19, 2026, for “the level of polish you have come to expect and deserve.” An extended look premiered on Netflix on August 27, 2026; Take-Two said it was the platform’s most-viewed title that week, with 31 million viewers in four days. Pre-orders opened June 25, 2026, and the launch trailer followed on October 15.

The Business Frame: Guidance, Estimates and the Share Price

Take-Two’s stated expectations predate the launch and were not revised for it. On November 9, 2026, the company reiterated net bookings guidance of $8.0 to $8.2 billion for its 2027 fiscal year and $3.35 to $3.50 billion for the December quarter. Chief executive Strauss Zelnick said pre-orders were “continuing to reach unprecedented levels” in what he called “an historic year,” and told Reuters: “We still aren’t ready to declare victory” and “We tend to avoid claiming success before it occurs.” Chief financial officer Lainie Goldstein called the pre-order level “extraordinary.” The estimates against which the launch is being measured were set by analysts in advance: Bank of America put the figure needed to satisfy expectations at about $1.9 billion in first-weekend sales and flagged November 20 to 23 as the window in which Take-Two might publish figures; Wells Fargo forecast 65 million units in the fiscal year; Oppenheimer estimated about $1.85 billion in the first three days; Wedbush estimated $3 billion and 30 million copies by year-end. The production cost has been reported at $1 billion or more by Reuters and estimated at $1.4 billion by Citi’s Jason Bazinet, as reported by The Wall Street Journal; speculation in coverage runs from $1 billion to $2 billion. Sensor Tower’s day-one estimate of about $2.36 billion stands above the Bank of America first-weekend threshold, and it remains an estimate: neither Rockstar nor Take-Two had confirmed any day-one figure when this report was filed. Video Games Chronicle, the Sensor Tower estimate

The share price recorded the two days separately. On Wednesday, November 18, the day the reviews appeared, Take-Two shares closed at $216.61, up 6.22 percent. On launch day, Thursday, November 19, the shares opened at $214.73, reached $225.69, and closed at $208.65, down 3.67 percent. In the Friday morning snapshot available to the desk, the shares stood near $209. The launch-day session is the pattern traders call selling the news: the rise came on the reviews, and the fall came on the event itself, with the company’s own sales figures still unpublished.

Launch-Day Condition: Outages, Firmware and Player Reports

The PlayStation Network failed for part of Wednesday, November 18, hours before the American unlock. Sign-in and connection failures climbed from about 1 p.m. Eastern; Sony’s status page warned that players “might have difficulty launching games, apps or network features.” The outlets’ resolution times disagree and are printed as reported: Axios recorded the status page showing issues resolved by about 2:30 p.m. Eastern; other outlets recorded about 3:05 p.m. and about 3:15 p.m. Sony named no cause. Neither Sony nor Rockstar has said the game caused the outage. Downdetector complaint counts ranged from 1,228, as cited by The Guardian, to more than 4,000; complaint counts measure reports to the tracker, not affected accounts. A second, shorter disruption fell at the American unlock itself: PlayStation status pages showed degraded games and social services from 05:33 to 06:04 UTC on November 19 across more than 60 countries, on Endgame Tools’ check, and the monitor IsDown recorded about 24 minutes of degradation beginning 5:34 a.m. UTC, the same instant reported in Pacific time as 9:34 p.m. on November 18. Downdetector’s American reports passed 8,000 to 9,000 around the midnight Eastern unlock. No comparable Xbox outage was reported. Axios, the November 18 outage TechRepublic, the two disruptions

Two defects in the physical and system layer were documented before the unlock. PlayStation 5 system software 26.07-14.23.00, about 1.27 gigabytes, is required before the game will run; Sony’s notes list security fixes only, and PlayStation Support advised installing it before midnight. Separately, Polygon reported from November 12 that some boxed copies contained no download code, only a grey strip where the code should be; retailer responses varied, with some Best Buy locations refusing refunds, Amazon returning money, and GameStop referring buyers to Rockstar as a manufacturing defect. Rockstar had not commented publicly in that report. Engadget, the required firmware Polygon, the copies without codes

Reports of defects in the game itself are player reports, and are filed here as such. Dexerto, on November 20, collected repeated crashes under error code CE-108255-1; a PlayStation 5 Pro owner crashing at the same point in the opening prologue; a crash in the early mission “Pipework” when using a plunger at a sink; and Xbox Series X players reporting crashes after 15 minutes to two hours of play, with cache clearing, database rebuilding, factory resets and reinstalling reported not to help. Launch trackers also collected reports of the character Lucia falling through the map in the prologue, geometry clipping, and an early mission that blocks progress after leaving a bank. As of the evening of November 19, Rockstar had posted no known-issues list and no patch notes, and its service status page showed all services operational. A widely shared post on X claiming Rockstar had apologized and scheduled a fix was unverified. Dexerto, the player reports

The Labor Case in Glasgow

One part of the launch record is before a tribunal. In October 2025, Rockstar dismissed 34 employees in the United Kingdom and Canada. The Independent Workers’ Union of Great Britain, which represents 23 of the dismissed workers in an unfair-dismissal claim, has called the dismissals “blatant union-busting”; that is the union’s characterization. Closing submissions were heard at the Glasgow employment tribunal on November 17 and 18, 2026, the two days before the launch, before Judge Lucy Wiseman; no judgment has been issued, and no finding has been made. Counsel for the claimants, Richard O’Keeffe, told the tribunal the 34 were dismissed “with no warning or process whatsoever,” that Rockstar had monitored the workers’ Discord server for three years through “covert informants,” that the dismissals came as the union approached 10 percent representation across the British workforce, and that the company’s evidence was “a whole series of lies.” Counsel for Rockstar, Thomas Cordrey, told the tribunal the employees were “caught red-handed” flouting contractual policies, making disparaging comments and sharing confidential information—including messages that could be taken to refer to unreleased game features and release timing—on a semi-public Discord server with hundreds of members, and that union membership and activity had nothing to do with the dismissals. Rockstar’s statement, issued during the proceedings, reads: “Individuals across the UK and Canada were dismissed for gross misconduct following the sharing of confidential company information, not because of alleged trade union membership or activity. The tribunal has heard our evidence and seen the facts, and the hearing has not yet concluded. We remain confident of a positive outcome.” The Guardian, which covered the dismissals in its pre-launch examination, reported that Rockstar declined to comment to the paper; one dismissed Edinburgh developer, Will Mesilane, who had worked on the game for two years, told it: “A two-minute phone call caused devastation to our lives that is still ongoing more than a full year later.” The Times, the closing submissions Glasgow Times, the tribunal reporting

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
