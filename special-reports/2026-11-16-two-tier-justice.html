One Phrase, Four Arguments—and the Record Each One Stands On

Objective

Two-Tier Policing and Justice in Britain and Europe: The Phrase, the Protests, the Henry Nowak Case, the Sentencing Fight, and the Official Record — Special Report

The allegation of a two-tier system—that Britain and Europe police, prosecute, and sentence unevenly according to race, religion, politics, or wealth—examined usage by usage against the record. The desk files the phrase’s documented history, from a 1998 Northern Ireland policing proposal to its 2024 adoption in the riot week; the protest-policing claims of Mrs Braverman and Mr Farage against the Metropolitan Police’s own arrest figures and the Police Race Action Plan dispute; the Henry Nowak case whole, from the sentencing remarks and the pathology evidence through the open IOPC investigation and the United States State Department’s intervention; the 2025 pre-sentence-report fight and the Act it produced; the 2024 riot sentences and the Just Stop Oil appeal read against the Ministry of Justice’s ethnicity tables, printed raw and controlled together; the speech cases in both directions, from Lucy Connolly to the Palestine Action arrests; the wealth usage, legal aid and the court backlog; the migration tiers in statute and in claim; and the official position stated whole, in the words of the Prime Minister, the Commissioner, the Attorney General, and the Home Affairs Committee. The desk weighs none of the contested claims; where a claim can be checked against the record, the record is printed.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1700 ET, Monday, November 16, 2026.

The Report in Brief

One phrase now carries four separate arguments, and this report files them separately. “Two-tier” is alleged of protest policing, of sentencing, and of speech enforcement, each time in the claim that the state treats one side more harshly than another; and it is used, in two older and quieter senses, of the divide between those who can buy a defence and those who cannot, and of the tiers into which migration law now sorts people by the route they travelled. Each usage stands on a different record. The desk prints the claims attributed to their makers, the strongest evidence each side offers, and the official record at the same depth, and weighs none of it.

Several load-bearing details in the circulating version of this story do not survive checking, and the corrections run in every direction. Henry Nowak was killed in December 2025, not in 2026; the year since has been his killer’s trial and the argument over his last minutes. The police sentence most quoted against the service—that equality does not mean treating everyone “the same” or being “colour blind”—is not in the 2022 Police Race Action Plan but in a Police Anti-Racism Commitment of March 2025, whose wording the government is now reviewing. The Sentencing Council guideline that began the sentencing fight never came into force in the form that caused it. The American criticism of British policing in the Nowak case, which reads like an embellishment, exists: the State Department posted it on June 4, 2026, and Reuters reported it the next day. And the Home Secretary’s settlement tiers—five years, twenty years, thirty at the maximum—are real, announced on November 2, 2026, and are not yet law.

The statistical spine of the argument is one set of official tables, read in opposite directions. The Ministry of Justice’s Statistics on Ethnicity and the Criminal Justice System 2024 records average custodial sentences for indictable offences of 18.4 months for White offenders, 28.6 for Black offenders, and 32.2 for Asian offenders—and, in its controlled analysis, no statistically significant association between ethnicity and sentence length overall. The Home Office records Black people stopped and searched at 3.8 times the White rate and arrested at 1.9 times it. The polling records a public that believes, in large numbers, that the police apply the rules unevenly—and that divides, by ethnicity and by party, on the question of in whose favour. Sections 2 and 4 print those tables whole, with the caveats their publishers attach to them.

1. The Phrase Itself: A Northern Ireland Proposal, an Asserted Origin, and a Word Now Pointed in Every Direction

The earliest documented life of the phrase is not a complaint about bias at all. In March 1998, Mike Brogden, professor of criminology at Queen’s University Belfast, published a working paper for Democratic Dialogue titled “Two-tiered policing: a middle way for Northern Ireland?” It proposed a structure: a specialised Royal Ulster Constabulary for terrorism, intercommunal offences, and serious crime, alongside “a community policing service, unarmed and locally accountable,” for quality-of-life concerns. The outline appears in print as early as 1994. The phrase began as the name of a reform, offered by a criminologist, about a divided society’s police architecture—two tiers as a design, not an accusation.

The origin most often claimed for the modern phrase is asserted, not documented. Accounts published in 2026—in The Lead and in Byline Times—state that the term was originally used around the time of the 1981 Brixton riots to describe over-policing and institutional racism against Black communities, and that its present users have inverted an allegation first made by the people now accused of benefiting from it. The desk located no contemporary 1980s text—no pamphlet, newspaper, parliamentary passage, or passage of the Scarman report—using the phrase in that sense, and no authoritative source using it of the 1984–85 miners’ strike. The Brixton origin is printed here as what it is on the record: a claim about the phrase’s history, made by its 2026 chroniclers, which the desk could neither confirm from a contemporary document nor refute.

The phrase’s modern career runs through two statements, neither of which is the one usually quoted. In November 2023, the Home Secretary, Suella Braverman, accused senior officers in The Times of a “double standard”—she did not use the words “two-tier policing”—and was sacked within the week (Section 2). On August 5, 2024, during the riots that followed the Southport murders, Nigel Farage gave the phrase its current form: “Ever since the soft policing of the Black Lives Matter protests, the impression of two-tier policing has become widespread.” Since then the word has been taken up in every direction the record offers: openDemocracy had used it in 2015 of the policing of migrants (Section 7); Progressive International published “A Two-Tier System of Protest Policing” in December 2025, about the arrest and charging of climate protesters; the United States State Department used “two-tiered policing” against Britain itself in June 2026 (Section 3); and the BBC’s 2026 definition is the neutral one this report works from—the idea that some groups, or some behaviour such as protests, are dealt with more harshly by law enforcement than others.

2. Policing and Protests: The Soft Touch, the Heavy Hand, and the Arrest Figures

The claim in its strongest published form is Mrs Braverman’s, and it was made from inside the government. On October 30, 2023, as Home Secretary, she described the pro-Palestinian marches then filling London as “hate marches.” On November 8, in The Times, she wrote that “there is a perception that senior police officers play favourites when it comes to protesters”; that “right-wing and nationalist protesters who engage in aggression are rightly met with a stern response, yet pro-Palestinian mobs displaying almost identical behaviour are largely ignored, even when clearly breaking the law”; and she asked, “During Covid, why was it that lockdown objectors were given no quarter by public order police yet Black Lives Matters demonstrators were enabled, allowed to break rules and even greeted with officers taking the knee?” The article’s final text had not been approved by Downing Street. The Prime Minister, Rishi Sunak, sacked her on November 13, 2023, after the Armistice weekend, in which counter-protesters who had come to “defend the Cenotaph” clashed with police while the national march proceeded. Mr Farage’s August 2024 statement (Section 1) carried the argument out of government and into the riot week, and he has renewed it since—in November 2024 calling the then-unresolved Manchester Airport case “as clear an illustration of two-tier policing, two-tier justice in two-tier Britain, as you could possibly see”; the Crown Prosecution Service said at the time it was still considering material in that case, and charges followed.

The arrest figures complicate the absolute form of the claim, and the desk prints them with the explanation their publisher attached. Metropolitan Police figures given to the London Assembly record, for central London in 2020: 281 arrests at Black Lives Matter protests in May and June, of which 19 were for breaches of the coronavirus regulations; 374 arrests at anti-lockdown protests between May and November, of which 351 were for coronavirus breaches; and 681 arrests at Extinction Rebellion protests. The force noted that no protest is the same and that conditions varied with the guidance in force—the anti-lockdown protests were, by definition, protests against the regulations under which their participants were arrested. Some officers did take the knee at the 2020 protests, as Mrs Braverman and Mr Farage say; the inspectorate later held that doing so on duty would “rarely be appropriate.” For the pro-Palestinian marches, the Met recorded 386 arrests for offences related to the Israel–Hamas conflict by mid-November 2023, 253 of them at protests or public gatherings. “Largely ignored” does not describe those numbers; what the numbers cannot settle is the comparison of manner—the speed of intervention, the tolerance of chanting and insignia—on which the claim, in its careful form, actually rests. The Met’s public-order lead, Assistant Commissioner Matt Twist, conceded one part of it in 2024: “we didn’t get everything right—particularly in the early stages in October. On occasion, we did not move quickly to make arrests.” His general answer stands in Section 8.

The comparison at the argument’s centre is between the marches and the summer of 2024, and the two events differ in kind on the official record. After the murders of three girls in Southport on July 29, 2024, and false online claims about the attacker, disorder spread to more than 27 towns and cities: the Home Affairs Committee counts 246 events, 1,804 arrests and 1,072 charges by January 22, 2025, and 302 police officers injured. The marches the claim sets against the riots were planned, notified, and largely peaceful; the riots involved missiles, arson, and assaults on officers, and were met accordingly. Whether that difference of kind explains the difference of treatment, or whether the difference of treatment tracks the politics of the crowd, is the contested question; the facts each side cites are the same facts.

The documentary exhibit in the case is a sentence about colour, and its date matters. The Police Race Action Plan, published in draft on May 24, 2022 by the National Police Chiefs’ Council and the College of Policing and signed by all 43 chief constables in England and Wales, commits the service to become “anti-racist and trusted by Black people” and to “explain or reform race disparities.” The sentence the plan’s critics quote—that racial equity means “responding to individuals and communities according to their specific needs, circumstances and experiences, with understanding that these will be racialised,” and that “it does not mean treating everyone ‘the same’ or being ‘colour blind’”—is from the Police Anti-Racism Commitment issued under the plan in March 2025, not from the 2022 text. Mr Farage, at Prime Minister’s Questions on June 3, 2026, rendered it as an instruction: “The instructions that are given to police officers from police bosses are clear and written down in ink. It says you must treat different ethnic groups in different ways.” The Shadow Home Secretary, Chris Philp, called the commitment “a formal policy requirement for two-tier policing.” Within days, the Policing Minister, Sarah Jones, disowned the wording—the document “gives the wrong impression,” she told the BBC; “I think this document is wrong”; it “does not form the basis of any training or any police activity”—and the NPCC announced a review of the wording, its chair, Gavin Stephens, defending the intent: to improve the quality of policing. The Home Secretary told the Commons the review would ensure “no ambiguity or suggestion of differential treatment.” No withdrawal of the plan itself is on the record.

The inspectorate has examined protest policing and the 2024 disorder, and has returned no verdict on the slogan—its one tilt finding runs the other way. His Majesty’s Inspectorate of Constabulary, in Getting the balance right? (March 2021), concluded that “the police do not strike the right balance on every occasion. The balance may tip too readily in favour of protesters… a modest reset of the scales is needed.” Its September 2024 inspection of activism and impartiality—commissioned by Mrs Braverman herself—found a “near-total absence” of any definition of impartiality in policing and that most chief officers reported improper political pressure or interference. Its two reports on the 2024 disorder (December 2024 and May 2025) found forces unprepared for its scale, national mobilisation decided too late, and intelligence wanting; they did not address the two-tier claim. The finding that does address it belongs to a parliamentary committee, and is printed in Section 8.

The polling finds the perception widespread and its direction unsettled. YouGov, in August 2024, found 51 per cent of Britons believing the police treat Black people differently from others—and that share split almost evenly on direction, 29 per cent saying more strictly, 22 per cent more leniently. YouGov’s fuller survey of June 2026 (3,116 adults, June 11–18) found ethnic-minority adults far more likely than white adults to say Black people are treated more strictly (53 per cent against 33 per cent; among Black adults, 65 per cent), while white adults were more likely to say Muslims are treated more leniently (37 per cent, against 17 per cent saying more strictly)—a pattern that reversed among ethnic-minority respondents. A ZenCity survey for the Police Foundation’s 2026 policing conference found nearly half the public believing the police apply the rules more strictly to people of certain political views; of that group, 43 per cent thought people of right-leaning views treated more harshly. The survey’s sample and question wording have not been published. What the polling establishes, in its own terms, is not a consensus that one named group is favoured, but a country that believes in unevenness and disagrees about its direction—along the same lines of ethnicity and party along which the argument itself is fought.

3. The Henry Nowak Case: What the Court Record Establishes, and What Is Not Yet Established

The death at the centre of the current argument occurred on December 3, 2025, in Portswood, Southampton. Henry Nowak, 18, from Chafford Hundred in Essex, was in his first term at the University of Southampton, studying accounting and finance—the first in his family to go to university. Walking home alone and unarmed shortly after 11 p.m., he met by chance Vickrum Singh Digwa, 22, a Southampton-born member of the Sikh Nihang order, carrying a large dagger in a sheath on a belt over his clothing. Nowak filmed him and asked, in the sentencing judge’s words, in a non-threatening tone, “perhaps cheekily,” whether he was a “bad man”—reasonably concluded to be because he had seen the dagger. Digwa replied that he was, seized the phone, and stabbed him repeatedly: two wounds to the leg, a wound to the abdomen, a cut to the face, and a deliberate thrust to the chest that passed between the ribs, caught a lung, and cut a vein behind the collarbone to a depth of eight centimetres. The pathologist’s evidence, recorded in the sentencing remarks, is part of the record the argument must carry: 1,200 millilitres of blood in the chest cavity, and no first aid, CPR, or expert treatment could have reached the bleeding vein—he would not have survived however quickly he was treated. He was pronounced dead shortly after midnight.

What happened next is established by the trial, and it begins with a lie. Digwa filmed the dying man, told him he had not been stabbed, and told his brother that Nowak had attacked him and called him a “Paki.” His brother’s 999 call repeated a claim of racial attack; Nowak can be heard in its background saying, “No I didn’t,” and a neighbour heard him say, “I am not a racist.” The sentencing judge, William Mousley KC, was categorical: “I am sure that Henry had said nothing racist. You are the only person to make that claim and it is completely at odds with his previous character.” The jury rejected self-defence; Digwa had confessed the stabbings in a covertly recorded conversation in a police van on December 5, agreeing with his brother to pretend otherwise. Officers arriving at the scene were given what the judge called “a convincing but wholly false narrative.” On it, they arrested Nowak and handcuffed him—for about a minute, the judge found—while he said “I’ve been stabbed” and “I can’t breathe”; one officer is heard on the body-worn video responding, “Don’t think you have, mate.” It was dark; Nowak wore a dark top; the judge found the chest wound would not have been obvious, and that the officers “honestly believed that there were reasonable grounds for suspecting Henry had committed an offence.” When his condition deteriorated, the handcuffs came off and an officer began CPR. The judge listed Digwa’s lies among the murder’s aggravating features: they “influenced their decision not only to arrest and handcuff Henry but also to give subsequent emergency first aid in ignorance of the fact that he had a serious chest wound.” Digwa, arrested at 11:55 p.m. on suspicion of attempted murder, was not handcuffed—a contrast now under formal investigation. His mother, Kiran Kaur, who took the dagger, sheath, and belt away at his request, was convicted of assisting an offender and sentenced to three years on July 17, 2026.

The sentence the case produced is no longer the sentence most reports carry. A jury at Southampton Crown Court convicted Digwa of murder on May 28, 2026. On June 1 he received life imprisonment with a minimum term of 21 years. On November 5, 2026, the Court of Appeal—the Lady Chief Justice presiding—acted on the Attorney General’s reference of the term as unduly lenient, quashed the 21 years, and substituted 25: “A minimum term of 21 years did not adequately reflect the overall seriousness of the murder. It was not only lenient, but unduly so.” Digwa’s own application against the sentence was refused as unarguable; an application to appeal the conviction awaits a decision.

The investigation of the police is open, and its course so far is itself part of the record. Hampshire and Isle of Wight Constabulary referred itself to the Independent Office for Police Conduct on the day of the death, as it must; the officers were at first treated as witnesses. In April 2026 the IOPC wrote to the family that “no concerns identified in relation to officer actions” meant no officer would face a conduct or criminal investigation, the decision kept under review. The body-worn video was released, with the family’s permission, on June 2, 2026, the day after sentencing. On July 1, the IOPC reversed its position: the two first-arriving officers are under investigation for potential gross misconduct—over failures to recognise that Nowak needed urgent medical attention, to act when he said he had been stabbed and could not breathe, and in the decision to arrest and handcuff him rather than give immediate first aid—and the investigation was widened to control-room decisions and to “whether the race or religion of Nowak or the Digwa family impacted officers’ actions.” As of the IOPC’s statement of November 6, 2026, the investigation continues and has made no findings; reporting in November put its conclusion early in 2027. Around it stand three more examinations: an HMICFRS review of the force’s systems, commissioned in June; an Independent Review under Alex Chalk KC, appointed by the Home Secretary on October 26–27, 2026, whose first phase, on what was known of Digwa beforehand and the family’s treatment by the system, reports by March 2027; and an Article 2 jury inquest, to open at Winchester on September 20, 2027, charged with the question the criminal court did not have to answer—whether any act, omission, or delay by the police caused or contributed to the death. The Chief Constable, Alexis Boon, apologised to the family on June 3 and has since said the force failed Henry “in his final moments, when he needed us most.”

The political explosion came on the footage’s release, and the family has answered it in terms. On the evening of June 2, about a thousand people gathered outside Southampton Central police station and marched to Belmont Road; in the disorder that followed, eleven officers and a police dog were injured, and 34 people were later charged—the sentencing judge in their cases called the violence “a hate crime borne out of a hatred for police and in some part racist views.” Mr Farage, in a video that day, called for the country to respond with “pure, cold rage,” and put the Race Action Plan to the Prime Minister the next day (Section 2). Mr Philp told the Commons that “police appeared more concerned with the accusation of racism than they were with helping Henry”; Mrs Badenoch called the treatment “absolutely appalling.” On June 4 the United States State Department posted: “Ideological conditioning and two-tiered policing are glaring symptoms of civilizational decline. They must be rejected across the West,” with condolences to the family; the Vice-President, JD Vance, posted the next day that Nowak would be alive had European elites resisted “the mass invasion of migrants.” A spokesman for the Prime Minister rejected the characterisation, and the Deputy Prime Minister, David Lammy, told Sky News he did not recognise “this caricature of Britain having a two-tier criminal justice system.” The family’s position has not moved since the day of sentence. Mark Nowak, outside the court on June 1: “We do not want his death to be used to create further division, hatred, or tension.” His mother, Lucy Ross, in November: Henry “would have hated” the riots; “I’m not interested in your political game. I want to know why Henry was on the floor, and he got treated the way he did, but that family got treated in a different way. What is that?” What the record does not yet contain is any finding—by the IOPC, the inspectorate, the review, or the inquest—that race, religion, or doctrine determined how Henry Nowak was treated in his last minutes. That question is open, and four separate processes are now charged with answering it.

4. Courts and Sentencing: The Pre-Sentence Report Fight, the Riot Sentences, the Climate Sentences, and the Disparity Tables

The sentencing branch of the argument began with a document, and the document’s exact words are the case. On March 5, 2025, the Sentencing Council published its revised Imposition of community and custodial sentences guideline, to take effect on April 1. It provided that a pre-sentence report—the background report that commonly precedes a non-custodial sentence—“will normally be considered necessary” if the offender belonged to one of a list of cohorts: those at risk of a first or short custodial sentence; young adults, typically 18 to 25; women; offenders “from an ethnic minority, cultural minority, and/or faith minority community”; those pregnant or post-natal; sole or primary carers; and, in a second list, offenders who are transgender, addicted, seriously ill or disabled, or victims of abuse, trafficking, or coercion. The list was not exhaustive—a report could be necessary for an offender in no cohort—and the 2017 guideline it replaced had contained no cohort list at all. The Council’s defence, in its chairman’s reply to the Justice Secretary, rested on evidence of disparity in sentence outcomes between White and minority-ethnic offenders for some offences: the report was the instrument by which a court learned the circumstances that produce such outcomes.

The fight took twenty-six days, and ended in a statute. The Justice Secretary, Shabana Mahmood, responded the day of publication: “As someone who is from an ethnic minority background myself, I do not stand for any differential treatment before the law, for anyone of any kind. There will never be a two-tier sentencing approach under my watch.” Her formal letter of March 20 held that “encouraging a judge to request a pre-sentencing report for one named cohort but not another, amounts to differential treatment.” The Shadow Justice Secretary, Robert Jenrick, wrote the same day the guideline appeared: “Under new guidance prison sentences will be less likely for ‘ethnic minorities’ and ‘faith minority communities’. This would create a two tier justice system.” The Council, maintaining on March 31 that its guideline was “necessary and appropriate,” delayed its commencement because a bill was coming that would make it unlawful. The bill became the Sentencing Guidelines (Pre-sentence Reports) Act 2025, Royal Assent June 19, 2025: sentencing guidelines about obtaining pre-sentence reports may not be framed by reference to an offender’s personal characteristics—in particular race, religion or belief, and cultural background. It does not restrict any court from ordering a report in an individual case. The Council amended the guideline on June 13, and it came into force on September 1, 2025, without the cohort list. The guideline that caused the fight therefore never operated in the form that caused it; what the episode established is that Parliament, presented with a guideline that sorted defendants by named characteristics, struck the sorting out within fifteen weeks.

Behind the guideline fight stood the summer before it: the fastest mass sentencing in modern British practice. After the 2024 riots the Director of Public Prosecutions, Stephen Parkinson, directed immediate charging decisions and said he was determined “that we will act swiftly and robustly”; the Prime Minister said on August 6 that he was “expecting substantive sentencing before the end of this week.” The Ministry of Justice’s official series (Criminal Justice System Statistics Quarterly, year ending December 2024, civil-disorder chapter) records, for August to December 2024: 912 defendants prosecuted, 680 convicted, 651 sentenced, 76 per cent of those sentenced receiving immediate custody, an average custodial term of 23.3 months, and terms ranging to nine years. The nine-year terms—Thomas Birley’s, and Levi Fishlock’s, the latter a 14-year extended sentence of nine years’ custody and five on licence, at Sheffield Crown Court—rested on arson with intent to endanger life at the Manvers hotel in Rotherham; violent disorder alone carries a maximum of five years. The speed is the critics’ exhibit and the government’s boast in the same words; the severity, on the official series, is the severity of the offences charged, which included attempted arson of an occupied hotel.

The comparison case was already in the same court system, and in March 2025 the Court of Appeal ruled on it. On July 18, 2024, at Southwark Crown Court, five Just Stop Oil conspirators in the November 2022 M25 gantry plot were sentenced for conspiracy to cause a public nuisance: Roger Hallam to five years, and Daniel Shaw, Lucia Whittaker de Abreu, Louise Lancaster, and Cressida Gethin to four years each—terms reported at the time as the longest ever imposed for non-violent protest in Britain. Sixteen appellants came before the Court of Appeal; on March 7, 2025, six succeeded. Hallam’s term fell to four years, Shaw’s and Lancaster’s to three, Whittaker de Abreu’s and Gethin’s to thirty months, and Gaie Delap’s from twenty months to eighteen; ten appeals were dismissed. The court’s reasoning is the answer the system gives to the two-tier charge in this direction: “The appellants’ conscientious motivation was a factor relevant to sentencing in each case,” falling to be weighed in culpability, though it “does not preclude a finding that any appellant’s culpability is still high”; the trial judge had “taken no account at all” of it, and the M25 terms were manifestly excessive. Mr Jenrick took the ruling itself as his exhibit, opening a thread on March 24, 2025: “Does the UK have a two-tier justice system? This month the Court of Appeal handed down a judgement for Roger Hallam, co-founder of Just Stop Oil. It’s a deeply troubling case that suggests we do.” The claim from the opposite side was made in the same terms by the sentences’ critics: Adrian Ramsay, the Green Party’s co-leader, in September 2024—“I don’t think anyone could really genuinely say that a peaceful protest should get a heavier sentence than someone who’s a rapist”—and George Monbiot, in The Guardian, comparing the M25 terms with suspended sentences the same trial judge had given for a violent assault on two off-duty police officers and other grave offences. After the appeal, Greenpeace’s Areeba Hamid called the reduced terms “still unprecedented… no place in a democracy that upholds the right to protest,” and Friends of the Earth’s Katie de Kauwe said locking up the conscientiously motivated “is neither right or makes any sense.” Both directions of the argument thus cite the appellate record: one side its reductions as indulgence, the other the surviving terms as severity.

The disparity tables are the argument’s foundation and its refutation, depending on which table is opened, and the desk prints the two together as their publisher does. The Ministry of Justice’s Statistics on Ethnicity and the Criminal Justice System 2024 (published November 27, 2025) records that of defendants prosecuted for indictable offences in 2024, 83 per cent were White, 7 per cent Black, 6 per cent Asian, 3 per cent Mixed, and 1 per cent Other, against 2021 Census population shares of 82, 4, 9, 3, and 2 per cent. The average custodial sentence for indictable offences in 2024 was 18.4 months for White offenders, 28.6 months for Black offenders, 32.2 for Asian, 22.9 for Mixed, and 22.2 for Other. The same compendium attaches its own cautions: offence mix differs, and White defendants pleaded guilty at 63 per cent against 54 per cent for Black defendants, guilty pleas reducing sentences; and its controlled analysis, holding offence, age, sex, plea, and court constant, found Black offenders at 8 per cent higher odds of custody than White, Other offenders at 18 per cent higher, Asian offenders at 12 per cent lower—magnitudes the Ministry classes as small, with no consistent pattern over five years—and no statistically significant association between ethnicity and the length of a custodial sentence. At the remand stage the gradient is steeper: magistrates’ courts remanded in custody 23 per cent of White defendants against 30 per cent of Black defendants in 2024, and at June 30, 2025, White prisoners were 74 per cent of the sentenced population but 65 per cent of the 17,701 people held on remand. The Home Office’s police statistics for the year ending March 2025 record 528,582 stops and searches, with Black people searched at 3.8 times the White rate (4.9 times on the combined measure), arrested at 1.9 times the White rate, and subject to use of force at 3.3 times the White rate outside London. The Lammy Review had stated the pattern in 2017: people from Black, Asian, and minority ethnic backgrounds were 14 per cent of the population, 25 per cent of adult prisoners, and 41 per cent of children in custody, and institutions must “explain or reform” such disparities. The Ministry’s standing warning, printed in the compendium, is that uncontrolled differences “should not be taken as evidence of bias.” Both the warning and the numbers are the official record; the two camps in this argument are, in large part, the people who quote one without the other.

5. Speech Enforcement: The Incitement Case, the Proscription, and the European Files

The selective-enforcement claim, as its principal institutional maker states it, is a claim about the breadth of the laws and the direction of their use. ADF International, the legal-advocacy organisation that litigates the European speech cases, states it in its executive director Paul Coleman’s words of October 29, 2025: “In a democracy, no one should face criminal prosecution for expressing deeply held beliefs. Criminalizing peaceful speech through so-called ‘hate speech’ laws not only silences important conversations—it endangers democracy itself.” The claim as made by its British proponents is that broadly drawn public-order and incitement statutes are enforced readily against conservative Christians, anti-migration speakers, and social-media posters of the majority population, and hesitantly against extremist rhetoric within minority communities. No source in the record before the desk verifies that proposition in general; what verifies is the casework, and the desk prints the cases in both directions.

The case the British argument turns on is Lucy Connolly’s, and its facts are fixed by the Court of Appeal. On July 29, 2024, the day of the Southport murders, Mrs Connolly, a childminder in Northampton, posted on X: “Mass deportation now. Set fire to all the fucking hotels full of the bastards for all I care…” The post was viewed 310,000 times. She was arrested on August 6, pleaded guilty on September 2 to stirring up racial hatred (section 19(1), Public Order Act 1986), and in October 2024 was sentenced to 31 months—a starting point of three years and six months for the highest category of the offence, reduced by a quarter for her plea, with 40 per cent to be served. The Court of Appeal refused her appeal against sentence on May 20, 2025 ([2025] EWCA Crim 657). She was released from HMP Peterborough on August 21, 2025. The Leader of the Opposition, Kemi Badenoch, wrote that day that her “punishment was harsher than the sentences handed down for bricks thrown at police or actual rioting.” The Prime Minister, asked about the case at Prime Minister’s Questions on May 21, 2025, answered in the terms Section 8 records.

The comparison case pressed against Connolly’s is Ricky Jones’s, and the procedural difference between them is in the record. Mr Jones, a Labour councillor, was filmed at a counter-demonstration in Walthamstow on August 7, 2024, saying of far-right protesters that they should have their throats cut. He was arrested the next day and charged with encouraging violent disorder. He pleaded not guilty, was tried, and a jury acquitted him on August 15, 2025. Mr Philp decried the acquittal in two-tier terms, Jones cleared while Connolly served 31 months. The distinction the record supplies is not a small one: Connolly pleaded guilty to a completed incitement offence and was sentenced under its guideline; Jones contested a different charge and twelve jurors were not sure of it. Whether that distinction answers the comparison or merely describes it is the argument; the two outcomes are as stated.

The largest speech-adjacent enforcement of the period ran in the opposite direction, and it is a matter of statute rather than discretion. Parliament voted on July 2, 2025, by 385 to 26, to proscribe Palestine Action as a terrorist organisation; the order took effect on July 5. Supporting a proscribed organisation is an offence under the Terrorism Act 2000—section 13, carrying or displaying an article arousing reasonable suspicion of support, carries up to six months; section 12, inviting support, up to fourteen years. On August 9, 2025, in Parliament Square, demonstrators organised by Defend Our Juries sat holding signs reading “I oppose genocide. I support Palestine Action.” The Metropolitan Police’s figure as of 9 p.m. that day was 466 people arrested for showing support for Palestine Action and eight more for other offences—474 in the day, the force’s largest single-day arrest total in at least a decade; the BBC’s weekend total reached 532. The first three defendants had been charged on August 7, and 67 by the middle of the month. The hesitation limb of the selective-enforcement claim must also reckon with the period’s heaviest speech-adjacent sentence: Anjem Choudary, convicted on July 23, 2024, at Woolwich Crown Court of directing Al-Muhajiroun, a terrorist organisation, and sentenced to life imprisonment with a minimum term assessed at 28 years.

Two British and Finnish files, filed in full in the desk’s Persecution Ledger of this date, are the European claim’s standard exhibits, and their endings are narrower than their citations. Isabel Vaughan-Spruce, arrested in Birmingham in December 2022 for silently praying within a clinic buffer zone, was acquitted on February 16, 2023, when the prosecution offered no evidence; arrested again in March 2023 and not charged; and received a £13,000 settlement from West Midlands Police in August 2024. She was charged on December 17, 2025, under section 9 of the Public Order Act 2023—the first charge under the national buffer-zone law—has pleaded not guilty, and stands trial in January 2027. Päivi Räsänen, the Finnish member of parliament prosecuted for ethnic agitation over a Bible-verse tweet, a radio interview, and a 2004 church booklet, was acquitted at two levels and finally, on March 26, 2026, acquitted on the tweet and convicted by three votes to two on the booklet, with day-fines of €1,800; her application to the European Court of Human Rights awaits a ruling. The desk’s Persecution Ledger, published today, prints both files whole, with the baselines of their own systems.

On the mainland, the verified record is thinner and stranger than the claim. In Germany, the Federal Interior Ministry’s ban of November 2, 2023 treats the slogan “From the river to the sea” as a symbol of Hamas, prosecutable under section 86a of the criminal code; a Berlin court convicted a demonstrator, Ava Moayeri, fining her €600 in August 2024, holding the phrase denied Israel’s right to exist, while the Mannheim regional court held in May 2024 that the phrase was not punishable as a Hamas symbol. The Federal Court of Justice has not definitively ruled: the German courts are, on this record, in two tiers of their own. In France, the exhibit resolves to a single case, Sanchez v. France: Julien Sanchez, a National Front councillor and candidate—later mayor of Beaucaire—was convicted at Nîmes in February 2013 and fined, reduced on appeal to €3,000, for failing promptly to delete third-party anti-Muslim comments posted on his public Facebook wall, being liable as its “producer” under French press law. The European Court of Human Rights’ Grand Chamber held on May 15, 2023 that his conviction violated no right under Article 10. No judgment at Strasbourg addresses selective enforcement as such; the court’s speech cases decide the case in front of them, and the pattern alleged by the claimants is assembled, if it is assembled, from the casework above.

6. The Third Usage: Wealth, Legal Aid, and the Price of a Defence

The phrase’s third life is the oldest of the three, and it is not about race or politics. In 2013, when the government proposed price-competitive tendering for criminal legal aid, the Bar Council of England and Wales warned: “Instead of having equal access to justice regardless of means, we will have a two-tier system: one for those who can afford high-quality representation and one for those who can’t.” The usage has been renewed in the same sense since, in narrower rooms: the Bar Council’s written evidence to the Commons Justice Committee’s access-to-justice inquiry described “one aspect of the two-tier justice that exists in children’s cases”—wealthy parents able to instruct independent social workers who complete in eight weeks the reports that the state service, Cafcass, takes more than twenty weeks to complete. A London defence firm, Advice Wise Solicitors, publishes the same argument in its own name; the desk files it as a practitioner’s marketing, and carries the argument on the institutional record below.

The contraction underneath the argument is measured by the Institute for Fiscal Studies on the Ministry of Justice’s own data. The Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012, in force from April 2013, restricted civil legal aid to a limited list of case types and cut fees. By 2024, on the IFS’s 2026 analysis, civil legal aid case volumes had fallen by about 57 per cent from pre-reform levels, and the number of providers had fallen from about 3,000 in 2010 to about 1,400. Analysis cited in the Justice Committee’s follow-up work in November 2026 put 14.49 million people—23.4 per cent of the population—in a legal aid desert, without a local provider in at least one category of law.

The criminal side of the system is short of lawyers, on the Justice Committee’s figures. The Committee’s report Access to Justice: Legal Aid (HC 541, July 17, 2026) records duty solicitors down 25 per cent since 2017, with only 7 per cent of them under 35, and records the estimate that in the first half of 2023 as many as half of defendants facing imprisonable summary offences in the magistrates’ courts had no legal representation recorded. Sir Christopher Bellamy’s Independent Review of Criminal Legal Aid (2021) had recommended a minimum 15 per cent uplift to all criminal legal aid fees as “the minimum necessary as the first step in nursing the system of criminal legal aid back to health.” The Ministry of Justice’s December 2024 announcement of up to £92 million a year took the total uplift since the review to about 24 per cent; the department told a Lords committee that no further rises are expected before at least March 2029.

The queue is the other half of the wealth argument: a defendant who can pay buys time as well as counsel. The Ministry of Justice’s Criminal court statistics quarterly records 80,829 cases open in the Crown Court at the end of June 2026, of which 23,706 had been open for a year or more, and 380,230 open in the magistrates’ courts, a peak since the comparable series began in 2019. The median time from charge to completion in the quarter was 50 days in the magistrates’ courts and 189 days in the Crown Court—245 days for defendants on bail. The Justice Committee’s judgment on the means test that decides who receives criminal legal aid at all was that the magistrates’ court threshold is “shockingly low,” not uprated since 2009 while prices have risen by around 64 per cent, and its summary of the whole was that access to justice in England and Wales “has been severely eroded by the declining availability of legal aid” and the wider sector “remains financially unsustainable.” The government’s response, in October 2026, the Committee called inadequate; its word for the policy was “managed decline.”

7. Migration: One Word, Three Directions

Applied to migrants, the phrase runs in three directions at once, and the desk prints them apart. The first is the claim that migrants and asylum seekers are favoured over citizens. The second is a description of statute: British and European law now sorts migrants into tiers by how they arrived, in so many words. The third, from human-rights bodies, is the claim in reverse—that migrants are held to procedures and subsistence levels no citizen would be asked to accept.

The first direction’s strongest exhibit is the asylum hotel, and the official figures are printed beside the claim. Reform UK and its leader Nigel Farage have argued that asylum seekers are housed ahead of local people; in June 2026 Mr Farage wrote that Britain is a “two-tier state against white people” and proposed barring foreign nationals from social housing. The structural record is that asylum seekers are not in the social-housing queue at all: they are excluded from local-authority allocation while a claim is pending, and are housed, if destitute, under a separate Home Office duty. That duty’s cost is the critics’ real material. The Home Office’s Immigration system statistics for the year ending June 2026 record 93,293 people on asylum support at June 30, 2026, of whom 16,021 were in hotels—half the figure of a year earlier and 71 per cent below the peak of 56,018 in September 2023. Asylum support cost £4.0 billion in 2024–25, including £5.5 million a day on hotels; the National Audit Office (May 2025) put a hotel place at about £170 per person per night against £27 for dispersal accommodation, with hotels holding about 35 per cent of accommodated people and 76 per cent of contract cost.

The deportation limb of the first direction rests on tribunal cases, and the record of its most-cited case includes the ending the citation omits. Critics point to foreign offenders who resist deportation under Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, the right to family life. In the case of Klevis Disha, an Albanian national convicted in connection with more than £250,000 of criminal cash, a First-tier Tribunal allowed his appeal as unduly harsh on his son, citing as its sole example of what the child would face abroad that he “will not eat the type of chicken nuggets that are available abroad.” The Upper Tribunal, on January 9, 2025, held that example did “not approach anywhere near the level of harshness” required, found an error of law, and set the allowance aside. The Bonavero Institute at Oxford, reviewing 379 media reports of such cases from January to June 2025, found more than 75 per cent of the reporting misleading, rarely clarifying that the cited reason was not the basis of the decision or that the decision had been overturned. The scale beside the anecdotes: the Home Office records 5,732 returns of foreign-national offenders in calendar 2025, and, across April 2008 to June 2021, 6,042 of 21,521 deportation appeals allowed, about 40 per cent of allowances on human-rights grounds.

The second direction is not an allegation but a document. On November 17, 2025, the Home Secretary, Shabana Mahmood, announced in Restoring Order and Control that refugee status would become temporary—30 months’ leave, regularly reviewed—with a “Core Protection” route carrying a 20-year wait for settlement and a faster “Protection Work and Study” route; a consultation paper the same month (CP 1448) proposed a ten-year baseline for settlement generally and drew 204,667 responses. On November 2, 2026, Ms Mahmood gave the final framework to the Commons, and the government’s response (CP 1707) prints the tiers: refugees arriving by designated safe and legal routes qualify for settlement in five years; refugees on Core Protection in twenty years; those who move onto Protection Work and Study in ten years counted from the move, not from arrival—the paper’s own worked example reaches eligibility at eleven years; and a further ten years is added for illegal entry, by small boat or otherwise, except where Article 31 of the Refugee Convention protects the entrant—taking an illegal entrant outside work or study to thirty years. “The standard qualifying period for all refugees who have not come by a safe and legal route will become 20 years,” the Home Secretary told the Commons. “A further 10 years will be added for those who entered the country by an illegal method, such as by small boat.” The framework is announced policy, not law: the Immigration Rules are expected to be laid in December 2026 and implemented during 2027, and settlement is to be assessed under the rules in force when the application is decided.

Europe’s tier is older and runs the other way: it is a privilege, conferred by nationality and date of flight. The European Union’s Temporary Protection Directive, adopted in 2001 and never used, was activated for the first time on March 4, 2022 (Council Implementing Decision 2022/382) for people displaced from Ukraine. It conferred, without any individual asylum examination, a residence permit, access to employment, housing, social welfare, medical care, and schooling for children—the rights an asylum seeker in the ordinary procedure waits months or years to approach, under restricted conditions and sometimes in detention. Some 4.37 million people held temporary protection in the EU at September 30, 2026 (Eurostat), and the Council has extended the regime to March 4, 2028. The Irish Times named the arrangement in a headline of May 22, 2023: “Europe’s two-tier asylum system: doors open for Ukrainians as others seeking refuge face barriers.”

The third direction is the critique of what the ordinary procedure does to the people inside it. Operation Nexus, begun by the Metropolitan Police and the Home Office in October 2012, placed immigration officers in sixteen police custody suites to status-check arrested foreign nationals; the Independent Chief Inspector of Borders and Immigration reported removals of foreign nationals more than doubling, from 418 in 2011–12 to 1,077 in 2013–14. An openDemocracy investigation in 2015 described Nexus as introducing “a two tier system of policing (migrant vs non migrant) where public scrutiny and accountability are virtually nonexistent,” and University of Bristol research found the operation classifying people on spent, petty, or non-conviction material, with about 3,000 people removed under it between 2012 and 2015; the Home Office’s own description of Nexus remains the targeting of “high harm” offenders, and it has not accepted the critique. Below the procedure sits the subsistence: asylum support under section 95 of the Immigration and Asylum Act 1999 pays £49.18 a week in self-catered accommodation and £9.95 a week in catered accommodation, ends 21 days after a final refusal, and, on a grant, runs through a 42-day move-on period before mainstream benefits and housing must take over. The Law Society of Scotland’s evidence to the Scottish Parliament in 2017 documented the destitution that gathers at those edges. The three usages share a word and nothing else: a claim about favour, a statute about routes, and a claim about abandonment, each standing on a different record.

8. The Official Position, Stated Whole

The Prime Minister’s answer was given on the day the phrase entered the riot week, and it has not changed. Sir Keir Starmer, after the Cobra meeting of August 5, 2024: “There is no two-tier policing. There is policing without fear or favour—exactly as it should be, exactly what I would expect and require. So that is a non-issue.” Two years on, at Prime Minister’s Questions on June 3, 2026, facing the Nowak case, he held both parts of the position at once: there are “serious questions to answer, including how accusations of racism informed police thinking,” the investigation should get to the bottom of them—and he did not believe there is two-tier policing, and was shocked at the case’s use in that cause. On free speech, asked about the Connolly case on May 21, 2025, he said he was “strongly in favour of free speech” and equally against incitement to violence.

The Commissioner’s answer was given two days later, and it names the cost he says the claim imposes. Sir Mark Rowley, Metropolitan Police Commissioner, August 7, 2024: “It’s complete nonsense. We have commentators from either end of the political spectrum who like to throw accusations of bias at the police because we stand in the middle, we operate independently under the law without fear or favour.” And: “The serious voices who echo those are of more concern to me, because the risk is they legitimise it, and they legitimise the violence that the officers I’m sending on mutual aid today will face on the streets. They are putting them at risk by suggesting that any of those officers are going out with any intent other than to operate without fear or favour in protecting communities.” The operational form of the same answer belongs to his public-order lead, Assistant Commissioner Matt Twist, in 2024: “In public order policing we are neutral as to the cause that is being protested. We base policing tactics on the threat, harm and risk based on the information and intelligence available to us. In that sense there is no such thing as ‘two-tier or differential policing’—there are in fact an infinite number of tiers of policing, depending on the threat, harm and risk.”

The Attorney General’s answer is the strongest language any official has used, and it is aimed at the comparison itself. Lord Hermer, in his first major interview, to the BBC on June 27, 2025, called claims of two-tier justice “disgusting” and “wrong,” and said politicians using the phrase should think about the “dangers” they pose to the country’s essential institutions: “What some people were seeking to do, bringing up ‘two-tier’, was to make a comparison with the way that people were being treated for trying to kill police officers—and I want to reiterate that, kill police officers—with the response to protests on the streets of London.” It was “frankly disgusting” to draw that comparison. “I think it’s offensive to our police. It’s offensive to our crown prosecutors who are trying to apply the law in the best faith. It is offensive to the courts, where independent judges are applying the law to reach the right sentences. We don’t have a two-tiered justice system. We have one justice system, that is an independent justice system… and I think we all need to get behind it, not seek to undermine it.”

The one institutional finding on the claim itself is Parliament’s, and it is a finding about the riots, not about everything. The Home Affairs Committee, Police response to the 2024 summer disorder (HC 381, April 14, 2025): “This was not protest. Those participating in disorder were not policed more strongly because of their supposed political views but because they were throwing missiles, assaulting police officers and committing arson. It was disgraceful to see the police officers who bore the brunt of this violence being undermined by baseless claims of ‘two-tier policing’.” Its chair, Dame Karen Bradley: “There is a difference in how police must deal with violence and how they deal with peaceful protests… Organised disorder is rightly met with a robust response; any implied equivalence with planned non-violent protests is simply wrong.” The committee found the police response “entirely appropriate.” Beside the finding stand the two absences this report has recorded: the inspectorate has issued no verdict on the claim (Section 2), and the Independent Office for Police Conduct has no general position—it investigates cases, of which the Nowak case, since July 2026, is one. The single point on which the official side has moved since 2024 is documentary: the Police Anti-Racism Commitment’s wording, disowned by the Policing Minister in June 2026 and under review by the chiefs who issued it. On everything else, the official position is the position of August 2024, restated: without fear or favour, and the claim a non-issue—held, since June of this year, alongside an apology to a dead student’s family and four open inquiries into how he was treated.

The Open Record at November 16, and the Disagreements This Report Does Not Resolve

The open files are dated, and most of them close in 2027. The IOPC’s Nowak investigation continues, with conclusion expected early in 2027; the Chalk review’s first phase reports by March 2027; the Article 2 inquest into Henry Nowak’s death opens on September 20, 2027, and Digwa’s further weapons proceedings, and his application to appeal his conviction, stand in the same year’s lists. Isabel Vaughan-Spruce’s trial under the national buffer-zone law is set for January 25–29, 2027; Päivi Räsänen’s application is before the European Court of Human Rights. The NPCC’s review of the Anti-Racism Commitment’s wording has no published end date. The earned-settlement Immigration Rules are expected in December 2026, for implementation during 2027. The disparity tables will be republished on their annual cycles, and will be cited, on the pattern of the years this report covers, by both sides.

The disagreements printed unresolved, in the house manner. First, the phrase’s origin: its 1981 Brixton life is asserted by its 2026 chroniclers and documented nowhere the desk could reach; its 1998 Northern Ireland life is documented and means something else. Second, the injury count for the Southampton disorder of June 2, 2026: eleven officers in the force’s reporting, twelve in some court reporting; the desk prints the force’s figure in the text and the discrepancy here. Third, the date of Lucy Connolly’s sentence: the Court of Appeal’s judgment gives October 31, 2024, and the Press Association and BBC reported the hearing on October 17; the desk prints October 2024. Fourth, the reading of the disparity tables: the raw sentence-length gaps and the controlled null result are both in the Ministry’s compendium, and this report has printed them together, as the Ministry does; which of them answers the two-tier question is the argument the country is having, and it is not settled by anything published to date. The final paragraph is the first paragraph’s restatement, as the form requires: a phrase that began as a Northern Ireland policing design is now the name of four arguments—about protests, about sentences, about speech, and about money and migration—each with its own record, its own strongest exhibits, and its own official answer, and the desk files all of it, attributed and graded, and weighs none of it.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
