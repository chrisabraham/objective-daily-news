Two Words in Daily Use, Filed From the Record

Objective

Dictator and Authoritarian: The Definitions, the President's Own Words, and the Record of the Labels in American Politics

The words are in daily use in the argument over the Trump administration. This report files the record: the definitions in the reference books, the president’s three public answers to the label in his own words, his critics’ words beside them, the executive-order count kept by the American Presidency Project, and the history of labels adopted by the people they were aimed at.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0900 ET, Wednesday, November 25, 2026.

The words dictator and authoritarian are in daily use in the American argument over President Donald Trump and his administration. His critics use the words as descriptions. His supporters use them in quotation marks, as a label applied to him. The president himself has taken the first of the words up in public three times on the record, and answered it each time in his own words. This report files the record in the order it stands: what the reference books say the words mean, what the president has said about the label, what his critics have said in theirs, the count of the executive orders on which much of the argument turns, and the documented history of political labels adopted by the people they were aimed at. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

The Definitions in the Reference Books

The Encyclopaedia Britannica defines a dictatorship as a “form of government in which one person or a small group possesses absolute power without effective constitutional limitations.” The entry adds that dictators usually gain power by force or fraud and maintain it “through the use of intimidation, terror, and the suppression of basic civil liberties.” The word itself descends from the Latin dictator, the title of an emergency magistrate of the Roman Republic: an officer appointed for a stated task in a crisis, holding complete authority for a term limited to six months, and expected to lay the office down when the crisis passed. The traditional type of the office is Cincinnatus, appointed in 458 BC, who by Livy’s account defeated the Aequi and resigned after sixteen days, returning to his farm.

Britannica defines authoritarianism as “the blind submission to authority and the repression of individual freedom of thought and action.” Authoritarian regimes, the entry says, “are systems of government that have no established mechanism for the transfer of executive power and do not afford their citizens civil liberties or political rights,” in which power “is concentrated in the hands of a single leader or a small elite.” Britannica defines totalitarianism, the third word in the family, as a “form of government that theoretically permits no individual freedom and that seeks to subordinate all aspects of individual life to the authority of the state.”

The two words in daily use are not synonyms in the reference books. Authoritarianism names the wider class of system. Dictatorship names the concentration of absolute power in one person or a small group. The political scientist Juan Linz, in his 1964 description of authoritarian regimes, set out four qualities: limited, not responsible, political pluralism; distinctive mentalities in place of an elaborate and guiding ideology; no intensive or extensive political mobilization; and power exercised within formally ill-defined limits that are actually quite predictable. The distinction the literature draws is one of scope and agency: authoritarianism describes the system, its closed political space and the rights it does not afford; dictatorship identifies the holder, the person or small group in whom unconstrained power sits.

The later scholarship worked the boundary between the two words. Carl J. Friedrich and Zbigniew Brzezinski, in Totalitarian Dictatorship and Autocracy (1956), described the totalitarian form as six mutually supporting traits: an official ideology, a single mass party typically led by one man, a terroristic secret police, monopolies of communications and of weapons, and a centrally directed economy. Steven Levitsky and Lucan A. Way named the modern hybrid competitive authoritarianism (2002): formal democratic institutions exist and are the main route to power, but incumbents’ abuse of the state skews the playing field—competition that is real but unfair. Andreas Schedler’s neighboring term is electoral authoritarianism: multiparty elections held under tight authoritarian control. Sergei Guriev and Daniel Treisman, in Spin Dictators (2022), divide modern dictators into dictators of fear, the twentieth-century pattern of intimidation, and dictators of spin, who rule by manipulating information while imitating democracy. In the entries and the literature alike, dictator is the name of a person; authoritarianism is the name of a condition of a system.

The President’s Own Words

On December 5, 2023, at a Fox News town hall in Davenport, Iowa, Sean Hannity put the label to the candidate directly. Hannity said that the media were running headlines about a Trump dictatorship and asked, “Under no circumstances, you are promising America tonight, you would never abuse power as retribution against anybody?” Trump answered: “Except for day one.” Hannity asked, “Meaning?” Trump said: “I want to close the border and I want to drill, drill, drill.” He continued, in the same exchange: “He says, ‘You’re not going to be a dictator, are you?’ I said: ‘No, no, no, other than day one.’ We’re closing the border and we’re drilling, drilling, drilling. After that, I’m not a dictator.”

On August 25, 2025, in the Oval Office, signing executive orders—including one directing National Guard quick-reaction forces for law enforcement, in the month he had deployed the Guard to Washington and placed the District’s police under federal control—Trump took up the label himself. He gave the reaction to the deployment as its opponents stated it: “Freedom, freedom. He’s a dictator, he’s a dictator.” Then he answered it: “A lot of people are saying, ‘Maybe we like a dictator.’ I don’t like a dictator. I’m not a dictator. I’m a man with great common sense and a smart person.”

On January 21, 2026, at a reception with business leaders in Davos, Switzerland, after his address to the World Economic Forum, the president returned to the word, in remarks transcribed in the Public Papers of the Presidents. “We had a good speech. We got great reviews,” he said. “Usually, they say, ‘He’s a horrible, dictator-type person.’ I’m a dictator. But sometimes you need a dictator. But they didn’t say that in this case. And no, it’s common sense. It’s all based on common sense, you know. It’s mostly—let’s say, 95 percent common sense, and that’s what we have.”

The Critics’ Words

On November 30, 2023, the Washington Post published an essay by Robert Kagan under the headline “A Trump dictatorship is increasingly inevitable. We should stop pretending.” It opened: “Let’s stop the wishful thinking and face the stark reality: There is a clear path to dictatorship in the United States, and it is getting shorter every day.”

In December 2023, former Representative Liz Cheney, promoting her book, warned that the country was “sleepwalking into dictatorship.” Her phrase was played back to Trump in the clips Hannity ran at the opening of the Davenport town hall.

The same night as the town hall, the Biden campaign answered the exchange in a statement from its campaign manager, Julie Chávez Rodríguez: “Donald Trump has been telling us exactly what he will do if he’s reelected and tonight he said he will be a dictator on day one. Americans should believe him.”

The Executive-Order Count

Much of the argument runs on the pace of executive action, and the count is kept by the American Presidency Project at the University of California, Santa Barbara, whose table runs from George Washington to the present and counts presidents of both parties by the same instrument. The modern entries, as of November 20, 2026, read: Barack Obama, 276 executive orders in two terms, an average of 35 a year; Donald Trump, first term, 220 orders, an average of 55 a year; Joseph Biden, 162 orders, an average of 41 a year; Donald Trump, second term, 293 orders in 1.83 years, an average of 160 a year. The second term’s orders run from number 14147; number 14371 closed calendar 2025, which is 225 orders in that year alone.

The Record of Labels Adopted by Their Targets

The adoption of an opponent’s label by the people it was aimed at has a record of its own, and it is long. Yankee Doodle began, by tradition, as a British song of the French and Indian War mocking American colonists as bumpkins; the lyrics are attributed by tradition to Dr. Richard Shuckburgh, a British army surgeon, about 1755. The colonists took the song up, made it the unofficial anthem of the Continental Army, and by tradition it was played at the British surrender at Yorktown in 1781.

On July 4, 1948, at Belle Vue in Manchester, the British health minister Aneurin Bevan said in a speech that the Conservatives were “lower than vermin.” Young Conservatives answered by founding the Vermin Club, wearing chrome rat badges, and conferring ranks—“vile vermin” for recruiting ten members, “very vile vermin” for twenty-five. Membership reached between 105,000 and 120,000. Margaret Thatcher was a member, and recalled the episode in her memoirs. Herbert Morrison, a Labour minister, said the remark did more to make Conservatives work and vote than Conservative Central Office could have done.

On September 9, 2016, at an LGBT for Hillary fundraiser in New York City, Hillary Clinton said: “You know, to just be grossly generalistic, you could put half of Trump’s supporters into what I call the basket of deplorables. Right? They’re racist, sexist, homophobic, xenophobic, Islamophobic—you name it.” She called some of those voters “irredeemable,” and the next day expressed regret for saying “half.” Trump’s supporters put the word Deplorable on T-shirts and wore it at rallies, and the label remained in use by its targets for the rest of the campaign.

The Record as It Stands

The definitions are in the reference books, and they distinguish the wider system from the single holder of absolute power. The president’s own words on the label are three, and they are printed above in full context: the day-one answer in Davenport in 2023, the common-sense answer in the Oval Office in 2025, and the Davos answer in 2026. The critics’ words are printed above beside them, dated and sourced the same way. The executive-order count is the American Presidency Project’s, kept by one instrument across administrations of both parties. The history of labels adopted by their targets runs from a British marching song to a chrome rat badge to a campaign T-shirt. The record stands as filed.
