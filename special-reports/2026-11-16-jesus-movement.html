Special Report

The Movement of 1967–1977 and the Claim of a New One, Filed from the Record—California, Chicago, Britain, France, China, Korea, and the Global Counts

Objective

The Jesus Movement and the New Jesus Movement — Special Report

The Jesus Movement is a documented thing with dates: it began in California missions and coffeehouses in 1967, put its name on the cover of Time on June 21, 1971, filled the Cotton Bowl in June 1972, built communes, newspapers, and a music industry, and was absorbed by the late 1970s into church networks—Calvary Chapel and the Vineyard—that stand today. Since February 2023 the name has been carried again, by a film, by ministries, by advocates, and by headline writers, and applied to present events: a sixteen-day prayer meeting at a Kentucky university, rising baptism counts in several denominations, record Bible sales, a French catechumenate that has tripled in ten years, and survey findings that point in both directions at once. This report files the historical record first, in the order it happened, and then the present-tense record country by country, every figure with its instrument named—including the British survey at the center of the “quiet revival” claim, which its own makers withdrew in March 2026. Where the record settles a claim, the report prints the settlement. Where it does not—the size of the crowd at Explo ’72, the number of Christians in China, the role of one preacher two movements wrote out of their histories—the report prints the question as open.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1820 ET, Monday, November 16, 2026.

The Record in Brief

The first Jesus Movement is a closed historical file, and its outlines are not in dispute. Between 1967 and the late 1970s, a population of young converts—many of them drawn from the counterculture the movement’s own preachers had come out of—built an identifiable religious world in the United States: street missions and coffeehouses in San Francisco’s Haight-Ashbury, a fast-growing church at Costa Mesa in Orange County, communes across the country, an underground press of some fifty newspapers, a music that became an industry, and a mass gathering in Dallas in June 1972 that drew the honorary chairmanship of Billy Graham. Time put the phenomenon on its cover on June 21, 1971, under the line “The Jesus Revolution,” five years after the same magazine had asked on its cover whether God was dead. The movement’s standard scholarly periodization, in the work of the historian Larry Eskridge, runs from 1966 to 1977. What ended it, on the record, was absorption: its people, its music, and its converts passed into durable institutions—the Calvary Chapel network, the Vineyard churches that split from it, and the contemporary Christian music industry—and the name went out of use as the name of a living thing.

The present-tense use of the name is a separate record, and it begins as a publishing and film event before it begins as anything else. Greg Laurie’s book Jesus Revolution appeared in 2018 with the subtitle “How God Transformed an Unlikely Generation and How He Can Do It Again Today.” The film of the same name, released by Lionsgate on February 24, 2023, took in $54.3 million worldwide on a reported $15 million budget, and its director, Jon Erwin, said in the film’s promotion that a movement was going on in America now and that there could be another Jesus revolution today. In the same month the film opened, a routine chapel service at Asbury University in Wilmore, Kentucky, ran continuously for sixteen days and drew visitors from hundreds of campuses. Since then the name and its synonyms—revival, outpouring, awakening—have been applied to campus ministries, mass baptism events, and survey results. No organization in any country bears the name Jesus Movement as its own. The label is applied by participants, advocates, and media; it is not self-carried, and the desk files its provenance as part of the record in Section 10.

The measured present-tense record is genuinely mixed, and this report prints it mixed. Specific instruments in specific countries register real increases: adult baptisms at Easter in France have tripled in ten years on the French bishops’ annual survey, the hardest institutional series in this file; the Southern Baptist Convention’s own church profile counts five consecutive years of rising baptisms; the Church of England’s Statistics for Mission records adult baptisms at 9,900 in 2025 and a fifth consecutive year of attendance growth; print Bible sales in the United States reached a twenty-one-year high in 2025; the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints reported more convert baptisms in 2025 than in any year in its history. The same years, on other instruments of equal standing, register the long decline continuing: the Pew Research Center’s Religious Landscape Study counts Christians at 62 percent of American adults, down from 78 percent in 2007; the Southern Baptist membership total fell for the nineteenth consecutive year in the same profile that counted the baptisms; French infant baptisms have fallen by more than half since 2000; and the British survey that supplied the “quiet revival” headlines was withdrawn by its makers in March 2026 after its polling firm admitted its quality controls had never been switched on. Both ledgers are filed below, instrument by instrument. The question the two halves of this report leave standing—whether the increases amount to a movement, and whether the movement they would amount to is the one whose name they have been given—is printed as open in Section 20, with the instruments named that could settle it.

1. San Francisco: The Living Room and the First Missions, 1967

The movement’s first address was a coffeehouse a block from the corner of Haight and Ashbury. In the summer of 1967, as an estimated 75,000 to 100,000 young people arrived in San Francisco’s Haight-Ashbury district—the figure is an estimate, and the record carries it as a range—a small evangelical mission organization took shape among the newcomers. Its principals were Ted Wise, a former drug user converted in late 1965 after a series of LSD experiences, and John MacDonald, pastor of Mill Valley Baptist Church, a Wheaton College graduate of 1943 and a classmate of Billy Graham. Wise, MacDonald, and their associates formed Evangelical Concerns, Inc. in the summer of 1967 and opened a coffeehouse called the Living Room on Page Street, a block from Haight and Ashbury, in the late summer of that year. The Living Room was a mission in the form the district understood: coffee, conversation, and conversion offered to runaways and street people, staffed in part by converts from the same streets.

The San Francisco work built the movement’s first commune and exported its most consequential preacher. In the summer of 1967 the Wises and several other couples established the House of Acts, a communal household in Novato, north of the city, where converts lived together under instruction. The Living Room closed in early 1969, and the Haight work faded as the district itself declined. Among the young men who passed through the Living Room circle was Lonnie Frisbee, a runaway and acid-scene preacher whom Wise met on a Haight street corner and who carried the Haight form of the message—direct address, communal living, baptism as public spectacle—south to Orange County in 1968. The San Francisco missions are the movement’s documented point of origin; the movement’s scale was made three hundred and eighty miles away, in Costa Mesa.

2. Costa Mesa: Calvary Chapel, Chuck Smith, and the Beach Baptisms

Calvary Chapel of Costa Mesa was a small Foursquare church when the hippies arrived. Chuck Smith became its pastor in December 1965, beginning with a congregation of twenty-five people. In 1968 the church broke from the Foursquare denomination. In March 1968 Smith took eighteen-year-old Lonnie Frisbee and his wife Connie into his own home; Frisbee, with John Higgins, ran a commune called the House of Miracles, and the converts began arriving in numbers the church had not seen: young people from the beaches and the counterculture, baptized in mass ceremonies in the Pacific at Pirate’s Cove in Corona del Mar. Frisbee served on the church’s staff in 1970 and 1971 and departed in 1971. The Costa Mesa church became the mother church of a fellowship of affiliated congregations; the Calvary Chapel Association was formalized in 2012, and the association’s own history counts more than 1,700 affiliated congregations worldwide, where secondary accounts put the figure at more than 1,500 by 2009—the desk prints the count as the association’s, with the discrepancy standing.

The Costa Mesa synthesis is the movement’s durable institutional invention. Smith supplied the structure—a verse-by-verse teaching church, denominational in everything but name, that made room for the converts’ dress, music, and communal households without adopting the counterculture’s politics or its theology of experience. Frisbee supplied the converts. The beach baptisms at Corona del Mar became the movement’s signature image, carried in the national press and, fifty years later, restaged deliberately: the Baptize SoCal event of 2023 was held at Pirate’s Cove, in the organizers’ stated framing, to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the Jesus Movement. What Costa Mesa built outlasted the movement that filled it; Section 9 files the absorption.

3. Lonnie Frisbee: The Record and Its Erasures

On the eyewitness record, Frisbee was the catalyst of both movements he served. Participants in Costa Mesa in 1968–71 and in the early Vineyard a decade later credited Frisbee’s preaching with the conversions that filled both. The documentary Frisbee: The Life and Death of a Hippie Preacher, made by David Di Sabatino in 2005 and broadcast on PBS in 2007, assembled those accounts and was nominated for an Emmy. On May 11, 1980—Mother’s Day—Frisbee spoke at John Wimber’s Calvary Chapel in Yorba Linda, and participant accounts on both sides of the later Calvary–Vineyard division describe that evening as the point at which Wimber’s congregation turned toward the charismatic practices that defined the Vineyard. The desk prints the catalytic role as the standard participant account, and prints beside it the fact that the official histories of Calvary Chapel and of the Vineyard long minimized or omitted him; the role is contested between the eyewitnesses and the institutions, and the contest is part of the record.

The reason for the omission is also documented, and the desk prints it at the same grade. Frisbee functioned as an evangelical preacher while privately living as a gay man. His early testimony at Calvary Chapel had mentioned leaving a homosexual lifestyle; that portion was later removed from the testimony as circulated. He was removed from leadership, and, in the formulation of the historians who recovered his story, he was written out of the official histories of the two movements he helped found. Connie Frisbee divorced him in 1973. He died on March 12, 1993, at the age of forty-three, of complications of AIDS. The 2023 film Jesus Revolution, which dramatizes the Costa Mesa years with Frisbee as a principal character, omits his sexuality entirely; the filmmakers stated that the principals did not know of it during the years depicted, a claim the OC Weekly’s 2005 cover story on Frisbee and the recollections it gathered stand against. The desk prints the film’s account and the earlier record side by side and settles neither.

4. The Underground Press and Jesus Music

The movement named itself in print before the national press named it on a cover. The scholarly attribution, in Stephen Hunt’s 2008 account, credits Duane Pederson with coining the terms “Jesus people” and “Jesus movement” in his writings. Pederson’s Hollywood Free Paper published its first issue in October 1969—a Fuller Seminary exhibit dates it October 7; some period sources say November—with a first run of 10,000 copies that Pederson handed out himself. By 1971 the paper claimed a circulation of some 200,000, and the ministry claimed one million copies for the special edition of January 1, 1971, distributed at the Rose Parade; the million is a ministry claim, unverified against any print record, and the desk files it as claimed. The paper ran from 1969 to 1978 and continued as Visit in 1979 and 1980. Berkeley’s Right On!, published by the Christian World Liberation Front, debuted on July 1, 1969, with 20,000 copies. At the peak, Time counted some fifty Jesus papers in circulation.

Jesus music followed the same path from the street to an industry, on a dated record. Larry Norman released Upon This Rock on Capitol Records in 1969 and stands in the record as the father of Christian rock. Calvary Chapel launched its own label, Maranatha! Music, in 1971, to record the music its converts were writing. CCM Magazine, the trade publication of the resulting industry, began publication in July 1978. By the 1980s the music the movement had made in communes and coffeehouses was a multimillion-dollar industry with its own labels, charts, and award shows—contemporary Christian music, a category name that outlived the movement that created the product. Section 14 files the same industry’s audience figures in the present tense, measured by Luminate half a century later.

5. Explo ’72 and the Mainstreaming

The movement’s largest single gathering was organized by its most establishment sponsor. Explo ’72, held in Dallas from June 12 to 17, 1972, was a Campus Crusade for Christ production—Bill Bright’s organization, directed on the ground by Paul Eshleman—with a stated goal of 100,000 student delegates. Daytime attendance was estimated at 80,000 to 85,000, trained in sessions across the city and gathered nightly in the Cotton Bowl. Billy Graham served as honorary chairman and spoke six times. Graham’s own road to Dallas is documented by the historian Larry Eskridge in Church History (1998): Graham encountered the movement at the Rose Parade on January 1, 1971, returned the Jesus People’s raised-index-finger “One Way” gesture, made the Jesus Revolution a motif of his 1971 and 1972 crusades, and published The Jesus Generation in 1971. President Richard Nixon sought an invitation to Explo ’72, on John Turner’s account of the episode. The week closed on Saturday, June 17, with an all-day Jesus Music Festival whose bill—Johnny Cash, Kris Kristofferson, Larry Norman, Love Song, and André Crouch among the performers—is itself a record of the mainstreaming.

The closing crowd is this report’s first exhibit of a claim growing in the retelling. Contemporary estimates of the festival crowd run from 100,000 to 200,000. Cru, the successor organization to Campus Crusade, states in its later memorial tribute to the event that 280,000 attended. No count was taken at the gates of an open field, and no single figure can be verified; the desk prints the contemporary range and the later figure side by side, attributed, and files the gap between them in the open column. Historians of the event add a qualification that belongs in the same paragraph: the young people who filled Dallas in June 1972 were in their majority mainstream evangelical youth from churches and campus ministries, not the countercultural Jesus People of the Haight and Costa Mesa. Explo ’72 is associated with the movement in every history and was, in its attendance, a different and larger population—the moment the movement’s forms were taken up by the evangelical mainstream that would absorb them.

6. The Communes: Jesus People USA and the Survivors

The communal movement was the movement’s social form, and one commune outlived the era. Jesus People USA began as Jesus People Milwaukee, founded in February 1971 under Jim Palosaari. A traveling team separated from the Milwaukee work in 1972 and settled in Chicago in January 1973, in the Uptown neighborhood, where the community remains. Its institutions accumulated over decades: the Resurrection Band, a rock group formed inside the commune; Cornerstone magazine, published from 1971 to 2003; and the Cornerstone Festival, which drew the movement’s dispersed tribes annually for a generation. In 1989 the community joined the Evangelical Covenant Church, a historic denomination—the counterculture’s commune filing itself, voluntarily, inside the institutional church. Current membership is estimated in the sources between roughly 200 and 500; the desk prints the range. Jesus People USA stands in the record as one of the few communes of the era still operating, its survival itself a datum about which forms lasted: the ones that acquired property, payrolls, denominational standing, and a second generation.

The wider communal record is mostly a record of endings. Hundreds of Jesus People communes formed in the early 1970s—houses of a dozen to several hundred converts, holding property in common, under the authority of a pastor or elder board. The great majority dissolved within the decade, from financial failure, leadership collapse, or the ordinary pressure of members marrying and taking jobs. The desk files the pattern without a census, because no census was taken: the communes are documented individually in local histories and denominational records, and the movement’s institutional survivors are the exceptions that prove the shape of the whole—a Chicago commune inside a denomination, a Costa Mesa church that became a network, and a music industry. Britain’s entry in the same form, filed in the next section, carries the era’s darkest communal record in full.

7. Britain in the Same Years: Renewal, Blessitt, and Bugbrooke

Britain’s documented movement of these years ran under a different name, and the desk keeps the names separate. The British phenomenon contemporary with the American Jesus People was the charismatic renewal: the Fountain Trust, founded in 1964 by Michael Harper, carried charismatic practice into the historic churches, and the Church of England’s General Synod took up the renewal in motions in 1975 and 1976. The American movement’s British echo is thinner and is documented as an echo. A Religious News Service year-end roundup of January 1973 recorded Jesus People at work in the Philippines, Sweden, Spain, and Vietnam. Archbishop of Canterbury Michael Ramsey welcomed the Jesus People with stated warnings. Arthur Blessitt, the Los Angeles street preacher who carried a wooden cross on foot across America, carried it through Europe and the British Isles in the same years. The distinction matters to this file: charismatic renewal is adjacent record, filed in Britain’s established churches; the Jesus People label in Britain attached to smaller, imported, and communal forms—one of which produced the record that follows.

The Jesus Fellowship at Bugbrooke is Britain’s principal Jesus Movement institution, and its record ends in a redress scheme. The fellowship was founded in 1969 at Bugbrooke Baptist Chapel in Northamptonshire by Noel Stanton (1926–2009); it became communal from 1974, was expelled by the Baptist Union in 1986, and launched a street-evangelism arm, the Jesus Army, in 1987. On May 26, 2019, its members voted to dissolve the church, after abuse claims against Stanton and others had accumulated over years. The Jesus Fellowship Redress Scheme’s final report, published September 10, 2024, records 890 applications from 601 individuals and 264 alleged perpetrators, 162 of them leaders; £7.7 million was paid out, and 96 percent of applicants received some redress. The BBC’s documentary Inside the Cult of the Jesus Army followed in 2025. The desk files the Bugbrooke record in the historical half of this report because it belongs there: a movement institution of the era, documented from its founding to its dissolution by its own scheme’s accounting.

8. The Children of God: A Separate Entry

One organization of the era is filed separately, under its own name, on its own record. The Children of God was founded in 1968 at Huntington Beach, California, by David Berg (1919–1994), a former Christian and Missionary Alliance pastor. The name itself was given by news media in late 1969. The group renamed itself the Family of Love in February 1978 and is known today as The Family International. Its internal record is documented in Berg’s own published letters, which instituted the practice the group called “flirty fishing”—sexual recruitment presented as evangelism. The group’s notoriety prompted the formation of FREECOG, the first organized anticult group in the United States, founded by parents of members. In 1995 a British court, in a judgment by Mr Justice Ward, found the group to be an authoritarian cult that had engaged in systematic physical and sexual abuse of children. Berg died in 1994. By 2020 the organization persisted as a mostly online network of some 1,600 members, on the accounting of the scholars Eileen Barker and Sarah Harvey.

The desk states the filing rule plainly, because the era’s literature often breaks it. The Children of God recruited in the same streets, dressed in the same clothes, and used the same vocabulary as the Jesus People, and general accounts of the period regularly merge it into the movement’s story or omit it from the story altogether. The record supports neither treatment. It was a separate organization with a separate founder, a separate doctrine, and a documented record of abuse established in court; it is filed here as its own entry, in the era where it operated, and it is not counted in any figure this report gives for the Jesus Movement.

9. Absorption: Calvary Chapel, the Vineyard, and the Fade

The movement’s afterlife is a denominational history, and its dates are the fade’s dates. In 1974 Kenn Gulliksen, sent out from Calvary Chapel Costa Mesa, planted a Los Angeles church that became the first Vineyard. John Wimber’s Yorba Linda congregation, founded as a Calvary Chapel in 1977, left the Calvary fellowship and became the Anaheim Vineyard in 1982; that year eight churches founded the Association of Vineyard Churches, and some thirty Calvary Chapel congregations followed Wimber’s network in the division. The charismatic practices at issue in the split—the Vineyard’s openness to prophecy, healing prayer, and the gifts of the Spirit as public ministry—entered Wimber’s church through the door Frisbee opened on Mother’s Day 1980, on the participant accounts filed in Section 3. Calvary Chapel, for its part, continued as a loose affiliation of autonomous churches for three more decades before formalizing its association in 2012.

By the late 1970s the named phenomenon was over, and the record shows absorption rather than defeat. Eskridge’s periodization closes the movement in 1977. The converts of 1968 were in their thirties, employed, and raising children inside the churches the movement had built or filled. The communes had mostly dissolved or, like Jesus People USA, taken denominational shelter. The music had become an industry with a trade magazine. The underground press had folded or renamed itself. Nothing in the record shows a collapse, a scandal that ended the whole, or a repudiation by its participants; the movement’s people and forms passed into institutions that dropped the name and kept the converts. That is the baseline against which the present-tense half of this report must be read: the first Jesus Movement is identifiable in retrospect precisely because it left institutions behind it—church networks, a music industry, a surviving commune—that a later observer can count.

10. Three Words: Movement, Revolution, Revival

The historical half of this report runs on three different words, and each word has its own provenance. The first is the participants’ own. “Jesus People” and “Jesus movement” were coined inside the movement, in Duane Pederson’s writings, and carried in its own newspapers from 1969; the people so named applied the words to themselves. The second is the media’s. “Jesus Revolution” entered the national record as a magazine’s cover line—Time, June 21, 1971—over an inside article titled “The New Rebel Cry: Jesus Is Coming!” The movement’s papers had been in print for two years before the cover appeared. The third word is the generic one. “Revival” is the term the contemporary material reaches for—the Asbury gathering, the campus ministries, and the baptism events of the present-tense half are all filed under it by their participants and their press—and it names a category of event, a meeting that runs long or a crowd that responds, where “movement” names a population with institutions. The present-tense claim travels under the second word: the 2018 book and the 2023 film that revived the subject are both titled Jesus Revolution, a media coinage of 1971 carried into the present by a publisher and a studio.

The word a researcher chooses decides the shelf the evidence comes from, and the desk files that as record. A file built on the word “revival” fills with the conference circuit, ministry press releases, and organizer counts—sources that exist to announce events. A file built on the word “movement” fills with history, sociology, and survey instruments—sources that exist to measure populations. The same gathering can stand on both shelves: the Asbury outpouring is a revival in its participants’ vocabulary and, in the instruments’ vocabulary, a sixteen-day event at one university whose attendance the university estimated and whose effect on national affiliation no instrument has registered. The desk has kept the three words in their separate provenances throughout this report, printing “movement” for the population of 1967–1977, “revolution” only where the record itself uses it, and “revival” attributed to the claimants who use it now. A reader who watches the words can watch the evidence change shelves.

11. The Counting Problem: Which Instrument Counts Whom

No two instruments in the present-tense half of this report count the same population, and their headline figures cannot be added together. The Pew Research Center counts self-identification: what adults tell an interviewer they are. On that instrument the world’s Christians numbered 2.3 billion in 2020. The Center for the Study of Global Christianity at Gordon-Conwell counts affiliation as the churches themselves count it—including baptized children and members that surveys miss—and on that instrument the world’s Christians number 2.674 billion in 2026. The two totals differ by hundreds of millions because they answer different questions, and both are printed in Section 18 with their methods named. Denominational instruments count what member organizations report upward: the Southern Baptist Convention’s Annual Church Profile counts what its churches file, and only 61 percent of them filed in 2025; the Official Catholic Directory counts what dioceses report. Event organizers count participation: a person baptized at a synchronized event is one count to the organizer, one baptism to a denomination if a member church reports it, and one self-identification to a survey only if an interviewer later reaches them.

The categories themselves overlap, by design, and the overlap is where the largest headline numbers are manufactured. The Center for the Study of Global Christianity’s 2026 table lists 1.279 billion Catholics, 638 million Protestants, 293 million Orthodox—and, across all of them, 676 million Pentecostals and charismatics and 424 million evangelicals. The charismatic and evangelical figures are cross-cutting movements counted inside the traditions; a Pentecostal Baptist stands in three of the table’s rows at once, and the rows sum to far more than the Christian total. “Evangelical” in an American survey is a self-description that crosses denominations and includes people who attend no church; in the Southern Baptist profile it is a membership roll; in Latin America, evangélico is the ordinary word for a Protestant of any kind. The original movement sits at the head of this problem. In its own papers its people were Jesus People who spoke of following Jesus; the denominational labels were applied afterward, by the churches that absorbed them—Calvary Chapel, the Vineyard, the Covenant Church—and by every instrument that has counted their descendants since. A claim that adds a survey’s evangelicals to a database’s charismatics to an organizer’s baptisms has counted some people three times and measured nothing. The desk has kept each figure inside its own instrument throughout, and the reader should hold any total that crosses instruments at the grade of its weakest part.

12. Asbury, February 2023

The present-tense record opens with an event whose host declined to name it a revival. On Wednesday, February 8, 2023, a routine chapel service at Asbury University, a small Christian university in Wilmore, Kentucky, did not end on schedule. Students remained in Hughes Auditorium—capacity about 1,500, in a town of about 6,000—and prayer and worship continued around the clock for sixteen days. The university’s own word for the event, used consistently in its statements, was “outpouring”; the university declined the word “revival.” President Kevin Brown estimated that 50,000 visitors came to Wilmore over the sixteen days, and the university documented student visitors from 285 colleges and universities. The last public session was held on February 23, 2023, by the university’s decision, and classes and ordinary operations resumed. Asbury had a documented precedent: an extended revival meeting in February 1970 stands in the university’s own history, and the 2023 gathering was measured against it from the first week.

The attendance figure grew after the event ended, and the desk prints the growth. The university’s estimate, given by its president, is 50,000 visitors. Later retellings in revival literature and ministry press give 50,000 to 70,000, and the higher figure circulates without a counter behind it; no gate count exists for an open campus event spread over sixteen days. The desk prints the university’s 50,000 as the instrument of record and the 70,000 as a retelling. In the weeks that followed, extended prayer and worship gatherings were reported at other campuses—Lee University in Tennessee and Samford University in Alabama among them—in each case reported by participants and campus ministries rather than measured by any outside instrument. Asbury is the best-documented event in the present-tense half of this report, and its documentation establishes a bounded thing: sixteen days, one auditorium, tens of thousands of visitors, a host institution that chose a modest word for it. What the event began, if anything, is the question the remaining sections take up instrument by instrument.

13. The United States: The Survey Instruments

The most-cited American evidence for a turn is the Barna Group’s State of the Church research, conducted with the technology company Gloo, and the desk prints it with its construction attached. Barna reported that the share of American adults agreeing that a personal commitment to Jesus remains important today rose from 54 percent in 2021—a thirty-year low in its tracking—to 66 percent in 2025, in findings released April 7, 2025. In a release of September 2, 2025, based on 5,580 online interviews conducted from January through July 2025, Barna reported that churchgoing members of Generation Z attend on average 1.9 weekends a month and Millennials 1.8—a measure of frequency among those who attend, not of the share of the generation that attends at all. On November 6, 2025, Barna reported weekly Bible reading among adults at 42 percent for 2025, up from 30 percent in 2024, on more than 12,000 interviews; the same release reported weekly reading among Generation Z rising from 30 to 49 percent. Barna is a research company serving churches, and Gloo is a technology company serving the same market; the desk names the sponsors because the instruments’ findings travel without them.

The counter-readings were published alongside the findings, by researchers using the same data. The sociologist Ryan Burge, the most prominent published critic of the revival reading, notes that Barna’s commitment question is worded in evangelical vocabulary and that 27 percent of non-Christians answer it yes—a measure of the question’s breadth as much as of the population’s commitment. The Hartford Institute for Religion Research’s Faith Communities Today survey, reported in April 2026 from 7,453 congregations, found the median congregation’s in-person attendance at 70 in 2025, above the pre-pandemic median of 65—the first increase in the survey’s twenty-five years of tracking, and a median, which describes the middle congregation and not the national total. In September 2026 Barna reported that 34 percent of Generation Z and Millennial adults said they were attending church more often than a year earlier, against 45 percent reporting no change and 21 percent attending less. The skeptical ledger proper—Pew’s Religious Landscape Study and Gallup’s membership series, the two largest American instruments—is filed in Section 19, where the tension between the ledgers is left standing.

14. The United States: Baptisms, Bibles, and the Organizers’ Events

The denominations’ own counts show baptisms rising and membership falling inside the same instrument. The Southern Baptist Convention’s Annual Church Profile, compiled by Lifeway, counted 263,075 baptisms in 2025, up 4.96 percent from 250,643 in 2024—the fifth consecutive annual increase, and the highest total since 2016 or 2017; denominational coverage differs by a year on the comparison, and the desk prints both. The same profile counted total membership at 12,331,954, the lowest figure since 1973 and the nineteenth consecutive year of decline, with weekly worship attendance of about 4.46 million, up 3.63 percent, and returns from 61 percent of the convention’s churches. The Catholic record runs parallel. The Official Catholic Directory counted 34,552 adult baptisms and 55,453 receptions into full communion for 2024, against a documented high point of 172,000 adult baptisms and receptions in 1999. An analysis released March 31, 2026 by Hallow, a Catholic prayer-app company, compiled reports from more than 140 of the 175 American dioceses and found the average diocese receiving 38 percent more entrants at Easter 2026 than at Easter 2025. The Archdiocese of Los Angeles reported 8,598 entrants at Easter 2026—2,452 catechumens and 6,146 candidates—in a series that runs 3,462 in 2023, 3,596 in 2024, and 5,587 in 2025. An Archdiocese of Chicago survey of 2,127 initiation participants across twenty dioceses found 34 percent from Generation Z and 35 percent Millennials. A ZENIT analysis of sixteen dioceses with detailed records found all sixteen above their 2024 figures in 2026 and only two above their year-2000 figures—a recovery from recent lows, in its own summary—and the Center for Applied Research in the Apostolate at Georgetown cautioned that the increase reports remained anecdotal until the dioceses’ formal reporting is complete.

Bible sales are the hardest commercial figure in the American record, and they point one way. Circana BookScan, the retail measurement service, counted 9.7 million print Bibles sold in the United States in 2019, 14.2 million in 2023, 17 million in 2024, and 19 million in 2025—a twenty-one-year high, up 12 percent on 2024 and double the 2019 figure, in the accounting of Circana’s Brenna Connor as published by Publishers Weekly on January 9, 2026. September 2025 alone accounted for 2.4 million copies, up 36 percent year over year, in the weeks after the September 10, 2025 assassination of Charlie Kirk; Circana’s analyst attributed the spike to the pattern of Bible buying in moments of national crisis. A sales figure measures purchases, not reading, attendance, or affiliation, and the desk files it as what it is: the strongest single commercial indicator in the file, and a measure of demand for the book rather than of membership in anything.

The campus ministry UniteUS supplies this report’s clearest specimen of a number growing in the retelling, and the desk prints the ladder whole. UniteUS, founded by Tonya Prewett, held its first event at Auburn University’s Neville Arena on September 12, 2023; the Billy Graham Evangelistic Association’s Decision magazine recorded 5,000 students at that gathering and 200 baptized afterward in a nearby lake. By the fall of 2024, Prewett told Decision that more than 70,000 students had gathered on 11 campuses, with about 5,000 saved and close to 2,000 baptized. In October 2025, Grand Canyon University’s newsroom reported more than 125,000 students on 21 campuses and more than 20,000 giving their lives to Jesus. Through the fall of 2026, CBN’s event coverage carried a standing formulation of more than 100,000 students at more than two dozen campuses—a cumulative figure lower than the one the ministry’s ecosystem had published a year earlier. The current biography page of the founder’s husband, on his official site, states that over 175,000 college students have gathered at 30 events, with more than 26,000 giving their life to Christ. No independent audit or campus-by-campus count exists at any point on the ladder. The events themselves continued through the fall of 2026—at Appalachian State on October 13, 2026, the ministry reported more than 700 decisions and about 100 baptisms; at Belmont University on November 10, 2026, it reported 329 decisions—and every figure in this paragraph is the ministry’s own, printed as claimed, with the growth of the cumulative total filed as part of the record.

The synchronized baptism events are counted the same way, by their organizers, and the desk prints their two published totals for the flagship event. Baptize America, organized by Pastor Mark Francey of Oceans Church in California, held its national event on Pentecost Sunday, June 8, 2025. The organizers’ preliminary count, published that night with about 10 percent of participating churches still unreported, was 26,657 baptized and 6,445 recorded salvations across 1,080 events at 650 churches in all fifty states. In their April 2026 press release for the following year’s events, the organizers gave the June 2025 figure as 27,858. The series behind it: Baptize SoCal baptized 4,166 at Pirate’s Cove in 2023, on the organizers’ count; Baptize California counted more than 12,000 statewide on May 19, 2024, with more than 6,100 at Huntington Beach; the Huntington Beach event of May 3, 2025 counted 7,752. On May 24, 2026, the movement ran Baptize the World from a hub at the Museum of the Bible in Washington, and its press release of May 28, 2026 reported 17,910 baptisms in 49 countries through more than 1,600 churches, with the total still climbing as reports arrived. Every figure in this paragraph is an organizer’s count, unverified by any outside instrument, and is printed as claimed. The music industry closes the American file: Luminate, the entertainment measurement company, reports that listeners who are Millennials or younger rose from 39 percent of the American Christian and gospel audience in the early 2020s to 45 percent in 2024, and that United States Christian and gospel on-demand audio streams grew 18.5 percent in 2025, to 30 billion—the 1970s movement’s own industry, measured by a commercial instrument, skewing younger.

15. Britain: The Quiet Revival and Its Withdrawal

The British claim rests on a single survey, and the survey has been withdrawn by its makers. In April 2025 the Bible Society published The Quiet Revival, built on two YouGov surveys—more than 19,000 adults in 2018 and more than 13,000 in late 2024, drawn from an opt-in online panel. The report found adults in England and Wales who identify as Christian and attend church at least monthly rising from 8 percent in 2018 to 12 percent in 2024; among eighteen-to-twenty-four-year-olds, from 4 percent to 16 percent, with young men at 21 percent; and among monthly churchgoers aged eighteen to twenty-four, Catholics at 41 percent against Anglicans at 20 percent—the finding carried in headlines as young Catholics outnumbering young Anglicans two to one. The findings were contested from the month of publication. British Social Attitudes, the NatCen survey that uses random probability sampling, shows the same monthly-attendance measure falling from 12 percent to 9 percent over 2018 to 2024. The British Election Study put the figure at 8 percent in 2015 and 6.6 percent in 2024. The Pew Research Center’s analysis concluded that the most robust data do not support a broad young-adult revival, and the sociologist David Voas called the findings too good to be true. The Catholic Church’s own counts for England and Wales show Mass attendance falling from 700,000 in 2019 to 575,500 in 2024. In March 2026 YouGov admitted that key quality-control technologies had not been activated in the 2024 survey—duplicate, out-of-geography, and automated responses had gone unfiltered—and on March 26, 2026 the Bible Society withdrew the report, stating that it “can no longer be regarded as a reliable source of information about the spiritual landscape in Britain”; YouGov’s Stephan Shakespeare apologized for the failure. The Bible Society’s follow-up report, The Quiet Revival One Year On, maintains the original’s core themes while conceding that the scale cannot be substantiated, and rests instead on two surviving indicators: British physical Bible sales, up 106 percent between 2019 and 2025 on the publisher SPCK’s research, and participation in the Alpha course, up 35 percent. YouGov has said it will repeat the study with its controls activated. The 2021 Census stands behind the whole dispute: Christian identification in England and Wales fell below half the population for the first time, to 46.2 percent.

Britain’s hard institutional series, published the week of this report, shows a narrower and real increase. The Church of England’s Statistics for Mission 2025, published November 13, 2026, counted 9,900 baptisms of adults in 2025, up more than 1,000 for the second consecutive year from 8,700 in 2024. Ken Eames of the church’s data team wrote that it may well be the case that the number of adult baptisms in 2025 was higher than it has ever been—with the recorded caveat that the counting basis changed in 2018, when the church stopped counting baptisms of everyone over thirteen as adult baptisms and began counting only those over eighteen. The same statistics record a fifth consecutive year of growth in both Sunday and weekly attendance, the first such run since current records have been kept; a worshipping community of 1,026,000; and average Sunday attendance of 590,000, up 1.6 percent. The same publication records the other direction in the same columns: total baptisms fell to 59,300, infant baptisms fell from 51,000 in 2019 to 24,600 in 2025, and weekly attendance remains 18 percent below its 2019 level. A headline characterization of the adult figure as up by a third, carried in the Christian press on the day of publication, cannot be reconciled to the church’s series—8,700 to 9,900 is about a seventh—and the desk prints the church’s figures. Britain’s record, read whole, is a denomination counting more adults choosing baptism and more people attending than in the pandemic years, inside totals that remain below where they stood before the pandemic and inside a census that records the Christian share of the country below half.

16. Europe: France’s Series and Its Neighbors

France holds the strongest institutional series in this entire report, and it runs in one direction for a decade. The French bishops’ conference has surveyed the catechumenate annually since 2002. Its count of adults baptized at Easter runs: 3,900 in 2015; 4,124 in 2016; 5,463 in 2023; 7,135 in 2024; 10,384 in 2025; and 13,234 in 2026, up 28 percent in the last year and triple the figure of ten years before. Adolescents aged eleven to seventeen baptized at Easter numbered 7,400 in 2025 and 8,152 in 2026. The combined total—21,386 at Easter 2026 against 17,788 at Easter 2025—is the figure that circulates in international coverage, and it counts a different population than the adult figure; the desk prints both, labeled, from the one instrument. Adults aged eighteen to twenty-five are 42 percent of the 2026 adult catechumens, the largest age group; the bishops’ own survey of 1,450 catechumens found 58 percent women. No name from the American record attaches to the French phenomenon: it is a Catholic catechumenate, administered by dioceses, of people who present themselves for preparation and are baptized at the Easter Vigil.

The same country’s other columns run the other way, and the desk prints them in the same section. Total baptisms in France, overwhelmingly of infants, fell from 513,501 in 1980 to 186,165 in 2024, on the Vatican’s statistical yearbook series; Le Monde put baptisms of children under seven at 380,000 in 2000 and 150,000 in 2024. The number of priests in France fell from 38,876 in 1980 to 12,590 in 2024. The national statistics institute’s survey finds 51 percent of French adults aged eighteen to fifty-nine reporting no religion and 29 percent identifying as Catholic. Germany’s bishops’ conference statistics, released in August 2026, show the same two directions at different magnitudes: baptisms of people aged fourteen and over rose 28 percent in 2025, to 2,650, while total baptisms fell from 116,274 to 109,002, 307,149 Catholics formally left the church, membership fell 2.6 percent to 19.2 million, and Mass attendance stood at 6.8 percent of members. Switzerland’s figures, from the Swiss Institute of Pastoral Sociology as analyzed by The Pillar on September 21, 2026 under the headline “Uptick but no ‘boom’,” record 339 Catholic adult baptisms in 2025—about 30 percent more than 2024, a third consecutive annual increase, on a base in the low hundreds—with church exits also falling. Belgium’s church statistics record 536 adult baptisms in 2025, against 362 in 2024 and 180 a decade earlier. On October 22, 2026, the Vatican’s Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith published Venite ad me, a document on the transmission of the faith that cites the French figures and cautions against triumphalism over them, observing that new baptisms do not always lead to lasting commitment; Bishop Matthieu Rougé of Nanterre, in the Paris region where the catechumenate has grown fastest, has said the influx of converts “remains limited and fragile, and should not give rise to any pride or misplaced triumphalism.” Europe’s record is a set of small, real, institutionally counted increases in adult entry, standing inside large, continuing declines in infant baptism, membership, and clergy—and no European instrument in this file uses the words Jesus Movement or revival for any of it.

17. Asia: China’s Range and Korea’s Decline

China’s Christian population cannot be stated as a figure, and the desk prints the range with its instruments attached. The Chinese government’s 2018 white paper on religious freedom counts more than 44 million Christians in registered churches. The Pew Research Center’s estimate for 2010 was 67 million. The Chinese General Social Survey of 2018 found about 2 percent of adults formally identifying as Christian—some 20 million adults. The China Family Panel Studies of the same year found 7 percent of adults saying they believe in Jesus or the Lord of Heaven—some 81 million. Independent and advocacy estimates run from 100 million to 130 million, and the World Christian Database counts about 120 million. A 2025 paper in Socius, drawing on nineteen nationally representative surveys, asks whether Chinese Christian growth has plateaued since 2010. Pew’s 2023 study Measuring Religion in China is the methodological anchor for the whole dispute: the population is real, its size is contested across a sixfold range, and the contest is a property of the instruments—registration counts, identity surveys, belief surveys, and advocacy estimates measure four different things. Hong Kong’s record is narrower and firmer: the diocese’s weekly, as reported by Agenzia Fides on March 13, 2026, counted 2,500 baptisms at Easter 2026—1,600 adults and 900 infants—a figure similar to 2025. Singapore’s diocesan reporting counted about 1,250 catechumens and candidates at Easter 2026, described as its largest group in a decade.

South Korea is the Asian country with a documented revival in its past, and its present record runs the other way on its own instruments. The Pyongyang revival of 1907 stands in Korean church history as the founding event of the country’s Protestant growth; in the following century Korea built some of the largest congregations in the world, Yoido Full Gospel Church in Seoul long cited as the largest of all. Gallup Korea, which has surveyed Korean religion since 1983, records the religious share of the population peaking at 54 percent in 2004 and standing at 40 percent in 2025—18 percent Protestant and 6 percent Catholic on Gallup’s count, where Hankook Research’s 2024 survey gives 20 percent Protestant, 11 percent Catholic, and 51 percent no religion; the desk prints both instruments. Gallup Korea finds 76 percent of Koreans in their twenties reporting no religion. The Catholic bishops’ own data record believers aged twenty-nine and under falling 34 percent in a decade. Korea’s missionary sending, filed in Section 18, continued to grow across the same decades in which its domestic affiliation fell—the two records are both Korea’s, and neither cancels the other.

18. The Global South: Growth Under Other Names

The world’s Christian population is growing and its center has moved, on both of the instruments that count it, and the same instruments record the North Atlantic declining. The Pew Research Center’s 2025 global report, built on more than 2,700 censuses and surveys, counts the world’s Christians rising from 2.1 billion in 2010 to 2.3 billion in 2020 while their share of world population fell from 31 percent to 29 percent; sub-Saharan Africa now holds the largest regional share, 31 percent of all Christians, 697 million people in 2020, up 31 percent in the decade. The Center for the Study of Global Christianity’s Status of Global Christianity 2026 counts 2.674 billion Christians, 32.2 percent of the world; 69 percent of them in the Global South, against 17.6 percent in 1900; about 780 million in Africa, growing at 2.6 percent a year; more than 415 million in Asia; and 676 million Pentecostals and charismatics worldwide. The same Center’s 2026 report, in the same tables, counts Europe’s Christian population at 553 million and falling at 0.41 percent a year, and North America’s at 275 million and falling at 0.16 percent a year, with Christianity worldwide growing at 0.95 percent a year against Islam’s 1.57 percent. The growth and the decline in this paragraph come out of one database, and the desk prints them together, as the database publishes them. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, in its own statistical reports, counted 251,763 convert baptisms in 2023, 308,682 in 2024—the most since 1997—and 385,490 in 2025, the highest single-year total in the church’s history, above the previous record of 330,877 set in 1990, with total membership at 17,887,212 at the end of 2025; the church’s membership is a cumulative count of the baptized who have not resigned, been removed, or reached the age of 110, and the church publishes no activity rate, a definition the desk prints with the figures.

Latin America’s transformation is the largest single religious shift in this report, and it is fifty years old. The Pew Research Center’s 2014 survey of Latin America—more than 30,000 face-to-face interviews in eighteen countries and Puerto Rico—counted 69 percent of adults identifying as Catholic and 19 percent as Protestant, against a historical series of 94 percent Catholic in 1910 and above 90 percent through the 1960s, with the decline beginning in the 1970s; 84 percent of adults had been raised Catholic, and about 65 percent of the region’s Protestants identified with Pentecostal churches. Brazil’s census bureau, IBGE, records the same shift in the region’s largest country: Catholics at 99.7 percent in 1872, above 90 percent in 1970, 65.1 percent in 2010, and 56.7 percent in 2022—100.2 million people—with evangelicals at 26.9 percent in 2022, 47.4 million people, up from 21.6 percent in 2010, outnumbering Catholics in two states, and the unaffiliated at 9.3 percent. The census also records the shift slowing: Brazilian demographers have moved the projected year in which evangelicals might outnumber Catholics from 2032 toward 2049. Pew’s own page for the 2014 study now directs readers to its 2026 follow-up, whose title states its finding: “Catholicism Has Declined in Latin America Over the Past Decade.” The words Jesus Movement appear nowhere in the Latin American record. The growth there is Pentecostal and evangelical, under its own names, in Portuguese and Spanish, and it predates the current American discussion by two generations.

The Global South’s churches are also present in the West as missionaries, on a dated and named record, and their stated purpose is printed as their claim. The Redeemed Christian Church of God, founded in Lagos in 1952 by Josiah Akindayomi and led since 1981 by E. A. Adeboye, arrived in Britain in 1988; British church directories count more than 800 of its parishes in the United Kingdom, and its Festival of Life at London’s ExCeL centre has drawn as many as 40,000 people. The church’s stated aim, in its own words, is a parish within five minutes of every home, “to make heaven and take as many people with us”; its own materials claim parishes in more than 190 countries and more than 50,000 parishes worldwide, figures the desk prints as the church’s claims, noting that its North America office has published lower worldwide totals in other years. Kingsway International Christian Centre, founded in London by the Nigerian pastor Matthew Ashimolowo, stands among the largest single congregations in Britain. In the Anglican Communion the flow of authority has reversed in documented steps: the Church of Nigeria, the communion’s largest province at an estimated 25 million members, established the Convocation of Anglicans in North America in 2006 and consecrated Martyn Minns as a missionary bishop in Abuja on August 20, 2006; he was installed on May 5, 2007 by Archbishop Peter Akinola, against the wishes of the Archbishop of Canterbury. The first GAFCON conference met in Jerusalem from June 22 to 29, 2008—1,184 participants from nineteen provinces—and issued the Jerusalem Declaration; the Anglican Church in North America formed in 2009 with the Nigerian convocation as a founding partner; and in 2025 the Nigerian primate, Henry Ndukuba, announced that his church would sever ties with the Church of England over the appointment of its first female archbishop. South Korea’s churches, whose domestic decline is filed in Section 17, field 21,917 long-term missionaries from 223 agencies in 174 countries, on the Korea World Mission Association’s count for 2023—the second-largest missionary force in the world after the United States, on the mission-research institutes’ accounting. The sending bodies state their purpose plainly: the re-evangelization of a secular West. The desk prints the purpose as their claim and the parishes, consecrations, and missionary counts as the measurable footprint. None of these bodies uses the name Jesus Movement. The name remains what its provenance says it is: an American label of 1969, revived by an American book and film, and the record shows no evidence that the churches growing fastest in the world have adopted it.

19. The Skeptical Ledger: The Same Instruments, Read Whole

The largest American instrument records a leveling. The Pew Research Center’s Religious Landscape Study of 2023–24, published February 26, 2025 from 36,908 respondents, counts Christians at 62 percent of American adults—78 percent in 2007, 71 percent in 2014—and the religiously unaffiliated at 29 percent. Pew’s own headline for the study is that the Christian decline has slowed and may have leveled off: the Christian share has held between 60 and 64 percent since 2019. The same study counts adults aged eighteen to twenty-four at 46 percent Christian and 43 percent unaffiliated, the narrowest gap of any age group. In December 2025 Pew published an analysis titled “Religion Holds Steady in America,” which found no clear evidence of a religious revival among young adults. Gallup’s membership series, running since 1937, counted Americans belonging to a church, synagogue, or mosque at 47 percent in 2020—below half for the first time in the series, against 73 percent in 1937 and about 70 percent as late as 1999. The Public Religion Research Institute’s 2026 Census of American Religion, as reported in the year’s analyses, found no evidence of a broad return to church. These instruments and the instruments of Section 13 were all running in the same years. Barna’s commitment index can rise while Pew’s affiliation share holds flat, because the two measure different things—the intensity of the affiliated and the size of the affiliation—and the desk prints the difference rather than resolving it.

The pattern repeats in every country file in this report, and the desk states it once, plainly. In the Southern Baptist profile, baptisms rose for five years while membership fell for nineteen. In France, adult baptisms tripled in a decade while infant baptisms fell by more than half in a generation. In England, adult baptisms reached 9,900 while total baptisms fell to their lowest recorded level outside the pandemic years. In Germany, baptisms of people over fourteen rose 28 percent while 307,149 members formally left. In the global database, Africa’s Christian population grows at 2.6 percent a year while Europe’s falls at 0.41 percent. The two columns are not in dispute with each other; every figure in this paragraph comes from the same instrument as the figure it stands beside. What is in dispute is only the summary word. “Revival” reads the first column. “Decline” reads the second. The record contains both columns, in full, and the desk’s finding is the record: entry into Christian practice is rising measurably in specific places and populations at the same time as inherited Christian affiliation continues to fall almost everywhere it is measured. Whether those two facts describe one phenomenon, and whether that phenomenon is a movement, are questions the instruments were not built to answer.

20. The Open Questions

The desk closes by naming what would settle the question, and what the record cannot currently show. The British question settles first, on a dated instrument: YouGov has said it will repeat the Bible Society study with its quality controls activated, and its result, read against the next wave of British Social Attitudes, will either restore a measured increase in young British churchgoing or close the claim. The American question settles on Pew’s next Religious Landscape Study, read against Barna’s next State of the Church release, with the two instruments’ different questions kept distinct: affiliation in the one, intensity in the other. The denominational questions settle themselves annually, in the same instruments that raised them—the Southern Baptist Annual Church Profile, the French catechumenate survey, the Church of England’s Statistics for Mission, the German bishops’ statistics—and five more years of those series will show whether the increases of 2023 to 2026 were an inflection or a fluctuation. The deepest open question is retention: whether the adults baptized in France, England, Los Angeles, and Dallas in these years are present in the same instruments five years from now is the measurement the Vatican’s October 2026 document points to, and no instrument in this file yet reports it.

What the record cannot show, the desk prints as plainly as what it can. It cannot show that the measured increases, added across their separate instruments and countries, amount to a single phenomenon; the taxonomy problem of Section 11 stands in the way of the addition itself. It cannot show that the phenomenon, if it is one, is a movement in the sense the historical half of this report documents—a self-named population building institutions that outlast it—because the present-tense population has not named itself, and the institutions it is entering are, in the main, centuries old. It cannot show that the name’s users in any two countries are describing the same thing: the French catechumenate, the Kentucky outpouring, the Nigerian parish network in London, and the Brazilian census describe four records that share a vocabulary of baptism and little else that an instrument can measure. And it cannot show the counterfactual the 1970s comparison invites—what the observers of 1971 would have made of the present record—because the observers of 1971 were themselves working from a magazine cover and a crowd estimate that grew by 80,000 in the retelling. The first Jesus Movement is a closed file: dated, named by its participants, and survived by its institutions. The second is an open one, and this report leaves it open, with the instruments named that will close it.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
