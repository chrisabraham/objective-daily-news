Measles in Canada, the United States, and Mexico, 2024–2026: Where the Outbreak Spread and Who It Reached

On November 17, 2026, the Pan American Health Organization withdrew the measles elimination status of the United States, Mexico, and Bolivia, a year after Canada lost its own. The record shows one virus lineage carried along kinship and migration routes between conservative Mennonite communities in Canada, Texas, Chihuahua, and Bolivia, then outbreaks in other close-knit groups, and then spread into general populations, Indigenous communities above all in Mexico. The hemisphere has counted 58,213 confirmed cases and 82 deaths in 2026, more than half of them in Guatemala.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1000 ET, Wednesday, November 18, 2026.

The Record in Brief

The decision. On Tuesday, November 17, 2026, PAHO Director Jarbas Barbosa accepted the recommendation of the organization’s regional verification commission, which met November 10 to 13, that endemic measles transmission had been reestablished in Bolivia, Mexico, and the United States, and he withdrew the three countries’ elimination status. The commission’s test is mechanical: the same genotype and lineage of the virus circulating without interruption for twelve months or more inside one country. Canada lost its status on that test on November 10, 2025, and the Americas lost theirs as a region on the same day. The United States had held the status since 2000, Mexico since 1996, and the region since 2016, with a lapse from 2018 to 2024 after outbreaks in Venezuela and Brazil.

The counts. Canada’s multijurisdictional outbreak, which began in October 2024, reached 6,417 cases across ten provinces and territories before the Public Health Agency of Canada declared it over in October 2026. The United States recorded 2,289 cases in 2025 and more than 5,400 in 2026 through mid-November, its highest count since 1991, with ten measles-associated deaths reported by states this year. Mexico’s Health Ministry counts 20,701 confirmed cases and 50 deaths across 2025 and 2026. Across the hemisphere, PAHO counted 58,213 confirmed cases and 82 deaths in 2026 as of November 13; Guatemala, with 34,748 cases and 53 deaths through October 31, accounts for the largest single share.

The pattern. PAHO reported in August 2025 that one genotype had been identified in outbreaks in eight countries, and in its words it was found “particularly among Mennonite communities.” Inside the United States, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has documented the same tendency across a quarter century: nearly every large outbreak since 2000 has run through a close-knit, undervaccinated community. The 2025–2026 outbreaks follow that pattern at their origins and depart from it as they grow: PAHO noted in the same 2025 report that cases were increasing outside these groups, and the largest national totals, in Guatemala and Mexico, are now dominated by general and Indigenous populations.

1. The Chain: New Brunswick, Ontario, Texas, Chihuahua

The Canadian outbreak began with an imported case. Health Canada’s briefing notes trace it to an internationally acquired infection and an exposure at a wedding in New Brunswick in October 2024; Canadian reporting identifies the traveler as arriving from Thailand and the wedding as a Mennonite gathering whose guests returned to southwestern Ontario. Ontario’s chief medical officer of health, Dr. Kieran Moore, told local health units that the outbreak was disproportionately affecting Mennonite, Amish, and other Anabaptist communities, and said the vast majority of Ontario’s cases were among them. Local health units translated measles materials into Low German, the language of the Mennonite communities most affected. Alberta followed, then Manitoba, which recorded 1,038 cases from February 2025 before declaring its outbreak over on October 16, 2026. Two Canadian deaths were recorded, both premature infants infected in the womb.

The Texas outbreak, first reported on January 17, 2025, was centered in Gaines County, in a largely Mennonite community around Seminole; about 54 percent of the Texas cases were in that county, and officials believed the virus had come from the Ontario outbreak. Two unvaccinated children died in Texas and an unvaccinated adult in New Mexico. The communities involved share a documented migration history: Low German-speaking Mennonites moved from Canada to Mexico and Paraguay in the 1920s, and from Mexico to Belize, Bolivia, and West Texas in the decades after, and families visit across those borders.

That route carried the virus south. Mexican health officials trace Chihuahua’s first case to an unvaccinated Mennonite schoolboy who visited relatives in Seminole early in 2025 and fell ill after returning home; his school closed within weeks. Chihuahua sequenced more than 100 cases and every one returned genotype D8, lineage MVs/Ontario.CAN/47.24, the strain first detected in Canada in 2024 and later in Texas. By the end of 2025 the state, roughly the size of Michigan, had about 4,500 confirmed cases, more than the entire United States that year. The virus then reached agricultural laborers, many of them Indigenous, and moved through all 32 Mexican states. Mexico’s director of epidemiological information, Dr. Miguel Nakamura, has said the national epidemic traces to the Chihuahua outbreak.

2. Where It Spread: The Communities in the Record

Low German Mennonite communities in Ontario, Alberta, West Texas, Chihuahua, and Bolivia are the common ground of the outbreak’s early chain. These are conservative branches of a broad Anabaptist tradition; Mennonite practice ranges from fully modern congregations to colonies that limit technology and contact with outside institutions, and health officials in all three North American countries have described language, distance, and distrust of government as barriers alongside religious objection.

Short Creek, on the Utah–Arizona line. An outbreak that began in August 2025 in the twin towns of Hildale, Utah, and Colorado City, Arizona, a former enclave of the Fundamentalist Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints that is now increasingly ex-FLDS, passed 500 cases by April 2026; the CDC linked it to the Texas outbreak. Utah’s statewide chain, which began two months earlier, in June 2025, ran to more than 600 cases by June 2026. Local officials describe a community rebuilding after the Warren Jeffs era, in which missed childhood vaccinations had not been a priority until measles arrived.

Spartanburg County, South Carolina. The largest single U.S. outbreak since elimination began in October 2025 and reached 997 cases before it was declared over on April 27, 2026. South Carolina’s Department of Public Health estimates that about 90 percent of the outbreak’s cases were in the area’s Ukrainian- and Russian-speaking community, which numbers about 15,000 people and centers on Slavic evangelical churches; exposures were traced to several Russian-language congregations, and one charter school serving the community reported a vaccination rate of 21 percent.

Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. Pennsylvania has the most U.S. cases of 2026, more than 1,350, over a third of them in Lancaster County, home to the country’s largest Amish population. The outbreak has especially affected the Plain communities of Amish and conservative Mennonites; a 2025 estimate put measles vaccination within the local Amish community at about a quarter. County officials have also stated that the outbreak is not contained to Plain communities and is centered among unvaccinated people generally. Pennsylvania has recorded its first measles deaths in 35 years; the state counted at least five by late October. Community leaders say the case count is an undercount, because many people with measles never see a doctor.

Mexico’s Indigenous communities. PAHO reported in 2025 that Indigenous communities had been hardest hit in Mexico, that most Mexican deaths were among Indigenous people aged 1 to 54, and that the case-fatality rate in those communities was about twenty times that of the general population. The 2026 Mexican epidemic is concentrated in Jalisco, with 7,290 cases this year, followed by Mexico City and Chiapas. Among roughly 7,000 Mexican patients whose vaccination status was known in early 2026, 91.2 percent had no record of vaccination.

The precedent. The last outbreak to threaten U.S. elimination status, in 2018 and 2019, ran through Orthodox Jewish communities in Brooklyn and Rockland County, New York; it began with an unvaccinated child infected in Israel, infected more than 600 people in Brooklyn, and ended after emergency vaccination orders and community-led outreach raised coverage among young children in the affected neighborhoods from under 80 percent to about 91 percent. New York repealed religious exemptions from school vaccination in June 2019. The 2025–2026 record does not place Orthodox Jewish communities among the drivers of the current outbreaks.

3. The Rest of the Hemisphere, and Beyond It

Bolivia lost its status alongside the United States and Mexico. Its outbreak began in Santa Cruz in April 2025; the Health Ministry traced the first case to a traveler arriving from Russia and the first chain of transmission to a Mennonite colony. Bolivia has more than 150 Mennonite colonies, most of them in Santa Cruz, and PAHO describes the outbreak as spreading from those colonies to isolated Indigenous communities and the general population, with more than 600 cases reported by early 2026.

Guatemala had one case in 2025 and has the hemisphere’s largest outbreak in 2026: 34,748 confirmed cases and 53 deaths through October 31, by PAHO’s bulletin of November 5. PAHO’s April situation report linked an early cluster to a Christian religious meeting and recorded spread to at least 19 of the country’s 22 departments. Two notes govern the figure. In March 2026 Guatemala stopped taking laboratory samples from epidemiologically linked cases, and in July a retrospective update added 20,078 cases confirmed by linkage, raising the national total from 7,067 to 27,145 at a stroke. Guatemala retains its elimination status; its outbreak began in 2026 and has not yet run the twelve months the test requires.

Elsewhere in the Americas, PAHO’s spring reporting recorded cases in Belize, Paraguay, Peru, El Salvador, Brazil, Argentina, Uruguay, Colombia, Costa Rica, Chile, Panama, and the Caribbean Netherlands, most of them imported or limited clusters; the August 2025 genotype finding named Mennonite communities in Belize, Argentina, Brazil, and Paraguay among those affected. Outside the Americas, the World Health Organization confirmed early in 2026 that Armenia, Austria, Azerbaijan, Spain, the United Kingdom, and Uzbekistan had lost their elimination status, and the European Union and its neighbors reported more than 35,000 cases in 2024. The WHO counted 186,168 cases in 165 member states from January through July 2026.

4. Vaccination Coverage and the Numbers Behind It

Measles requires about 95 percent two-dose coverage in every community to stop sustained spread; the CDC puts two doses of the MMR vaccine at about 97 percent effective. National averages conceal the gaps the outbreaks found. U.S. kindergarten MMR coverage stood at 95.2 percent in the 2019–2020 school year, fell to 92.5 percent by 2024–2025, and stands at 92.4 percent in 2025–2026. Pennsylvania’s kindergarten rate was 93.2 percent last year; school coverage in Spartanburg County fell from 95 percent to under 89 percent over a decade; Mohave County, Arizona, which includes Colorado City, reported 78.4 percent in 2024. In southern Alberta, reporting from the outbreak period put two-dose coverage among children at about 56 percent. More than 90 percent of U.S. cases in 2026 have been in people unvaccinated or of unknown status. Mexico’s gap has its own history: the measles vaccine reached Mexico years after the 1963 U.S. licensure, and health officials there have described both vaccine shortages in recent years and complacency in a generation that no longer saw measles.

The CDC issued an alert this week reporting 112 confirmed measles infections during pregnancy in the United States from January 2025 through September 2026, nearly all in women unvaccinated or of unknown status, and 13 cases of congenital measles, transmitted to the infant before birth, the first such cluster reported in the country in decades. The MMR vaccine cannot be given during pregnancy.

5. The Policy Argument in the United States

The U.S. resurgence has become an argument about the Secretary of Health and Human Services, Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who led Children’s Health Defense, an organization critical of federal vaccine policy, before taking office. The record of his statements runs in both directions. In April 2025 he wrote that “the most effective way to prevent the spread of measles is the MMR vaccine,” a post that drew anger from anti-vaccine activists. In the same period he called the vaccine “leaky” and said it had not been safety tested, a claim reporters and fact-checkers have called false. In 2021, before taking office, he told an Amish audience on a Lancaster County farm that health authorities wanted them vaccinated because they could not stand that the Amish were healthy. Across seven congressional hearings in April 2026 he said the department promotes the vaccine, said he had never been anti-vaccine, and declined to accept responsibility for the outbreaks; he has repeatedly said the United States is controlling measles better than any other country, a claim FactCheck.org found faulty. Senator Raphael Warnock and other Democrats have called for his removal. Federal officials, Kennedy among them, now describe vaccination as the best protection, and the CDC continues to recommend two doses. CDC Director Erica Schwartz said this month that the loss of elimination status would not change the agency’s approach.

The comparative record bears on the argument from both sides. Public-health critics point to declining trust in federal health messaging and to the weaker response in Texas in 2025 compared with New York in 2019. The same record shows Canada losing its status first, a year ahead of the United States; Mexico and Bolivia losing theirs alongside the United States; Guatemala carrying the hemisphere’s largest outbreak; and the decline in U.S. kindergarten coverage beginning in the 2019–2020 school year, during the pandemic disruption of routine childhood care. PAHO’s own explanation for the regional resurgence names immunity gaps in specific communities and geographic areas, barriers to health services, missed vaccinations, misinformation, and hesitancy, and it names no government.

6. What Losing Elimination Status Means

Elimination is a finding about transmission. It never meant zero cases; it meant measles could enter a country but could not sustain a chain of its own for a year. Losing it reclassifies the country as endemic and changes no law or treaty. Barbosa called the loss “a serious warning” and said it is not irreversible; elimination, in his phrase, is not a trophy won once and placed on a shelf. To regain the status, each country must interrupt its chain and then document twelve months without endemic transmission. Canada has interrupted its chain and is now working toward reverification. Mexico’s health authorities have said the national outbreak may be nearing its end.

7. What the Record Does Not Settle

The record establishes the chain’s early route through related Mennonite communities in Canada, Texas, and Chihuahua by sequencing and case investigation. It does not establish the full route of every later outbreak; the Spartanburg outbreak’s initial source is unknown, and PAHO’s spring report identified the D8 lineage across at least ten countries without assigning lineage to each. Case counts are confirmed counts and are undercounts by the agencies’ own statements, with Guatemala’s figure shaped by a change in its confirmation method. The record does not apportion the U.S. decline in coverage among the pandemic’s disruption of routine care, changes in public trust, federal messaging, and local exemption law, and no study on the shelf has done so. The cocked hat is printed as found: one lineage, carried first through close-knit communities linked by family and migration across three borders, then into the general and Indigenous populations of the countries it reached.
