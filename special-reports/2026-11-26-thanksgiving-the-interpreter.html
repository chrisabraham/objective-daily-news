Thanksgiving Day: The Interpreter and the Empty Village, Filed From the Record

Objective

The Interpreter and the Empty Village: How Plymouth and the Wampanoag Found Words, 1602–1622

The feast of the autumn of 1621 is the most retold meal in the American record, and it rests on one paragraph. This report files the record underneath the paragraph: a century of European trade on the New England coast before the Mayflower, and the kidnappings that made its interpreters; the epidemic of 1616–1619, which emptied the village the colony was built on, and the open question of its cause, printed as the paper’s cocked hat; Tisquantum of Patuxet, stolen in 1614, schooled in English in London, home in 1619 to a village of the dead; Samoset of Maine, who walked into Plymouth speaking the fishermen’s English; the six points of the treaty of March 22, 1621; and Winslow’s letter, the only primary account of the feast, in its own words. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0800 ET, Thursday, November 26, 2026.

In the autumn of 1621, at a harbor village the English were renaming Plymouth, two peoples who had no language in common sat down together for three days. Massasoit, the sachem the English record calls the Wampanoag’s greatest king, came with some ninety men. The colonists numbered about fifty, of the 102 who had crossed on the Mayflower the winter before. The two sides feasted, exercised their arms, and understood one another. The understanding is the part of the record that takes explaining, because it was not improvised on the day. It had been built over a century of trade and kidnapping, on ground an epidemic had emptied four years before the Mayflower arrived, and it ran through the mouths of two men: a Maine sagamore who had picked up English from fishermen, and a Patuxet man who had learned it in London because an English captain had stolen him to sell in Spain.

A Century of Contact Before the Mayflower

The Mayflower was late. Bartholomew Gosnold coasted New England in 1602 and named Cape Cod for its fish. Martin Pring followed in 1603. George Waymouth came in 1605, and John Smith mapped the coast in 1614 and gave it the name New England. Behind and between the named voyages ran the unnamed traffic: English, French, Breton, and Basque fishing and trading vessels working the coast season after season, exchanging kettles, cloth, knives, and beads for furs and fish. By the time any Englishman proposed to settle, the coast had been a market for a hundred years, and the market had a language.

The traffic took people as well as goods. Waymouth carried five men from the Maine coast to England in 1605. One of them, Assacumet, was still in England years afterward, in the keeping of Sir Ferdinando Gorges, the promoter of New England colonization, and was set to teaching English to a later captive. Kidnapping was a standing instrument of the trade: a captive who learned English was a chart, a phrasebook, and a negotiator in one body, and the English who took them understood the value of what they had taken.

Epenow’s file is the fullest. A sachem of Capawack, the island the English called Martha’s Vineyard, he was seized in 1611 by Captain Edward Harlow, who carried some twenty-nine captives to England on that voyage. Epenow was exhibited in London as a wonder; Gorges recorded that he had learned so much English as to bid those who wondered at him “Welcome! Welcome!” Housed with Assacumet, he became fluent. He then invented a gold mine on his home island, let the invention reach Gorges, and in 1614 was shipped home under Captain Nicholas Hobson as guide and interpreter to the mine that did not exist. Off Capawack, with his people’s canoes around the ship, he addressed them in English for the English and in his own tongue for his own people, and went over the side under cover of their arrows. He lived. In 1619, Captain Thomas Dermer found him on the island, and Epenow told him the story of the escape, laughing. The coast’s English speakers had been made in both directions: by commerce, and by capture.

The Emptying, 1616–1619

Between 1616 and 1619 an epidemic ran the coast from Maine to Narragansett Bay. The Pilgrim Hall Museum’s summary of the record states that the Wampanoag lost at least 75 percent and as much as 90 percent of their entire population; the published estimates of the death rate in the literature run from one third of a local population to nine in ten. Villages were abandoned whole. Survivors left scenes they could not bury, and English parties who walked the coast afterward reported the dead still above ground where towns had stood. John Smith, summarizing the reports, wrote of three plagues in three years successively along near two hundred miles of coast.

Patuxet, a village of about 2,000 people on the harbor where Plymouth now stands, lost nearly everyone. One survivor is known by name—Tisquantum—and he survived because he was not there: he was in England, in the middle of the captivity described below. West and south of Narragansett Bay the sickness stopped. The Narragansett, the Wampanoag’s rivals, largely escaped it, and emerged the stronger power, pressing the weakened Wampanoag for tribute. The alliance Massasoit made at Plymouth two years later was negotiated by a sachem whose people had been cut down around him and whose enemies had not.

What the disease was, the record does not say, and the paper prints its cocked hat. The symptoms the sources preserve are fever, headache, bleeding from the nose, jaundice, and lesions of the skin. The older attributions were smallpox, plague, and yellow fever; the fuller list of candidates in the literature runs to influenza, chickenpox, typhus, typhoid fever, trichinosis, meningitis, and a paired infection of hepatitis B and D. The leading modern hypothesis, published by John S. Marr and John T. Cathey in Emerging Infectious Diseases in 2010, is leptospirosis in its severe form, Weil’s syndrome: a bacterial infection carried in the urine of rats—ship’s rats, landed from European vessels—into stored food and fresh water. The hypothesis fits the recorded jaundice and the bleeding from the nose. It also fits the epidemic’s selectivity, which no virus on the list explains as cleanly: the Native population lived in repeated contact with stored provisions, rodent runs, and surface water, in ways the European fishermen who haunted the same coast—eating and sleeping aboard ship or in seasonal stations—did not, and the disease emptied the villages while it spared the Europeans among them. It remains a hypothesis. Its authors offer it, in their own words, not as a definite answer but as a heuristic for others to criticize and explore. No candidate has been proved, and the desk files the disagreement as it stands.

The Interpreter: Tisquantum of Patuxet

In 1614 Captain John Smith mapped the coast and sailed for England, leaving Captain Thomas Hunt to finish the trading. Hunt instead lured twenty-four men and boys of Patuxet and Nauset aboard his ship and carried them to Málaga, in Spain, to be sold as slaves at £20 a head. Friars in Málaga intervened, took the captives out of his hands, and kept them for instruction in the Christian faith. Tisquantum, one of the twenty-four, made his way from Spain to London.

In London he lived in the household of John Slany, a merchant and shipbuilder and the treasurer of the Newfoundland Company, and there his English became thorough. Slany sent him to Cuper’s Cove, in Newfoundland, in 1616, to serve as an interpreter. In 1618 he was back in England. In 1619 he shipped as interpreter on Captain Thomas Dermer’s expedition to New England, and in June of that year Dermer brought him home to Patuxet. Dermer’s letter of December 27, 1619, records what the survey found up and down the coast: places once populous stood deserted, their people dead in the epidemic. Tisquantum had been carried across the Atlantic and back again to return to a village of the dead, and he went to live under the protection of Massasoit.

In March 1621 Massasoit sent him to Plymouth, and he stayed. Governor William Bradford’s history records the work: Tisquantum directed the colonists how to set their corn, where to take fish, and how to procure other commodities. Winslow’s letter home describes the corn manured with herrings, in the manner of the Indians, and a harvest that came in where the English crops had been indifferent. Tisquantum interpreted every consequential meeting between the colony and the Wampanoag. He also worked an account of his own: the record shows him using his place between the two peoples to magnify himself at Massasoit’s expense, telling the outlying villages that the English kept the plague itself buried in their storehouse and could loose it on whomever he named, until Massasoit demanded his surrender under the terms of the treaty and Bradford refused to give him up.

In November 1622, on a trading voyage along the coast with Bradford aboard, Tisquantum fell ill at Chatham harbor, of a fever, bleeding much at the nose—which the Indians took as a symptom of death—and died within a few days, desiring Bradford to pray for him, that he might go to the Englishmen’s God in heaven. Bradford recorded the death as a fever, and called the man in print “a special instrument sent of God for their good beyond their expectation.” Poison has been suspected since, in the interest of a sachem who had cause to be rid of him. The record does not settle that either.

Samoset Walks In

The first words at Plymouth were not Tisquantum’s. On Friday, March 16, 1621, while the colonists were busied about their military orders, a man walked alone up the street of the settlement and straight to their rendezvous. Mourt’s Relation, the colony’s own journal, printed in London in 1622, records it in the journal’s words: “He saluted us in English, and bade us welcome, for he had learned some broken English among the Englishmen that came to fish at Monchiggon, and knew by name most of the captains, commanders, and masters that usually come. He was a man free in speech, so far as he could express his mind, and of a seemly carriage.” Monchiggon is Monhegan Island, off the Maine coast, where the English kept a fishing station.

He was Samoset, a sagamore of the Abenaki of Maine, eight months in the country on a visit to Massasoit. He asked for beer. The colony had none, and gave him strong water, and biscuit, and butter, and cheese, and pudding, and a piece of mallard, all of which, the journal notes, he liked well, having been acquainted with such among the English. He talked through the afternoon—of the whole country, of every province, of their sagamores and their numbers and strength—and stayed the night. He told them the ground they had built on was called Patuxet, and that about four years earlier all of its inhabitants had died of an extraordinary plague, and there was neither man, woman, nor child of them remaining. On March 18 he returned with five men bringing deerskins to trade; it was the Sabbath, and the colonists would not trade, but fed them. On March 22 he came back with Tisquantum, and Massasoit followed.

Why English Was Already the Coast’s Language

A common tongue is a trade artifact, and this one had been a century in the making. English served as the coast’s lingua franca by 1621 for the plain reason that English ships were thick in the trade that made one necessary: a Wampanoag or Abenaki man who dealt with the seasonal fleets dealt in English words for English goods, in prices, and in the names of captains. Samoset’s English was the fleet’s English, learned at a fishing station, broken and sufficient. Tisquantum’s was the deep version, learned across five years in Spain, London, and Newfoundland, and it made him the one man on the coast who could carry a treaty word for word. Below the interpreters ran the ordinary machinery of people trading without a shared language: gesture, demonstration, goods laid out and pointed to, numbers counted off—the working talk of every frontier market, which moves merchandise and cannot negotiate an alliance.

Within a generation the current reversed, and the filed evidence of the reversal is two books. Roger Williams, the founder of Providence, learned Narragansett living among its speakers, and in 1643 published in London A Key into the Language of America, the first study of a Native American language printed in English—a phrasebook of the Narragansett tongue bound together with observations of the people’s customs, from salutations to burial. John Eliot, the minister at Roxbury, learned the Massachusett language and translated the whole Bible into it. The New Testament was printed at Cambridge in 1661, and the complete Bible in 1663: Mamusse Wunneetupanatamwe Up-Biblum God, the first Bible printed in America, its type set in part by James Printer, a Nipmuc apprentice in the Cambridge print shop, working on a book in his own language for readers of his own nation. The bridge the kidnappers had built by accident, the missionaries rebuilt on purpose, in the other direction.

The Treaty and the Feast

On March 22, 1621, Massasoit came to the brook below the settlement with his brother Quadequina and sixty men, and the articles of peace were agreed through Tisquantum’s interpreting. The treaty, as Mourt’s Relation prints it, ran in six points: that none of Massasoit’s people should hurt the English, nor the English his; that if any of his did hurt an Englishman, he would send the offender to Plymouth to be punished, and the English would do the like; that tools or goods stolen on either side would be restored; that if any people unjustly warred against Massasoit, the English would aid him, and he the English likewise; that he would send word of the peace to his neighbor confederates, so that they might be alike comprehended in it; and that his men, when they came to Plymouth, would leave their bows and arrows behind them, as the English left their pieces behind when they came to him. It was the colony’s first treaty. The peace it made held for the rest of Massasoit’s life.

The feast stands on one paragraph. Everything the record knows of it comes from a letter Edward Winslow wrote to a friend in England on December 11, 1621, printed the next year in Mourt’s Relation. Winslow’s words, in full, are these: “Our harvest being gotten in, our governor sent four men on fowling, that so we might after a special manner rejoice together after we had gathered the fruits of our labors; they four in one day killed as much fowl, as with a little help beside, served the company almost a week, at which time amongst other recreations, we exercised our arms, many of the Indians coming amongst us, and amongst the rest their greatest king Massasoit, with some ninety men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted, and they went out and killed five deer, which they brought to the plantation and bestowed on our governor, and upon the captain and others.”

That is the whole of the primary account. Four fowlers. Fowl enough, with a little help beside, for almost a week. About fifty colonists—the Mayflower had landed 102 people, and roughly half of them were dead by the spring. Ninety Wampanoag men. Three days. Five deer. Winslow names no turkey, and no dish beyond the fowl and the deer. He does not call the gathering a thanksgiving; in the Plymouth record the word belonged to days of prayer, and its attachment to this feast came more than two centuries later. The colonists who sat at it had come through their first winter partly on stored corn taken from caches on Cape Cod—a taking the colony afterward paid for—and had raised the harvest Winslow was reporting out of Patuxet’s cleared fields, planted in the Indian manner, on the instruction of the village’s one named survivor.

The Record as It Stands

The record, as filed, is this. When the Mayflower arrived, the coast she landed on had been trading with Europe for a century and burying its dead from an epidemic for four years. The colony survived its first year on cleared land the sickness had emptied, ate first from stores the dead and their neighbors had laid by, and then from fields a Patuxet man taught it to plant. It made its first peace and held its first feast through words carried by two interpreters: a Maine sagamore who had picked up his English from fishermen at Monhegan, and a Patuxet man who had learned his in London because an English captain had stolen him to sell in Spain. Tisquantum died in November 1622, fourteen months after the feast. The treaty outlived him by the length of Massasoit’s life. The desk draws no conclusion from the record. The record is printed whole.
