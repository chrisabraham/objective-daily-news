The Apparatus in Every Era, Filed in Chronological Order—Stone, Print, Radio, Television, and the Feed

Objective

A Global History of Propaganda—Special Report

The practice is older than the word by more than three thousand years, and no country in this file holds a monopoly on it. This report files the record in the order it happened: an Egyptian king carving a drawn battle into a victory on five temple walls; a Persian king publishing his version of a contested throne on a cliff face in three languages and ordering it copied across an empire; Indian and Chinese treatises that made espionage and deception chapters of statecraft; Greek theatres and Roman autobiography in stone; the Roman congregation of 1622 that gave the activity its name, as an administrative term carrying no disgrace; the printing press and the revolutionary pamphlet; an emperor’s bulletins and a Russian distinction between propaganda and agitation; the offices every belligerent built in the First World War, and the atrocity story one of them later admitted in Parliament had never had any foundation; the ministries of the interwar dictatorships and, in his proportion, one American publicist among them; the Second World War’s bureaus and its named radio voices on every side; the Cold War’s stations, front organizations, and documented disinformation services; a 1990 congressional testimony that moved a war debate and was then investigated, retracted in part, and disputed in part, printed here in its three separate states; and the platform era, in which the apparatus became a troll farm, a commentator corps, and a set of government boards, one of which, in the United States in 2022, was announced, paused, and dissolved inside four months. Where the record settles a claim, the report prints the settlement. Where it does not—the authorship of the Arthashastra, the date of the Sun Tzu text, the germ-warfare charges of 1952, the popular claim that Goebbels built his campaign on an American publicist’s book—the report prints the question as open.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1736 ET, Monday, November 16, 2026.

The Record in Brief

Propaganda begins, in the surviving record, as royal self-publication. The earliest exhibits in this file are not arguments addressed to a public in the modern sense; they are a ruler’s account of himself, fixed in the most durable medium available and placed where it could not be answered—temple walls, a cliff on an imperial road, bronze pillars in a capital. The audience was the gods, posterity, rival courts, and the ruler’s own subjects, in varying proportions. What changes across the five thousand years that follow is not the impulse but the machinery: each era’s apparatus is built out of that era’s dominant medium, and each era’s persuaders describe their work in the vocabulary their time allows—truth against the lie, the faith propagated, enlightenment, publicity, public relations, information, and, in the present century, the governance of disinformation.

The word arrives late and innocently. “Propaganda” enters the record in 1622 as part of the name of a Roman Catholic administrative body, the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith, and for three centuries it named a function, not an offense. Its modern disgrace dates from the First World War, when every belligerent built a persuasion office and the postwar reckoning—including a British foreign secretary’s admission that a famous atrocity story had no foundation—attached suspicion to the whole trade. The men who rebuilt the trade in the 1920s under the name “public relations” were working in that shadow, and one of them, Edward Bernays, said so in print. He is filed in this report where the chronology puts him: one practitioner in a decade when the Soviet Union, Italy, Germany, and Japan were each building persuasion into the structure of the state.

The desk’s method in this file is the paper’s standing one. Every load-bearing fact was checked against the independent record—scholarly histories, encyclopedias, primary documents, and the reporting of the events themselves—before it was set down. Figures that rest on a single interested source are attributed to that source in the text. Claims in wide circulation that the record does not settle are printed as open questions, in a section of their own where they occur, and the desk’s list of the claims it could not verify is kept with the desk’s research file. The chronology is the structure; no country is the frame.

1. Before the Word: Egypt and Persia

The Battle of Kadesh, fought about 1274 BCE, is the earliest case in this file in which both the event and its publicity survive. Ramesses II of Egypt marched into Syria against the Hittite king Muwatalli II and was ambushed outside Kadesh; his division was caught unprepared, and the king’s own account concedes the army’s failure around him. The battle ended without a decision: the Egyptians withdrew, and Kadesh remained in Hittite hands. What Ramesses did with the drawn battle is the exhibit. He had it published across his kingdom in two official texts—a literary account, the so-called Poem of Pentaur, and a shorter official Bulletin—and carved, with reliefs showing the king single-handedly routing the enemy, on the walls of at least five temples: Abu Simbel, Karnak, Luxor, the Ramesseum, and Abydos. In the carved version, the ambush becomes the king’s solitary triumph. The settlement of the war is also in the record, and it contradicts the walls: about 1259 BCE Ramesses and the Hittite king Hattusili III concluded a peace treaty, the earliest international peace treaty whose text survives from both sides, preserved in an Egyptian version at Karnak and a Hittite version from the archives at Hattusa. The treaty treats the two kings as equals. The temple walls do not.

The Behistun inscription, carved about 520 to 518 BCE, is a throne’s defense published to an empire. Darius I of Persia had seized the throne in 522 BCE after killing a man he identified as Gaumata, an impostor posing as the king’s brother; rebellions followed across the empire. Darius had his account cut into a cliff face on the road between Babylon and Ecbatana, near modern Kermanshah, in three languages—Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian—with a relief showing the king triumphant over nine rebel leaders, each labeled in the text as a “liar king.” The inscription’s argument is doctrinal: Darius rules by the favor of the god Ahura Mazda and embodies arta, Truth, against drauga, the Lie, which is what every rebellion is said to serve. The text also records its own distribution: Darius states that he had the inscription copied and sent throughout the empire, and copies in other languages have been found as far away as Elephantine, an island garrison town in Egypt, where an Aramaic version circulated on papyrus. The cliff could not travel; the text was made to. The site was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2006. The desk notes the dating plainly: the seizure of the throne in 522 BCE is fixed; the carving is dated by the specialists to about 520–518 BCE, and the round figure of 515 BCE found in general references was not adopted.

What these two exhibits establish is the form, not yet the institution. In both, the persuader is the state itself, the medium is monumental, the claim is unfalsifiable by its audience, and the subject is the ruler’s legitimacy. Neither text argues with an opponent; each overwrites him. The opponent’s version survives in the Kadesh case only because the Hittites kept archives too. That asymmetry—whose record survives—runs through the whole of this file, and the desk flags it at the start because it never goes away.

2. The Treatises of India and China

The Arthashastra made persuasion a department of government on paper. The Sanskrit treatise on statecraft, traditionally attributed to Kautilya—identified in the tradition with Chanakya, the teacher of Chandragupta Maurya, founder of the Mauryan empire—contains the ancient world’s most systematic surviving treatment of covert persuasion. Its chapters organize a standing intelligence service: spies in fixed stations and roving agents, recruited and disguised as ascetics, merchants, astrologers, entertainers, and household servants; coded communication; the sowing of discord among an enemy’s allies and within his court; the use of rumor and of agents posing as defectors; and countermeasures against the same arts used in return. The attribution is printed here as the record gives it, which is qualified: scholars hold that the text was composed, expanded, and redacted over centuries, most plausibly between the second century BCE and the third century CE, by more than one hand, and the identification of its author with Chandragupta’s minister is tradition, not settled fact. The treatise was lost to scholarship for centuries and recovered in 1905, when a manuscript reached the Mysore Oriental Library; R. Shamasastry published it in 1909. What is not in dispute is the content: a state manual, from the Indian tradition, that treats the management of belief—at home and in enemy territory—as ordinary administration.

China’s surviving treatise puts deception at the head of the art of war and espionage at its end. The Art of War, attributed to Sun Tzu—in the tradition, Sun Wu, a general in the service of King Helü of Wu—opens with the maxim that all warfare is based on deception, and closes, in its thirteenth chapter, with a classification of spies into five kinds: local spies, recruited from the enemy’s population; inward spies, his officials; converted spies, his own agents turned; doomed spies, fed false information to carry back; and surviving spies, who return with reports. The chapter argues the economics of the thing: foreknowledge, it says, cannot be got from spirits or by calculation, but only from men who know the enemy’s situation. The dating carries the same caution as the Arthashastra’s. The traditional setting is the late Spring and Autumn period, about 500 BCE; scholars who date the received text by its language and its warfare—mass infantry armies rather than aristocratic chariot duels—place it in the Warring States period, centuries later, and the thirteenth chapter itself, which names a strategist of the late fourth century BCE, is treated by some scholars as a later addition to a twelve-chapter core. Bamboo-slip copies excavated at Yinqueshan in 1972 confirm the text existed in near-modern form by the second century BCE. The desk prints the contents as the text’s, and the authorship as the tradition’s.

The two treatises differ from the monuments in audience and in candor. Kadesh and Behistun were addressed outward, to be believed. The Arthashastra and The Art of War were addressed inward, to rulers and their officers, and they describe manipulation without embarrassment because their readers were its practitioners, not its targets. Persuasion as a published claim and persuasion as a classified technique thus enter the record together, in the same centuries, on different continents. The file will meet that pairing in every era that follows.

3. Greece and Rome: The Speech and the Stone

Athens built persuasion into its constitution and its theatre. In a democracy that decided war, peace, and verdicts by mass vote, the ability to move an assembly was a governing skill, and the Greeks studied it as one. Aeschylus’s tragedy The Persians, staged in 472 BCE, eight years after the battle of Salamis, presented the Persian court’s grief at its defeat to an Athenian audience that included men who had fought in it—the enemy’s catastrophe rendered as Athens’s civic memory, in the city’s own theatre, at public expense. The sophist Gorgias, in his Encomium of Helen, made the theory explicit: speech, he argued, is a powerful lord, which by means of the finest and most invisible body achieves the most divine works—it can stop fear, remove grief, and compel the soul of the listener. Pericles’ funeral oration over the first dead of the Peloponnesian War, preserved by Thucydides, is the surviving specimen of the state eulogy as an instrument: the dead are praised by praising the city that produced them, and the city’s ideology—democracy, openness, sacrifice—is stated in its most durable form at a graveside. None of this was called propaganda. All of it was public persuasion in the service of a state’s account of itself, conducted in the open and judged by its audience’s applause and votes.

Rome’s contribution was the commander’s dispatch turned into literature, and the emperor’s curriculum vitae turned into architecture. Julius Caesar’s commentaries on the Gallic War, written in the third person and in a plain style that Roman critics praised, were addressed to the Roman public whose continued support his career required; they present a decade of conquest as a sequence of necessities, each campaign forced on a reluctant commander by the conduct of others. Augustus, his heir, perfected the form in the Res Gestae Divi Augusti—the deeds of the divine Augustus—a first-person account of his career finalized before his death in 14 CE and inscribed, by his instruction, on bronze pillars before his mausoleum in Rome. The bronze original is lost; copies were carved on temples across the empire, and the fullest survivor, the Monumentum Ancyranum at modern Ankara, carries the text in Latin and Greek. Its thirty-five sections enumerate offices, expenditures, buildings, games, and conquests, and record that the Senate voted the author its honors. What the text omits is as structured as what it includes: the proscriptions, the civil wars’ losers, and the mechanics by which a republic became a monarchy do not appear; the civil wars are present only as their settlement. The document is the state’s founder writing his own inscription, and its survival in provincial copies shows the distribution was imperial policy, not vanity alone.

Between Athens and Rome the medium changed and the function did not. The Greek instruments were performed—spoken in assemblies, sung in theatres, delivered over graves—and died with the occasion except as texts. The Roman instruments were inscribed, and the inscription outlasted the regime that cut it. In both cases the persuader spoke with the state’s voice and the state’s resources, and the claim advanced was the same claim Darius cut into the cliff at Behistun: that the existing order of things is the true one, and its enemies are liars, barbarians, or disturbers of a peace the ruler merely defends.

4. The Word Itself: Rome, 1622

The word “propaganda” is a piece of Vatican administration. On January 6, 1622, Pope Gregory XV announced the creation of a new congregation of cardinals charged with the oversight of the Roman Catholic Church’s missions worldwide; on June 22, 1622, the bull Inscrutabili Divinae formally instituted the Sacra Congregatio de Propaganda Fide—the Sacred Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith—composed of thirteen cardinals and two prelates, with central authority over missionary activity in every part of the world. The Latin propagare means to spread, to propagate, to extend—a gardener’s word, used of setting out shoots. The congregation’s context was the Counter-Reformation: Protestant churches had carried their message across northern Europe, and Rome built a permanent office to carry its own across the globe, training missionaries, printing catechisms in local languages, and reporting to a single desk. In its name, and for long after, the word carried no disgrace. It named a function—the organized spreading of a faith—and the men who performed it used it of themselves.

The word’s fall is a twentieth-century event, and this file will date it where the record does. Through the nineteenth century “propaganda” remained a neutral term for organized advocacy of any doctrine; political movements used it of their own work without apology, and, as Section 6 records, the Russian revolutionary tradition built its central vocabulary on the word and its pair, agitation. The pejorative sense—propaganda as the other side’s lying—is a product of the First World War and of what was admitted afterward about that war’s persuasion campaigns. The desk flags the point here because the whole later history turns on it: every era’s practitioners call their own output information, education, or publicity, and reserve “propaganda” for the adversary’s. The 1622 congregation is the last major institution in this file to take the word into its own name with pride.

5. Print and Revolution

The printing press did not invent persuasion; it industrialized it, and the first industrial campaign in the record is Martin Luther’s. Between 1517 and the mid-1520s, Luther’s case against the Roman Church was carried in pamphlets—Flugschriften, short, cheap, vernacular, and printed in runs that dwarfed anything manuscript culture could produce. The bibliographic record, as compiled in Mark U. Edwards’s Printing, Propaganda, and Martin Luther (1994), counts more than 1,800 printings of Luther’s works by the end of 1525, between 83 and 85 per cent of them in German rather than Latin; across 1518 to 1544, Luther’s works went through some 2,551 printings against 514 for his Catholic opponents combined, and he out-published his nearest evangelical competitor by roughly eleven to one. Elizabeth Eisenstein’s conservative estimate puts sales of Luther’s writings at about 300,000 copies in the years 1517 to 1520 alone. The historian Henri-Jean Martin estimated that between 1518 and 1523, about a third of everything published in German was produced by the Reformation side. The Catholic response—the congregation of 1622 among it—was built a century later, with the same instruments, by men who had learned what print could do from the receiving end.

The eighteenth century’s revolutions were the first fought in a mature print market, on both sides of the Atlantic and on both sides of each argument. Thomas Paine’s Common Sense, published in Philadelphia in January 1776, argued the American colonies into independence in plain language addressed to ordinary readers; its circulation became a legend in its own time, and the desk prints the legend as a legend—the figures commonly repeated for its sales, from 100,000 upward, are disputed among historians, and no agreed number exists. What is documented is the form: a cheap pamphlet, reprinted in edition after edition, read aloud in taverns and camps, answered by loyalist pamphlets in the same form. The French Revolution, from 1789, multiplied every instrument at once: an uncensored press that produced hundreds of new newspapers within months, Marat’s L’Ami du peuple and its rivals, printed decrees posted on walls, engravings and songs, and a revolutionary state that staged its legitimacy in public festivals and rebuilt the calendar itself. Counter-revolution printed back—royalist journals, émigré presses abroad, and Edmund Burke’s Reflections, itself a pamphlet-war artifact. By 1800 the pattern of the next two centuries was set: wherever print was cheap, every cause acquired a press, and every government acquired a reason to license, subsidize, or suppress one.

6. The Nineteenth Century: Bulletins, the Mass Press, and a Distinction

Napoleon Bonaparte was the first ruler of the print era to run his reputation as a daily operation. The bulletins of the Grande Armée, drafted under his supervision during his campaigns, were printed in the official Le Moniteur universel, posted in public places, and read across Europe; they reported victories in the emperor’s own framing, minimized or omitted reverses, and were famous in their own time for exactly that—the contemporary proverb ran mentir comme un bulletin, to lie like a bulletin, a phrase his secretary Bourrienne’s memoirs record as common speech. The domestic press was handled as an instrument of the same office: in January 1800 the Paris political journals were cut by decree from seventy-three to thirteen, and new ones were forbidden. Defeats were rewritten after the fact—the account of Marengo was revised into a planned triumph—and in exile on St Helena the emperor dictated the memoirs that fixed his legend for the century. The nineteenth century’s other persuasion systems were built on infrastructure rather than personality: the penny press and then the mass-circulation newspaper made a daily persuadable audience a commercial fact; the telegraph agencies distributed a common version of events across continents; and the colonial empires maintained information services that presented the imperial project to the colonized and to the home public in the language of improvement. The desk files the colonial record in proportion: it was pervasive, it was printed, and its individual campaigns are less documented in the sources the desk could check than its existence, which is not in doubt.

The century closes with the distinction that organized the next one. The Russian Marxist Georgi Plekhanov drew the line that the revolutionary tradition then built into its institutions: propaganda, in his definition, is the promulgation of many ideas to an individual or a small group; agitation is the presentation of a single idea to a large mass of people. Lenin took the distinction up in What Is to Be Done? (1902) and gave it its working form: the propagandist operates in print, presenting the whole explanation of a question—the causes of a crisis, the structure of a system—to the few who will study it; the agitator operates in speech, seizing on one glaring fact and driving a crowd to action on it. The paired term that resulted, agitprop, takes its name from the Agitation and Propaganda Section of the Communist Party’s Central Committee—the department the Bolsheviks built in 1920, filed in Section 8. The distinction matters to this history because it is the first theory of the practice written by its practitioners as an organizational chart: two products, two audiences, two crafts, one office. Every ministry in the sections that follow is, in its own vocabulary, a version of that chart.

7. The First World War: Every Belligerent Builds an Office

Britain’s office was secret, literary, and aimed abroad. The War Propaganda Bureau was formed in September 1914, with Cabinet approval on September 5, under Charles Masterman, a Cabinet minister, and housed—its address unpublished—in Wellington House, the London offices of the National Insurance Commission. On September 2, 1914, Masterman had met there with twenty-five of Britain’s leading authors, among them Thomas Hardy, Arthur Conan Doyle, H. G. Wells, G. K. Chesterton, J. M. Barrie, and John Galsworthy, and recruited them to the work. The bureau’s product was print for foreign and especially neutral opinion, above all American: more than 1,160 pamphlets over the war, distributed through commercial and private channels so that their origin did not show. The British public did not learn the bureau existed until 1935. By February 1918 the work had been folded into a Department, then a Ministry, of Information—the wartime state’s persuasion arm made permanent and public only as the war ended.

The United States built its office in the open and staffed it with the press. The Committee on Public Information was created by Executive Order 2594 on April 13, 1917, days after the declaration of war, and chaired by the journalist George Creel. The CPI produced posters, pamphlets, films, and a daily official bulletin; it organized the speaking campaign of the Four Minute Men, some 75,000 volunteers who delivered short patriotic addresses in cinemas and public meetings across the country—Creel’s own published account boasted of 7.5 million speeches delivered to 300 million listeners, figures the desk prints as the chairman’s, unaudited. Among the committee’s staff was a young Edward Bernays, who worked on its foreign publicity, including its Latin American news service; the war was his apprenticeship, and Section 9 files what he made of it. The committee was dissolved in 1919. France maintained its own apparatus through the Maison de la Presse and the foreign ministry’s information services; Austria-Hungary ran a war press headquarters that managed correspondents at the front; the desk files those offices in brief because the record the desk could check documents their existence and structure more fully than their campaigns.

Germany’s most ambitious campaign of the war was aimed at the Muslim world, and it ran through the Ottoman Empire. Germany’s Nachrichtenstelle für den Orient—the Intelligence Bureau for the East—was founded in Berlin in September 1914 under the archaeologist and diplomat Max von Oppenheim, whose October 1914 memorandum proposed fomenting revolution in the Islamic territories of Germany’s enemies. On November 14, 1914, in Istanbul, the Sheikh-ul-Islam Ürgüplü Hayri proclaimed five fatwas calling the world’s Muslims to jihad against the Entente powers—the Central Powers excepted—and the German bureau produced the supporting print, including the journal al-Jihad and propaganda material for the camps at Zossen and Wünsdorf where Muslim prisoners of war were held and recruited. The Dutch orientalist Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje, watching the campaign, called it “jihad made in Germany.” The call did not produce the general rising Berlin had planned for; the file records the apparatus, which was extensive, and the result, which was not.

The war’s most durable exhibit is a story that was not true, and its unmaking is also in the record. In April 1917, The Times and the Daily Mail reported that Germany was rendering down the corpses of its own soldiers in Kadaververwertungsanstalten—corpse-utilization plants—for fats, glycerine, and fertilizer. The story’s root was a German newspaper item, by the correspondent Karl Rosner in the Berliner Lokal-Anzeiger, describing a rendering plant behind the lines; in German, Kadaver denotes an animal carcass, and the plant described was for animals. The tale passed through Belgian exile papers and rumor into the British press as a story about men. It was believed, repeated, and used. In 1925 the Foreign Secretary, Austen Chamberlain, told the House of Commons that there had never been any foundation for it. Brigadier-General John Charteris, who had run British military intelligence in France, claimed in 1925 that he had fabricated the story himself by transposing the captions of two photographs; recent scholarship does not credit the claim—the historians who have examined it trace the story to the Northcliffe press and to a circulating folktale rather than to an official invention—and the desk prints Charteris’s boast as a claim, not a fact. Arthur Ponsonby’s Falsehood in War-Time (1928) catalogued the corpse factory with the war’s other atrocity legends and made the catalogue famous. The episode’s consequence is the one this file has already noted: after 1918, “propaganda” itself became a suspect word, and every government’s denials of the next war’s true atrocity reports would be discounted by readers who remembered this one.

8. The Interwar State: Agitprop, the Ministry of Popular Culture, the Propaganda Ministry, the Bureau

The Soviet Union institutionalized Plekhanov’s distinction. In 1920 the Bolsheviks created a Department of Agitation and Propaganda in the Central Committee of the Communist Party, with a state counterpart, Glavpolitprosvet, for political education; the department, in successive forms, ran until 1991. Its instruments were every medium the era offered. The ROSTA Windows—stencilled poster sheets produced by the Russian Telegraph Agency and displayed in empty shop windows—ran from the autumn of 1919 to January 1921 in some 1,550 to 1,600 designs, the poet Vladimir Mayakovsky among their principal artists, with several hundred designs attributed to him. Agitation trains and steamboats carried printing presses, film projectors, and lecturers into the countryside; living newspapers dramatized the line for audiences who could not read it. Censorship was the same office’s other hand: Glavlit, the central censorship administration, was created in June 1922, and the party’s newspapers Pravda and Izvestia and the TASS wire carried the authorized version as a matter of structure. The Soviet innovation, filed plainly, is permanence: persuasion was not a wartime expedient to be dissolved at the peace, as the CPI had been, but a standing department of the state in all years.

Fascist Italy built its apparatus in stages and ended with a ministry that instructed editors daily. The Istituto Luce, founded in 1924, made the state’s films; a decree of April 3, 1926 required every cinema in Italy to show Luce material, and its newsreel, the Giornale Luce, ran from 1927. Radio was a state monopoly through the EIAR. The press machinery climbed the administrative ladder in public: a Press Office, raised in 1934—under Galeazzo Ciano—to an Undersecretariat for Press and Propaganda, raised again on June 24, 1935 to a Ministry of Press and Propaganda, and renamed on May 27, 1937, by royal decree, the Ministry of Popular Culture—Minculpop. The ministry’s daily practice is the documented detail: it sent editors written directives, the veline, specifying what to print, what to omit, and how to phrase what remained. Persuasion and instruction were one office, and the instruction was in writing.

Germany’s ministry was created within weeks of the Nazi seizure of power and said what it was for. The Reich Ministry for Public Enlightenment and Propaganda was established on March 13, 1933, with Joseph Goebbels as minister. In his first address to the press on March 15 and to radio officials on March 25, Goebbels described the work as a “spiritual mobilization” of the German people and called radio the “eighth great power” among the instruments of the age. The receiver was subsidized to match the doctrine: the Volksempfänger, the people’s receiver, sold at 76 marks—a smaller model at 35—with 1.5 million manufactured in 1933 and more than 7 million in all. By the middle of 1939, over 70 per cent of German households owned a radio, then the highest rate in the world. The ministry’s remit ran across press, radio, film, theatre, and exhibitions; the desk files its structure and its instruments, which are documented in full, and notes that its minister’s diaries and the ministry’s directives survive as the most complete self-record any apparatus in this file left behind.

Japan assembled the same structure on the same timetable, by committee, division, and bureau. A Cabinet Information Committee was formed on July 1, 1936, coordinating officials of the ministries and the armed services; on September 25, 1937, two months into the war in China, it was upgraded to a Cabinet Information Division, applying censorship to all media, issuing detailed guidelines to publishers, and publishing its own photographic weekly, Shashin Shühō, from February 1938. On December 6, 1940 the division became the Cabinet Information Bureau, consolidating the information functions of the Home and Foreign ministries and the services under a president responsible to the prime minister, with a staff of about six hundred and five divisions covering planning, news, external propaganda, censorship, and culture. Section 10 files the bureau at war. The interwar pattern, stated once for all four cases: in each state the persuasion office was permanent, cabinet-level, and fused with censorship, and in each the office’s own publications said the arrangement was necessary because the nation was encircled by other nations’ propaganda—a claim each of the others’ existence made partly true.

9. Edward Bernays, in Proportion

Edward Bernays was the American practitioner who named the postwar trade, and the record of his career is documentary. Born in Vienna in 1891, a nephew of Sigmund Freud, he served in the Committee on Public Information during the First World War, working on foreign publicity. In 1923 he published Crystallizing Public Opinion, which set out the role he called the public relations counsel—a specialist who interprets his client to the public and the public to his client. In 1928 he published Propaganda, whose opening sentences state the theory without euphemism: “The conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses is an important element in democratic society. Those who manipulate this unseen mechanism of society constitute an invisible government which is the true ruling power of our country.” Elsewhere in the book: “Propaganda is the executive arm of the invisible government.” His acknowledged sources were the crowd psychology of Gustave Le Bon and Wilfred Trotter, Graham Wallas, and Walter Lippmann’s Public Opinion (1922). The later phrase by which his method is usually known, “the engineering of consent,” is the title of his essay of 1947. He died in 1995. The books are in print and the quotations above are from their pages; the desk files Bernays as the trade’s theorist because he wrote the theory down, which most practitioners, and all ministries, did not.

His most documented campaign is also the smallest in this file, and it is filed at its size. In 1929 Bernays worked for George Washington Hill, president of the American Tobacco Company, whose Lucky Strike cigarettes Hill wished to sell to women, for whom smoking in public carried a taboo. The psychoanalyst A. A. Brill advised Bernays that cigarettes could be presented to women as symbols of freedom. On Easter Sunday, March 31, 1929, during the Fifth Avenue Easter parade in New York, a group of young women—recruited for the purpose, with photographers positioned in advance and the press notified that a women’s-rights demonstration would occur—lit cigarettes at a signal. The New York Times of April 1, 1929 carried the story under the headline “Group of Girls Puff at Cigarettes as a Gesture of ‘Freedom’,” and the cigarettes entered the record under Bernays’s name for them: torches of freedom. In another account from his own memoirs, Bernays described polling physicians on the healthfulness of a substantial breakfast and publicizing their endorsement on behalf of the Beech-Nut company’s bacon; the desk prints that account as his, and notes that the exact number of doctors commonly attached to it in retellings could not be verified against a checkable source. The campaigns are the practitioner’s specimens. They persuaded consumers, in one country, in peacetime, on behalf of companies; they are filed after the ministries of Section 8 because that is where, in scale and in kind, they belong.

One claim about Bernays is in wide circulation, and the record does not settle it; the desk prints it as open. It is often said that Goebbels studied Bernays’s work and based the Nazi campaign against the Jews on Crystallizing Public Opinion. The claim’s only source is Bernays himself: in his 1965 memoir, Biography of an Idea, he recounts a 1933 conversation in which the Hearst correspondent Karl von Wiegand told him that Goebbels had shown him a propaganda library, kept Bernays’s book in it, and was using it, in von Wiegand’s reported words, as a basis for his campaign. No entry in Goebbels’s diaries, no inventory of his library, and no German document the desk could find corroborates the story. That Nazi propagandists studied American advertising and publicity technique in general is documented separately and is not the same claim. The specific claim—the book, the library, the basis—rests on one interested witness reporting another man’s words thirty-two years later, and the report files it at that grade: possible, unproven, and not a foundation on which the desk will rest the German ministry’s genealogy, which is documented from German sources and is filed in Section 8.

10. The Second World War: All Sides on the Air

The Soviet Union’s wartime voice was organized on the war’s third day. The Soviet Information Bureau—Sovinformburo—was established on June 24, 1941, under the People’s Commissariat for Foreign Affairs, and issued every official report from the fronts; its communiqués, read on radio by the announcer Yuri Levitan, were the Soviet public’s war. Abroad, the bureau dispatched some 135,000 articles to foreign newspapers, journals, and radio stations during the war; its writers included Ilya Ehrenburg, whose work the United Press alone placed in more than 1,600 newspapers. The bureau also ran the war’s five sponsored committees—among them the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee—which addressed appeals to foreign audiences in their own names. It was succeeded in 1961 by the Novosti press agency.

Britain divided its output by candor and kept the division in separate buildings. The Political Warfare Executive, formed in August and September 1941 under the Foreign Office and announced to the Commons on September 11, ran the clandestine war. Its black propaganda, directed by Sefton Delmer from Woburn Abbey and Milton Bryan, impersonated the enemy to the enemy: the station Gustav Siegfried Eins, on air from May 23, 1941, purported to be the transmitter of a dissident German officer, “der Chef,” voiced by the German exile Peter Seckelmann, broadcasting obscenity, gossip, and defeatism as a patriot’s protest; Soldatensender Calais, from November 1943, used the powerful Aspidistra transmitter to pose as an official German forces station, mixing accurate news and music with fabricated items designed to corrode morale—its credibility resting on the accuracy of everything except the items that mattered. The BBC’s European services ran the opposite doctrine as a matter of policy: factual accuracy, including British defeats, as the white propaganda’s claim on the enemy listener’s trust. The two doctrines were run simultaneously, by the same state, for the same war.

The United States consolidated late and broadcast early. The Voice of America began broadcasting in German in February 1942—the exact day is disputed between the service’s own institutional dating and archival script research, and the desk prints the month—opening with the announcer William Harlan Hale’s statement of method: “Here speaks a voice from America. Every day at this time we will bring you the news of the war. The news may be good or bad. We shall tell you the truth.” The Office of War Information was created by Executive Order 9182 on June 13, 1942, under the journalist Elmer Davis, with an Overseas Branch under Robert E. Sherwood; the Voice of America was placed under it. The American apparatus, like the British, ran leaflets, film, and clandestine stations alongside the attributed voice, and, like the British, dissolved its wartime office at the peace—in the American case into the State Department, from which the Voice emerged into the Cold War structure filed in Section 11.

Japan’s bureau went to war with the wire and the shortwave as its foreign voice. The Cabinet Information Bureau, completed in December 1940, held complete authority over news, advertising, and public events at home—in February 1941 it circulated to editors a blacklist of writers not to be printed, and in February 1942 the country’s newspapers were ordered to merge into single prefectural papers or close. Abroad, the bureau’s two channels were the Domei news agency’s wire and the NHK’s shortwave service, Radio Tokyo, both under its control. By November 1943 NHK was broadcasting more than thirty-two hours of overseas programming daily in twenty-five languages. War results were announced separately by the Press Department of Imperial General Headquarters, whose communiqués, from the middle of 1942 onward, departed increasingly from the events they described—defeats announced as victories, losses omitted—so that the Japanese public’s official war and the war itself diverged for three years, a divergence the home audience could not check and the bureau’s censorship existed to protect.

The war’s radio voices have names, and the record of each is a court file as much as a broadcast file. Iva Toguri, an American citizen and UCLA graduate stranded in Japan by the war, broadcast on Radio Tokyo’s The Zero Hour under the name Orphan Ann—some 340 programs, by the count in the appellate record—playing American records and reading scripts for an audience of American servicemen, who collectively named the voices of such programs “Tokyo Rose”; no broadcaster used that name of herself. Toguri was convicted in the United States in 1949 on one of eight treason counts—for speaking into a microphone words concerning the loss of ships—sentenced to ten years and a $10,000 fine, and released in 1956; witnesses against her later stated they had been coerced into perjury, and in January 1977 President Ford pardoned her, restoring her citizenship—the only pardon in an American treason case of the war. Mildred Gillars, broadcasting from Berlin as “Axis Sally”—on air, “Midge”; another broadcaster, Rita Zucca, used the Sally name from Rome—was convicted in 1949 on one count, for a recorded radio drama of 1944, Vision of Empire, and sentenced on March 10, 1949 to ten to thirty years; she was released in 1961. William Joyce, American-born and holding a British passport obtained by false statement, broadcast from Germany as the voice the British public named Lord Haw-Haw; he was hanged for treason in January 1946. The desk files the three together because their audiences heard them as one phenomenon—the enemy’s familiar voice in the mess tent and the barracks—and because their fates, a pardon, a served sentence, and a hanging, measure how differently three legal systems priced the same act.

11. The Cold War: Stations, Services, and Active Measures

The American structure was rebuilt in 1953 and its clandestine half was exposed in 1967. The United States Information Agency was created in August 1953 and took the Voice of America from the State Department, running libraries, exhibits, publications, and broadcasting as the attributed arm. The unattributed arm was older: Radio Free Europe made its first broadcast on July 4, 1950, from Lampertheim in West Germany, addressed to Czechoslovakia, presented to the world by the National Committee for a Free Europe—an organization the record now shows was a Central Intelligence Agency front, organized with Allen Dulles’s participation. Radio Liberty, addressed to the Soviet Union itself, began on March 1, 1953. Both stations were covertly financed by the CIA—by one scholarly accounting, some $500 million over twenty years—until the financing was exposed, beginning with the Ramparts magazine disclosures of 1967 and the admission that year that foundation money had been passed through; covert funding ended in the early 1970s, open congressional funding followed through a Board for International Broadcasting from October 1973, and the two services merged in 1976, broadcasting from Munich until 1995. The exposure is filed as part of the apparatus’s history, not as a footnote to it: a station’s claim to be a free voice was its product, and the funding was the fact its audience had not been told.

The Soviet Union’s corresponding instrument was a doctrine with a name. Aktivnyye meropriyatiya—active measures—was the KGB’s term for operations to influence foreign opinion and policy short of espionage: forged documents, planted stories, agents of influence, and front organizations, run by the First Chief Directorate’s disinformation unit, Department D, upgraded in 1962 to Service A. The word dezinformatsiya itself had been defined in the Great Soviet Encyclopedia of 1952. The best-documented single operation is Operation INFEKTION, begun in 1983, which planted and amplified the claim that the AIDS virus had been engineered at Fort Detrick, Maryland, as an American biological weapon: the story appeared first in 1983 in The Patriot, a newspaper in India, and was carried over the following years through sympathetic and unwitting outlets worldwide. The operation is documented in the archive copied by the KGB archivist Vasili Mitrokhin and brought out in 1992, published with the historian Christopher Andrew in The Sword and the Shield (1999) and The World Was Going Our Way (2005); the desk notes the archive’s nature—one officer’s transcriptions, extensively cross-checked since—and files Thomas Rid’s Active Measures (2020) as the standard scholarly history of the doctrine. Active measures did not end with the Soviet Union; Section 13 files their platform-era successor under its own name.

The non-aligned world built apparatuses of its own, and Egypt’s was the era’s most listened-to. The Voice of the Arabs—Sawt al-Arab—first aired on July 4, 1953, as a half-hour program on Cairo Radio, a year after the Egyptian revolution, and grew into a dedicated service broadcasting across the Arab world. Under its director and chief announcer Ahmed Said, who ran it until 1967, it was President Gamal Abdel Nasser’s instrument for carrying pan-Arabism over the heads of governments to their publics: anti-colonial, revolutionary, and carried on the music of Umm Kulthum as much as on commentary. In June 1967, during the Six-Day War, the station broadcast reports of Egyptian victories while Egyptian forces were being destroyed; its credibility collapsed with the defeat and did not recover. The desk files the Egyptian case in full because it is the Cold War’s clearest demonstration, outside the two blocs, of the whole cycle this report tracks: a state builds a voice, the voice builds an audience no ministry could buy, and a single season of checkable falsehood spends it.

Two Asian cases close the era, one an allegation the record has not settled and one a voice. In February 1952, during the Korean War, North Korea’s foreign minister Pak Hon-yong formally charged that American aircraft were dropping insects infected with plague, cholera, and other diseases on North Korea—a campaign China and the Soviet Union amplified worldwide. The United States denied the charge absolutely and proposed an investigation by the International Committee of the Red Cross; the Soviet Union vetoed the proposal in the Security Council. An International Scientific Commission, convened under the World Peace Council and chaired by the British biochemist Joseph Needham, worked in China and North Korea in the summer of 1952 and reported on September 15, 1952 that the charges were true; American prisoners of war who had confessed to germ warfare retracted their confessions on repatriation. In 1998, documents from former Soviet archives were published indicating that the campaign had been fabricated under Soviet direction. Scholars remain divided—the archival case for fabrication is strong and is disputed, and Chinese specialists maintain the original charge. The desk prints the case as what it is: the Cold War’s largest propaganda allegation, formally investigated twice to opposite conclusions, and open. The voice is Trinh Thi Ngo, who joined the Voice of Vietnam in 1955 and, from 1965 to 1973, broadcast in English to American troops in Vietnam under the name Thu Huong—three thirty-minute programs a day at the height of the war, her scripts written by the North Vietnamese Defense Ministry’s propaganda department, reading the names and home towns of American dead taken from the army newspaper Stars and Stripes. American servicemen named her Hanoi Hannah. She died on September 30, 2016; the obituaries record that her listeners, by their own accounts, tuned in for the music and the company, and that no defection is known to have resulted from her broadcasts—the file’s recurring gap, in her case as in Toguri’s, between the size of the apparatus and the measurable size of its effect.

12. The Nayirah Testimony, 1990: What Is Proven, What Was Retracted, What Remains Contested

The case belongs to the lineage of Section 9: it is the public-relations campaign as an instrument of war policy, run between the Cold War’s end and the platform era. On October 10, 1990, two months after Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait, a fifteen-year-old girl identified to the public only as Nayirah testified before the Congressional Human Rights Caucus in Washington. She said she had volunteered at the al-Addan hospital in Kuwait City and had watched Iraqi soldiers enter the maternity ward, take babies out of their incubators, take the incubators, and leave the babies to die on the cold floor. The testimony was televised, quoted by the President of the United States in speeches, and cited by senators in the debate over authorizing war. Because its parts do not stand at the same grade of proof, the desk files them in three separate paragraphs, and asks the reader to keep them separate.

What is proven. The hearing, the testimony, and its text are matters of record; the testimony was not given under oath, and the body that heard it was a caucus of legislators, not a committee of Congress with subpoena power. The witness’s surname was withheld from the public at the time on the stated ground of protecting her family. She was Nayirah al-Sabah, the daughter of Saud Nasser al-Sabah, Kuwait’s ambassador to the United States—a fact established in print by John MacArthur in The New York Times of January 6, 1992, and not since disputed. Her appearance was organized and she was prepared for it by the public relations firm Hill & Knowlton, retained by Citizens for a Free Kuwait, a Washington organization funded by the Kuwaiti government in exile; the firm’s filings under the Foreign Agents Registration Act show $11.9 million channeled through the organization, of which some $10.8 million went to the firm. The numbers attached to the story grew as it traveled: the witness’s own written statement spoke of fifteen babies in incubators in the room she described; figures of three hundred and more dead infants circulated in the months that followed and were carried by Amnesty International’s December 1990 report. The testimony’s use is also record: President George H. W. Bush cited the incubator story repeatedly in the case for war, and senators cited it in the authorization debate that ended on January 12, 1991.

What was retracted, and what the postwar investigations found. After Kuwait’s liberation, the story was investigated on the ground. Middle East Watch, the Human Rights Watch division, conducted fieldwork in Kuwait in March 1991 and published its findings in February 1992: its investigators interviewed dozens of doctors, nurses, administrators, and cemetery workers and examined hospital and burial records, and found no credible evidence that Iraqi forces had removed babies from incubators and left them to die. Doctors at the maternity hospital told the investigator that the ward had held twenty-five to thirty incubators, that none had been taken, and that no babies had been taken from them. The organization’s director, Andrew Whitley, stated the conclusion in terms: there was no truth to the charge, which he called manufactured from germs of truth by people outside the country who should have known better. Amnesty International, whose December 1990 report had corroborated the story from evacuee accounts, withdrew its support after its own postwar inquiries, stating that it had found no reliable evidence that Iraqi forces had caused the deaths of babies by removing them or ordering their removal from incubators. Post-liberation reporting by American networks established the adjacent fact the story had been built near: patients, including premature babies, did die in occupied Kuwait’s hospitals when doctors and nurses fled the country and care collapsed—and that Iraqi troops, in that reporting’s judgment, had almost certainly not stolen incubators and left hundreds of babies to die. The investigations also record, and the desk prints, that Iraqi forces committed documented abuses in occupied Kuwait, including against hospitals; it is the incubator-removal charge, specifically, that the evidence failed.

What remains contested. Hill & Knowlton’s president, Thomas E. Eidson, answered the exposure in a letter to The New York Times in January 1992, denying that the firm had at any time collaborated to produce knowingly deceptive testimony, and maintaining that Nayirah had been in Kuwait at the time and had volunteered at the hospital under an assumed name. That account was never reconciled with the investigation record, which found no proof of her presence or of the events, and the question of whether she was in Kuwait at all remains disputed between the firm’s statement and the investigators’ findings. The Kuwaiti government commissioned its own inquiry from Kroll Associates, which worked nine weeks and conducted more than 250 interviews; by the published accounts of that inquiry, Nayirah no longer claimed to have witnessed the scene she had described to the caucus, saying instead that she had seen one baby outside an incubator, briefly. The desk files the three grades as they stand: the staging, the identity, the coaching, and the citations are proven; the central atrocity claim was investigated and retracted; the witness’s own whereabouts, and what the firm knew, remain contested between named parties. The episode is the bridge between this file’s eras: an atrocity story in the First World War’s form—compare Section 7’s corpse factory, admitted unfounded by a foreign secretary in 1925—produced in 1990 not by a ministry but by a public relations firm on a government’s contract, and unmade not by an admission but by investigators with notebooks, arriving after the war it had helped to authorize.

13. The Platform Era: Troll Farms, Commentator Corps, and Governance Boards

The successor to Service A presented itself as a marketing company. The Internet Research Agency, based in St. Petersburg, Russia, operated from 2013 as an organization of salaried posters working shifts under editorial direction, financed—by the American prosecutors’ account—through the Concord companies of Yevgeny Prigozhin under a program named Project Lakhta, with a monthly budget exceeding $1.2 million by 2016. Its staff worked under false identities on foreign social platforms, organizing real-world rallies as well as posting. On February 16, 2018, a United States federal grand jury indicted thirteen Russian nationals and three Russian entities, the agency among them, for conspiracy to defraud the United States through interference in its elections; the Special Counsel’s report of 2019 described the interference as “sweeping and systematic.” The desk files the indictment as an indictment—the defendants were never tried in an American court—and the operation’s existence, staffing, and premises as documented by the indictment, by platform disclosures, and by the Senate Intelligence Committee’s bipartisan volumes. The structural point is the one the chronology makes: the KGB’s disinformation service required a bureaucracy of forgers and cut-outs; its successor required an office, a payroll, and accounts on platforms built by its target country.

China’s documented system works by volume and distraction, and its measure comes from a leak. The Chinese government’s corps of online commentators—formally “internet commentators,” known by the nickname “50 Cent Party” from a rumored per-post payment—was measured in the first large-scale empirical study of the system, by Gary King, Jennifer Pan, and Margaret E. Roberts, published in the American Political Science Review in 2017. Working from a leaked archive of a district Internet Propaganda Office’s correspondence, the researchers identified 43,797 fabricated posts and estimated the government’s total output at some 448 million fabricated social media comments a year. Two of the study’s findings corrected the common account, and the desk prints the corrections: the posters were found to be mostly government employees writing as an ordinary part of their jobs, not freelancers paid by the post; and the posts did not argue with the government’s critics—they avoided controversial subjects and worked by cheerleading and changing the subject, timed in bursts around events with potential for collective action. The study’s figure is an estimate from a sample, and the desk files it as one. The system it describes is Plekhanov’s distinction at industrial scale, with the agitation half automated by volume rather than by oratory.

The platforms themselves are the era’s third apparatus, and they are not states. The distribution of all of the above—state campaigns, commercial campaigns, and ordinary speech—runs through ranking systems operated by a small number of companies, which select what each user sees by criteria the companies set and revise. The documented consequences for this file are two: a state operation no longer needs to own a transmitter, a printing plant, or a news agency to reach a foreign public at scale, which is the material fact underneath the Internet Research Agency’s budget; and the defense against such operations has become, in part, a question of who governs the ranking—the companies, their home governments, or the governments of the audiences reached. That question produced the era’s fourth apparatus, the governance bodies, to which the file now turns.

Three governments’ answers, filed in the order they were built. The European Union established the East StratCom Task Force in its External Action Service in 2015, after the annexation of Crimea, to identify and answer disinformation campaigns directed at the Union and its neighbors; its EUvsDisinfo project maintains a public, searchable database of cases attributed to pro-Kremlin sources, which has passed 12,000 entries—an exposure model: the state publishes its findings and leaves the speech standing. Singapore’s Protection from Online Falsehoods and Manipulation Act, passed in May 2019 and in force from October 2019, empowers ministers to issue Correction Directions requiring that a statement adjudged false be accompanied by a published correction, with criminal penalties behind the direction—a correction model: the state attaches its answer to the speech itself. The United States’ attempt, the Disinformation Governance Board, was an advisory body inside the Department of Homeland Security, formed internally on March 2, 2022 and announced in public on April 27, 2022, when Secretary Alejandro Mayorkas described it in budget testimony to a House subcommittee as a means of coordinating the department’s work against disinformation touching homeland security; Mayorkas stated that the board would have no operational authority and would not monitor American citizens. Its executive director was Nina Jankowicz, a disinformation researcher and former Wilson Center fellow. Within days of the announcement, a video from February 2021 in which Jankowicz sang a parody of the Mary Poppins song “Supercalifragilisticexpialidocious”—rewritten to explain “information laundering,” the obscuring of a falsehood’s origin by repetition through respectable channels—was circulated widely by the board’s critics, who named the board with a label from Orwell’s 1984. On May 18, 2022, twenty-one days after the announcement, the department paused the board’s work and Jankowicz resigned; on August 24, 2022, following the recommendation of the Homeland Security Advisory Council, Mayorkas terminated the board and rescinded its charter. Jankowicz afterward became a vice president at the Centre for Information Resilience, an organization based in the United Kingdom, and in 2024 co-founded the American Sunlight Project, a Washington nonprofit examining disinformation, as its chief executive. The three models stand in the record side by side—exposure, correction, and coordination—and the American entry is the only one of the three that did not survive its first season. The desk prints the sequence without a verdict on any of the three; the reader has the dates.

14. The Shape of the Record

Read end to end, the file’s constants are few and plain. The apparatus follows the medium: stone and bronze for the ancient empires, print for the reformations and revolutions, the subsidized radio receiver and the newsreel for the interwar states, the shortwave station and the front organization for the Cold War, the troll farm and the commentator corps for the platforms. The vocabulary rotates to protect the practitioner: propagation of the faith, enlightenment, publicity, public relations, information, strategic communication, and the governance of disinformation have each named, in their time, work that an adversary in the same years was calling propaganda. The two crafts Plekhanov separated—the full explanation for the few, the single arousing fact for the many—are present in every era, from the Behistun inscription’s theology of truth and lie to the commentator corps’ division between argument avoided and distraction supplied. And the record’s asymmetry, flagged in Section 1, persists: what survives is what the apparatus published and what its opponents and successors later investigated, which is why this file’s most solid sections are the ones where an archive, an indictment, a leaked correspondence file, or a retracted story gives the desk something harder than a claim to print.

The open questions are part of the record, and the desk closes by restating them as open. The authorship and date of the Arthashastra and of The Art of War are unsettled. The circulation of Common Sense is legend, not figure. Whether Goebbels ever opened Bernays’s book is asserted by one interested memoir and corroborated by nothing the desk could find. Whether the United States used biological weapons in Korea was investigated to opposite conclusions in 1952 and after 1998, and scholarship has not closed it. Whether Nayirah al-Sabah was in Kuwait in the autumn of 1990 remains disputed between her sponsors’ account and the investigators’. The measured effect of any single campaign—on an election, a war’s authorization, a public’s mind—is nowhere in this file established to the standard the desk would print as fact, including in the cases where the campaign itself is documented to the last payroll record. The apparatus is documented. The persuasion is asserted. A reader who holds those two sentences apart has the whole of this report, and the desk’s work is done.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
