The Definition File — Coinage, Convention, and the Courts

Objective

Genocide and Conquest: The Definition, the Intent Test, and Where the Courts Drew the Line

“Genocide” is a word with a birth certificate: coined in print in 1944, defined by treaty in 1948 as five acts committed with intent to destroy a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part, as such, and applied by courts only where that intent is proven to the standard the International Court of Justice set—the only reasonable inference from the record. “Conquest” is the older word: it names the transfer of peoples and territory by force, and it carries no legal test at all. The desk files the coinage, the Convention, the older record the word was carried back to, and the judgments, in that order.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 2000 ET, Sunday, November 8, 2026.

The File in Brief

The word is younger than the practice it names, and the record keeps the two apart. Raphael Lemkin, a Polish jurist, coined “genocide” in his 1944 book Axis Rule in Occupied Europe, joining the Greek genos (race, tribe) to the Latin -cide (killing), and defined it as “the destruction of a nation or of an ethnic group”—a coordinated plan against the essential foundations of a group’s life, of which mass killing is one instrument among several. The United Nations General Assembly adopted the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide on December 9, 1948; it entered into force on January 12, 1951. Article I declares the crime punishable “whether committed in time of peace or in time of war.” Article II lists five acts—killing, serious bodily or mental harm, conditions of life calculated to destroy, measures to prevent births, forcible transfer of children—and attaches all five to a single mental element: intent to destroy the group, in whole or in part, as such. The courts call that element dolus specialis, specific intent, and the International Court of Justice ruled in 2015 that it is “the essential characteristic of genocide, which distinguishes it from other crimes.” On that test the adjudicated record is narrow. The Rwanda tribunal’s 1998 judgment in Akayesu was the first genocide conviction by an international court. The Cambodia tribunal convicted the Khmer Rouge leaders Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan of genocide in 2018, for the Vietnamese and, in Nuon Chea’s case, the Cham. The International Court of Justice in 2007 found that the July 1995 killings at Srebrenica—more than 7,000 Bosniak men and boys—were genocide, and found Serbia in breach of its duty to prevent them; the same judgment declined to find genocide across the wider Bosnian war. In 2015 the Court dismissed both Croatia’s claim against Serbia and Serbia’s counter-claim, holding in each direction that the material acts were established and the specific intent was unproven: the campaigns aimed at forced displacement, the record’s word, and displacement stands outside the Article II list. Executive governments apply the word on a different instrument: the United States has issued eight formal genocide determinations since 1993, the eighth being Secretary of State Antony Blinken’s March 21, 2022 determination on the Rohingya, and President Joseph Biden’s April 12, 2022 statement calling Russia’s war in Ukraine genocide was a presidential characterization that no formal determination followed. The word’s spread through print is measurable: Google Books data assembled by the historian Dominik Schaller show decided rises in the second half of the 1960s and in the 1990s, and “genocide” has appeared in books more often than “massacre” since the late 1990s. The older record, the centuries of conquest the word now gets carried back to, was written in a different vocabulary: Thucydides records Athens killing the men of military age it captured on Melos in 416 BC, enslaving the women and children, and settling 500 colonists on the island, and no word in his account corresponds to the 1944 coinage. The desk files the coinage and the Convention first, the older record second, the retrospective application third, and the courts’ line last, closing in a four-column ledger.

1. The Coinage: A Word With a Birth Certificate

Raphael Lemkin put the word into print in November 1944. Axis Rule in Occupied Europe: Laws of Occupation, Analysis of Government, Proposals for Redress, published by the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, is a compilation of the occupation law of Axis Europe, and its ninth chapter, titled “Genocide,” opens with the coinage: “New conceptions require new terms. By ‘genocide’ we mean the destruction of a nation or of an ethnic group. This new word, coined by the author to denote an old practice in its modern development, is made from the ancient Greek word genos (race, tribe) and the Latin cide (killing), thus corresponding in its formation to such words as tyrannicide, homicide, infanticide, etc.” Lemkin’s own biography sits inside the book’s occasion: he was a Polish lawyer of Jewish descent who had fled Poland in 1939, and 49 members of his family were killed in the Holocaust.

Lemkin’s definition ran wider than killing, and he said so in the same passage. “Generally speaking, genocide does not necessarily mean the immediate destruction of a nation, except when accomplished by mass killings of all members of a nation. It is intended rather to signify a coordinated plan of different actions aiming at the destruction of essential foundations of the life of national groups, with the aim of annihilating the groups themselves.” The chapter then works through the fields in which such a plan operates—political, social, cultural, economic, biological, physical, religious, and moral—and states the aim in terms of substitution as well as destruction: the oppressor’s own national pattern imposed on the emptied ground. The object of the crime, in Lemkin’s construction, is the group as an entity; the acts fall on individuals as members of the group.

The word entered a courtroom document within a year, as description rather than as charge. The indictment of the major German war criminals at Nuremberg, filed October 18, 1945, charges under Count Three, war crimes, that the defendants “conducted deliberate and systematic genocide, viz., the extermination of racial and national groups, against the civilian populations of certain occupied territories in order to destroy particular races and classes of people and national, racial or religious groups, particularly Jews, Poles and Gypsies and others.” The Charter of the International Military Tribunal, the instrument that defined the tribunal’s charges, contains no such word; genocide appears in the indictment as a term for conduct charged under the existing counts. On December 11, 1946, the General Assembly’s Resolution 96 (I) affirmed genocide as a crime under international law, and the drafting of a convention followed. The sequence is the file’s spine: a scholar’s coinage in 1944, a prosecutor’s descriptive use in 1945, an Assembly affirmation in 1946, a treaty definition in 1948.

2. The Convention: Five Acts, Four Groups, One Intent

The treaty text is short, and its architecture is exact. Article I: “The Contracting Parties confirm that genocide, whether committed in time of peace or in time of war, is a crime under international law which they undertake to prevent and to punish.” Article II defines the crime as any of five acts “committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such”: (a) killing members of the group; (b) causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group; (c) deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part; (d) imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group; (e) forcibly transferring children of the group to another group. Article III makes punishable the crime itself and, beside it, conspiracy to commit genocide, direct and public incitement, attempt, and complicity. The Convention’s preamble records that “at all periods of history genocide has inflicted great losses on humanity”—the treaty’s own acknowledgment that the practice predates the word. Article IX sends disputes between states over the Convention’s interpretation and application to the International Court of Justice, the door through which every state-versus-state case in this file arrived.

Four features of the definition govern everything the courts have done with it. First, the protected list is closed: national, ethnical, racial, and religious groups. Political groups stand outside it; the destruction of a political class, however complete, is charged under other law. Second, three of the five acts involve no killing: serious mental harm, birth prevention, and child transfer can each complete the crime. Third, the acts carry the crime only when joined to the intent to destroy the group as such; the same killing, committed with the intent to expel, to punish, or to win ground, is a different offense in the Convention’s scheme. Fourth, Article I severs the crime from war in the treaty’s first sentence: the crime exists in peacetime on the same terms as in wartime, and war crimes law, which attaches to armed conflict, has no corresponding clause. The Convention entered into force on January 12, 1951, ninety days after the twentieth ratification, and its definition was carried nearly verbatim into the statutes of the Rwanda and Yugoslavia tribunals, the Cambodia chambers, and the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court.

The intent element is where cases are won and lost, and the courts have priced it high. In its judgment of February 3, 2015, in Croatia v. Serbia, the International Court of Justice stated the doctrine at paragraph 132: the “intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group as such” is “the essential characteristic of genocide, which distinguishes it from other crimes,” a dolus specialis, a specific intent, which must be present in addition to the intent required for each individual act. Where intent is to be inferred from a pattern of conduct, the Court’s standing test, applied in both the 2007 and 2015 judgments, requires that the genocidal intent be the only reasonable inference the pattern supports. Discriminatory intent—targeting members of a group for abuse, expulsion, or killing as enemies—does not by itself satisfy the test; the inference must run to destruction of the group, in whole or in part, as an end. Every judgment in section 6 of this report turns on that paragraph and that test.

3. The Older Record: Conquest Before the Word

The ancient record describes the outcomes in the vocabulary of war and victory, and the outcomes themselves need no translation. Thucydides, in Book V of his History of the Peloponnesian War, records the Athenian expedition against the island of Melos in 416 BC: a neutral Dorian island that refused submission to the Athenian alliance. His account gives the negotiation as the Melian Dialogue, in which the Athenian envoys state the operating rule in the open—right is in question between equals in power; the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must—and then gives the result in one sentence: the Melians surrendered at discretion, the Athenians put to death all the grown men they had taken, sold the women and children for slaves, and sent out 500 colonists to settle the place themselves. The passage is the classical world’s own description of a conquest that ended a people’s civic existence on its island. Thucydides files it as war.

Carthage supplies the largest ancient specimen, and its numbers come with their instruments attached. The Yale historian Ben Kiernan’s study “The First Genocide: Carthage, 146 BC,” published in Diogenes in 2004, assembles the ancient accounts: Cato the Censor’s standing close to his Senate speeches from 153 BC, Delenda est Carthago—Carthage must be destroyed—which Kiernan identifies as possibly the first recorded incitement to genocide; the three-year siege; a population he puts at 200,000 to 400,000, of whom at least 150,000 perished; and the end in 146 BC, when Scipio Aemilianus razed the city and dispersed the 55,000 survivors, including 25,000 women, into slavery. Kiernan also records the limits of the file: the famous salting of the earth appears in no ancient source, and the earliest claim of it he traces dates to 1930. The ancient writers themselves—Polybius, an eyewitness attached to Scipio’s staff, and Appian, writing three centuries later—describe destruction, extermination of the city’s population in the fighting, and enslavement of the remnant, in the vocabulary available to them. The word applied to the event in Kiernan’s title dates from 1944.

Lemkin himself drew the line of descent, in a footnote. Chapter nine of Axis Rule cites, as classical examples of wars of extermination in which nations and groups were completely or almost completely destroyed, the destruction of Carthage in 146 BC and of Jerusalem by Titus, the religious wars of Islam and the Crusades, the massacres of the Albigenses and the Waldenses, and the siege of Magdeburg in the Thirty Years’ War, and adds the wholesale massacres in the wars of Genghis Khan and Tamerlane. His unfinished History of Genocide, planned in three volumes and left in manuscript at his death in 1959, assigned nine chapters to antiquity—among them Assyria, Carthage, Gaul, and the Celts—twelve to the Middle Ages, among them the Albigensians, the Mongols, the Moors, and medieval England, and forty-one to modern times. The retrospective application of the word, documented in section 4, begins with the word’s own inventor, and it begins as history rather than as law: Lemkin’s chapters judge the dead by a definition written in 1944, and no court has ever been asked to do the same, the Convention having no retroactive reach.

4. The Word Carried Backward: Retrospective Application

The scholarly application of “genocide” to conquests widened in steps, and the steps are datable. The word’s frequency in print, measured in the Google Books corpus of more than five million books and assembled by Dominik Schaller in Genocide Studies and Prevention in 2011, shows no single explosion: decided rises came in the second half of the 1960s—Schaller and the literature after him associate the rise with the fiftieth anniversary of the Armenian genocide in 1965, with the violence of post-independence Rwanda and Burundi, and with the Vietnam War and the American civil-rights movement—and again in the 1990s, with the field of genocide studies expanding rapidly after the Cold War’s end. Since the late 1990s the corpus has used “genocide” more often than “massacre.” The retrospective shelf grew with the frequency: David E. Stannard’s American Holocaust: Columbus and the Conquest of the New World (Oxford University Press, 1992) argued that within twenty-one years of Columbus’s first landing the island he renamed Hispaniola was effectively desolate, nearly 8,000,000 people killed, in Stannard’s accounting, by violence, disease, and despair; that historical demographers had found post-contact depopulation rates of 90 to 98 percent region by region, with an overall decline of about 95 percent as a working rule of thumb; and that the destruction of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas was “far and away, the most massive act of genocide in the history of the world.” The desk files Stannard’s numbers as his: the pre-contact population of Hispaniola is contested across a wide range in the demographic literature, and Stannard’s figures stand at the high end of it, a placement his appendix argues for directly.

States have also applied the word backward, and the instruments they use record the strain of doing so. In May 2021, after negotiations begun in 2015, Germany and Namibia initialled a joint declaration on the German colonial war of 1904 to 1908 in what is now Namibia, in which General Lothar von Trotha’s extermination order of October 2, 1904 stands at the head of a campaign in which, on the standard estimates, about 65,000 Herero—about 80 percent of the people—and about 10,000 Nama—about 50 percent—perished by killing, starvation, and the camps. The declaration’s operative sentence is written in the present tense looking back: the atrocities “culminated in events that, from today’s perspective, would be called genocide.” Germany coupled the acknowledgment to €1.1 billion over thirty years for reconstruction and development programs, and both governments framed the money as reconciliation; the declaration carries no legal duty of reparations, Germany’s stated ground being that the prohibition of genocide in international law postdates 1904 to 1908. Descendant organizations of the Herero and Nama rejected the declaration, on the record, for negotiating over their heads and for the exclusion of reparations. In the same year, 2021, the United States recognized the Armenian genocide of 1915 to 1916, a determination that arrived a century after the events and against the background of the Ottoman Empire’s wartime deportations and massacres. In each case the naming instrument is political—a declaration, a presidential statement—and in each case the namer states, or the record shows, that the legal category postdates the events it is carried back to.

5. Genocide Without War: The Crime Detached From Battle

Article I’s clause—in time of peace or in time of war—has a case record, and Cambodia is its center. On November 16, 2018, the Trial Chamber of the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia convicted Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan, the two surviving senior leaders of Democratic Kampuchea, of genocide, and sentenced both to life imprisonment, for crimes committed between April 17, 1975 and January 6, 1979. Both men were convicted of genocide against the Vietnamese ethnic, national, and racial group; Nuon Chea was convicted in addition of genocide against the Cham ethnic and religious group, on the basis of his superior responsibility. The convictions arose from a state’s campaign against groups inside its own territory and under its own administration—minorities marked out within a population the regime already ruled—and the same judgment’s structure displays the Convention’s boundary from the other side: the far larger killing of ethnic Khmers by the Khmer Rouge, the regime’s own national group destroyed in the hundreds of thousands as class and political enemies, was prosecuted in the case as crimes against humanity, because Article II’s closed list covers national, ethnical, racial, and religious groups and the Khmer victims were targeted as political and social categories. The largest part of one of the century’s largest killings stands outside the genocide counts of its own tribunal, on the text of the definition.

The first conviction under the Convention carries the same lesson in miniature. On September 2, 1998, the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda convicted Jean-Paul Akayesu, the bourgmestre of Taba commune, on nine of fifteen counts, including genocide and direct and public incitement to commit genocide, and sentenced him to life imprisonment—the first genocide conviction by an international tribunal, fifty years after the Convention’s adoption. The judgment also held, for the first time in an international court, that rape and sexual violence can constitute acts of genocide when committed with the specific intent, under the Article II head of causing serious bodily or mental harm. The Rwanda killings of 1994 ran alongside a civil war, and the tribunal’s statute covered both; the genocide counts in the judgment attach to intent and to the protected group, and the war supplies setting rather than element. Article III completes the detachment: conspiracy and direct and public incitement are punishable as such, so the crime in law can be complete before any of the five acts occurs, in a radio studio as readily as on a battlefield.

6. War Without the Finding: The Intent Test Applied

The Bosnian case is the fullest worked example of a war passing through the test and emerging divided. Bosnia and Herzegovina sued Serbia and Montenegro under Article IX in 1993, alleging a campaign of genocide across the war of 1992 to 1995. In its judgment of February 26, 2007, the International Court of Justice concluded that the acts committed at Srebrenica in July 1995—the killing of more than 7,000 Bosniak men and boys by forces of the Republika Srpska—fell within Article II (a) and (b) and were committed with the specific intent to destroy in part the group of the Muslims of Bosnia and Herzegovina as such, and were accordingly acts of genocide. The Court found that Serbia had neither committed that genocide nor been complicit in it, and it found Serbia in breach of the Article I obligation to prevent it, and of its obligations to cooperate with the Yugoslavia tribunal, including by failing to transfer Ratko Mladić. The wider campaign—killings, detention camps, and mass expulsion across Bosnia, documented in the same record—produced no genocide finding. One enclave in eleven days met the test; a three-year war around it did not, and the difference the Court recorded is the presence, at Srebrenica, of an intent it could name.

The Croatia case ran the instrument in both directions and it returned the same reading twice. Croatia claimed Serbia was responsible for genocide in the war of 1991 to 1995; Serbia counter-claimed over Operation Storm of August 1995, in which more than 200,000 Serbs fled or were expelled from the Krajina. On February 3, 2015, the Court rejected Croatia’s claim by fifteen votes to two and Serbia’s counter-claim unanimously, in a single judgment. For each claim the Court found the actus reus established—killings and serious harm within Article II’s list, committed by the other side’s forces—and the dolus specialis unproven: the conduct, on the evidence, aimed at the forced displacement of the population from territory, and displacement, however violent, is an aim the Convention’s list does not convert into destruction of the group. The judgment is the standing citation for the gap this report files: a war can satisfy the physical half of the definition at scale, on both sides, and satisfy the mental half on neither.

Darfur shows the same events passing through two different instruments and returning two different answers, six years apart. The United States ran its own instrument first: a State Department team interviewed 1,136 Darfurian refugees at nineteen locations in eastern Chad between July 12 and August 18, 2004, of whom 74 percent reported Sudanese military involvement in the attacks on their villages, and on September 9, 2004 Secretary of State Colin Powell told the Senate Foreign Relations Committee that “genocide has been committed in Darfur and the Government of Sudan and the jinjaweed bear responsibility—and genocide may still be occurring”—the first invocation of the Convention by a sitting United States official for an ongoing crisis. The International Criminal Court’s instrument ran slower and under judicial standards: its first warrant for President Omar Al Bashir, March 4, 2009, charged war crimes and crimes against humanity and carried no genocide count, the Pre-Trial Chamber’s majority holding the genocidal intent not then established to its standard; after the Appeals Chamber ruled that standard too demanding for the warrant stage, a second warrant issued on July 12, 2010, adding three counts of genocide—by killing, by causing serious bodily or mental harm, and by deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to destroy—against the Fur, Masalit, and Zaghawa groups, on reasonable grounds to believe Al Bashir acted with the specific intent. Al Bashir was never surrendered to the Court and never tried. The executive determination of 2004 and the judicial warrant of 2010 name the same crime over the same ground; the six years between them are the distance between the two instruments’ standards of proof.

7. The Word in Present Wars: Determinations, Characterizations, and Provisional Measures

Governments now apply the word through a standing administrative practice, and the practice has a ledger. Secretary Blinken’s statement of March 21, 2022, at the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, announcing his determination that members of the Burmese military committed genocide and crimes against humanity against the Rohingya, counted the practice aloud: “Beyond the Holocaust, the United States has concluded that genocide was committed seven times. Today marks the eighth.” The seven, in the order the United States recognized them, were Bosnia, Rwanda, Iraq, Darfur, the territories ISIS held and the Yazidis it targeted, China and the Uyghurs, and Armenia. A determination is an executive act: it carries political and statutory consequences inside the issuing government and binds no court. Beside the ledger stands the looser instrument of characterization. On April 12, 2022, in Iowa, President Biden said of Russia’s war in Ukraine, “Yes, I called it genocide,” adding that it had become clearer that President Putin was “just trying to wipe out the idea of even being able to be Ukrainian,” and in the same exchange assigned the legal question elsewhere: “We’ll let the lawyers decide internationally whether or not it qualifies, but it sure seems that way to me.” No formal State Department genocide determination on Ukraine followed; the Department’s formal accusation of March 23, 2022 charged war crimes. Other governments divided on the word in public: President Emmanuel Macron of France declined to adopt it for Ukraine, describing Russian actions as war crimes. Russia’s own stated ground for its invasion included the claim that Ukraine was committing genocide in the Donbas, and Ukraine carried that claim to the International Court of Justice as the applicant: in its judgment on preliminary objections of February 2, 2024, the Court found jurisdiction to entertain Ukraine’s request for a declaration that there is no credible evidence Ukraine committed genocide in the Donetsk and Luhansk oblasts, and found it lacked jurisdiction over Ukraine’s submissions on Russia’s use of force; by an order of December 5, 2025, the Court found Russia’s counter-claims in the case admissible, with pleadings now running into 2027. The word in that file has been claim, counter-claim, and stated cause of war, and the Court has so far ruled on which questions it may hear.

At the Court, the live genocide cases stand at the provisional stage, and the provisional stage has a precise meaning that the record states in its own terms. In The Gambia v. Myanmar, the Court’s unanimous order of January 23, 2020 found the rights The Gambia claimed for the Rohingya plausible, found a real and imminent risk of irreparable prejudice to them, and ordered Myanmar to take all measures within its power to prevent acts within Article II; the order decided nothing on the merits, which remain pending. In South Africa v. Israel, filed December 29, 2023, South Africa alleges that Israel’s campaign in Gaza breaches the Convention; Israel’s stated position, presented at the January 2024 hearings, is that the case presents a grossly distorted picture, that Israel is fighting Hamas in self-defense after the October 7, 2023 attack, and that the intent element of genocide is, in its counsel’s words, completely missing. The Court’s order of January 26, 2024 found at least some of the rights South Africa claimed plausible—including the right of Palestinians in Gaza to be protected from acts of genocide—and indicated provisional measures; further orders of March 28 and May 24, 2024, the last by thirteen votes to two, ordered Israel immediately to halt its military offensive in the Rafah Governorate and any other action there risking conditions of life capable of bringing about the group’s physical destruction in whole or in part. The Court has issued no finding that genocide occurred; its then-president, Judge Joan Donoghue, stated publicly in April 2024 that the Court decided the Palestinians had a plausible right to protection and did not decide that the claim of genocide was plausible. A separate United Nations body, the Human Rights Council’s Commission of Inquiry, concluded in September 2025 that genocide had been committed in Gaza, finding four of the five Article II acts and intent as the only reasonable inference; Israel rejects the Commission’s findings, and the Commission is an investigative body whose conclusions bind no court. Written pleadings in the Court case run to 2029. The desk files the three instruments at their own grades: a court’s provisional measures, which protect rights pending judgment; an inquiry’s conclusions, which persuade or fail to; and a merits judgment, which does not yet exist in the case.

8. The Two Categories, as the Record Defines Them

War and genocide, in the instruments this file has used, are defined on different axes, and the adjudicated record shows the overlap and the remainder on both sides. The laws of war attach to armed conflict: the Geneva Conventions of 1949 govern international and non-international armed conflict, and a war crime in the tribunal statutes requires conduct linked to such a conflict. The category is defined by the parties and the fighting. Genocide is defined by a target and an intent: a protected group, and the purpose of destroying it in whole or in part. Scale appears in neither definition as a threshold. The genocide finding of the modern record rests on eleven days and more than 7,000 victims at Srebrenica, because the intent was proven; the expulsion of more than 200,000 people in Operation Storm drew no such finding, because it was not. Of the three tribunal matters that have produced genocide convictions—Rwanda in 1998, Srebrenica in the Yugoslavia tribunal’s judgments, and Cambodia in 2018—two arose inside wars and one inside a regime’s administration of its own state. The executive ledger and the press usage run wider than the judicial record in every period the file covers, and the distance between the word as governments and newspapers use it and the word as courts apply it is the quantity the cases measure: identical in spelling, different in standard, and separated, in the instances documented here, by the single element of proven intent to destroy a group as such. The older word, conquest, belongs to neither axis: no treaty defines it, no court applies it, and its record—Melos emptied and recolonized, Carthage razed and its survivors enslaved, peoples transferred and territories annexed across the centuries the word governed—was written by its makers as victory and by its subjects as catastrophe, in vocabularies that predate the legal one by two thousand years and more.

Claimed-but-unproven: asserted by named makers, awaiting or lacking adjudication. Disproven: tested by the instrument built to test it and failed.

The claim that the International Court of Justice found genocide, or a plausible claim of genocide, in Gaza: the Court’s January 2024 order found claimed rights plausible and its then-president stated on the record that it did not decide the claim of genocide was plausible; no merits finding exists. The claim that mass expulsion satisfies the Convention: tested in Croatia v. Serbia (2015) with the acts established on both sides, and failed on intent in both directions. The salting of Carthage’s earth: no ancient source records it; the earliest trace Kiernan found dates to 1930. The claim that genocide requires a war: Article I’s text and the Cambodia convictions stand against it. The claim that the Nuremberg Charter charged genocide: the word appears in the indictment’s description under Count Three and in no article of the Charter.

The file, closed, reads as follows. A word coined in 1944 for an old practice acquired, in 1948, a definition with four protected groups, five acts, and one intent; the courts that apply the definition have sustained it in a handful of matters and refused it in wars whose violence is not in dispute; governments apply the word by determination and characterization at lower standards and on faster clocks; and the centuries of conquest that precede the word sit in the record under their own vocabulary, carried into the modern one by historians and declarations that state, in their own texts, that the category postdates the events. The distinction the instruments draw is consistent across all of it: war is measured by its parties and its conduct, genocide by its target and its proven intent, and the two measures return different answers on the same ground often enough that the cases, rather than the vocabulary, are where the line is kept.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
