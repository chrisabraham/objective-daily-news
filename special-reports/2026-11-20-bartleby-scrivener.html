Objective

Bartleby the Scrivener: Could It Happen in New York Today?

Herman Melville’s “Bartleby, the Scrivener,” published in 1853, records a Wall Street copyist who stops work, takes up residence in his employer’s office, is arrested as a vagrant, refuses food in the Tombs, and dies in the prison yard. The desk audited the story beat by beat against two records: what stood between a destitute man and death in the New York of 1853, and what stands there in 2026. Some of the story’s beats can no longer occur. Some now occur by different machinery. Some still occur. The record, section by section, follows.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1330 ET, Friday, November 20, 2026.

The Record in Brief

The story’s beats divide into three classes under the modern record. Two beats are now impossible as written: no American jurisdiction may arrest a man for vagrancy as a condition of being—the Supreme Court voided the classic vagrancy ordinances in 1972—and no pretrial detainee in New York may be warehoused unexamined while he starves, because the charge the story’s conduct now maps to carries mandatory release, and a refusal to eat in custody triggers medical monitoring and, at the point of danger, a court proceeding on feeding that New York’s highest court resolved in the state’s favor in 2013. Three beats are rerouted: the dismissal, the removal from the office, and the arrest each still happen, but each now generates process—a personally communicated order, a desk appearance ticket or an arraignment inside about a day, a competency examination if the defendant cannot or will not participate. One beat remains possible, and it is the story’s first: the refusal itself. New York City holds a legal right to shelter unique in the United States, and in the fiscal year ending June 30, 2025, it recorded 634 deaths of people experiencing homelessness, 329 of them people classified as non-sheltered. Fifteen people died in the city’s jail custody in 2025. The nets exist, are named in law, and are documented in operation. They are also, by design and by constitutional ruling, nets a competent adult may decline.

The Story, Retold in Full

The narrator is an elderly Wall Street lawyer, a Master in Chancery, who describes himself as an eminently safe man doing a snug business in rich men’s bonds, mortgages, and title-deeds from chambers at No. — Wall Street. His copyists—the scriveners who reproduced legal documents by hand, four copies at a time—are Turkey, an Englishman near sixty, steady in the morning and reckless after his midday dinner; Nippers, a young man of ambition and indigestion; and Ginger Nut, the office boy. Needing a third copyist, the lawyer advertises, and Bartleby answers: a man with no first name in the story and no history, “pallidly neat, pitiably respectable, incurably forlorn.” He is set behind a folding screen in the lawyer’s own room, so the lawyer can summon him without leaving his chair, and at first he copies steadily and silently, day and night.

On the third day, asked to help examine a document, Bartleby answers: “I would prefer not to.” He says it mildly, gives no reason, and returns to his screen. The refusals widen. He will not examine copies. Then he will not copy. Then he does nothing at all, standing at his window in what the lawyer calls dead-wall reveries, gazing at the brick wall ten feet away. He does not quarrel, steal, drink, or raise his voice. He declines, in the same formula, every request put to him, including requests to explain himself.

The lawyer, arriving one Sunday morning, finds his office door locked from inside and Bartleby in his shirt-sleeves: the scrivener has been living in the chambers. His whole estate is there—a blanket, a tin basin, a razor, a small store of ginger-nuts and cheese. He has no other home. The lawyer is moved, then worried, then, as visiting attorneys remark on the strange creature kept in the office and his professional reputation begins to suffer, resolved to be rid of him. He dismisses him with extra pay; Bartleby leaves the money untouched under the paperweight. He offers him other situations, one by one—copying elsewhere, a clerkship in a dry-goods store, bartending, traveling to collect bills, a companion’s place in Europe—and each is declined. He offers his own dwelling: come home with me, live there until some arrangement can be made. Bartleby: “No: at present I would prefer not to make any change at all.”

Unable to move the man, the lawyer moves himself. He transfers his offices to new chambers and gives fair notice; on removal day Bartleby remains standing behind the screen until it is folded away, and is left the motionless occupant of a naked room. Days later the incoming tenant and the landlord descend on the lawyer: Bartleby has been turned out of the room and now haunts the building, sitting on the banisters by day and sleeping in the entry by night; clients are leaving; something must be done. The lawyer makes a final attempt in person and fails. The landlord then sends to the police, and Bartleby is removed to the Tombs—the city’s Halls of Justice—as a vagrant. He offers no resistance. He goes silently, arm in arm with a constable, through the noon streets.

In the Tombs, being under no disgraceful charge, he is permitted to wander the prison and its enclosed grass yard. The lawyer visits, vouches for him as a perfectly honest man greatly to be compassionated, and suggests that if nothing else can be decided upon, the almshouse must receive him. He hires the prison’s grub-man—a contractor, Mr. Cutlets, who for a fee provides prisoners who have friends with dinners better than the prison fare—to give Bartleby the best dinner he can get. Bartleby declines it: “I prefer not to dine to-day. It would disagree with me; I am unused to dinners.” He takes his position facing the yard’s high wall and says, to the lawyer, “I know where I am.” Some few days later the lawyer returns and finds him in the yard, huddled at the base of the wall, knees drawn up, head on the cold stones, his dim eyes open, dead. The grub-man peers in: “His dinner is ready. Won’t he dine to-day, either? Or does he live without dining?” “Lives without dining,” the lawyer says, and closes his eyes.

The story closes with a rumor the narrator could never verify: that Bartleby had been a subordinate clerk in the Dead Letter Office at Washington, removed by a change of administration—a man who had spent his working life handling letters that never reached anyone, sorted by the cart-load for the flames. “Ah Bartleby! Ah humanity!” Project Gutenberg, the story itself

New York, 1853: What Stood Between a Man and the Ground

The story appeared anonymously, in two installments, in Putnam’s Monthly Magazine in November and December 1853, and was collected in Melville’s The Piazza Tales in 1856. It was scantly reviewed; the short story was then a minor form, and the wide critical attention the tale now receives dates from the Melville revival of the 1920s. Scholars identify an advertisement for a novel, The Lawyer’s Story, printed in the Tribune and the Times on February 18, 1853, as a principal prompt. Nothing in the record suggests the events were reported fact; the audit that follows tests the fiction against the machinery of its own city and year, and of ours. New American Studies Journal, the story’s reception Wikipedia, the story’s publication record

The machinery of 1853 was real, and it was sorting machinery. The city’s almshouse department ran the institutions on Blackwell’s Island in the East River, where by 1850 the poor were classified into separate establishments: an almshouse for the destitute, a lunatic asylum (opened 1839, then the largest in the country), a penitentiary, and provision for vagrants, who were classed to labor in the workhouse; when the crush of vagrants exceeded the workhouse, its overflow—2,500 people in 1851—was confined with the convicts in the penitentiary. Between 1853 and 1856 the new almshouse held some 7,000 people, and the cost of operating it rose 240 percent. The city also distributed outdoor relief, aid in the home, through the almshouse department—a practice the private Association for Improving the Condition of the Poor, founded in 1843 under Robert M. Hartley, campaigned against as indiscriminate. The AICP divided the city into sections, assigned a visitor to each, investigated applicants in their homes, and made its relief an instrument of supervision: not alms, but inspection. Churches and private charity stood alongside, uncounted and uncoordinated. New York City HRA, the history of relief in the city Burrows and Wallace, Gotham, on Blackwell’s Island

At the bottom of the system stood the charge Bartleby received. Vagrancy in 1853 New York was a police-court matter of status as much as conduct: the narrator, debating what ground could possibly hold his scrivener, runs the categories himself—“a vagrant, is he? What! he a vagrant, a wanderer, who refuses to budge? It is because he will not be a vagrant, then, that you seek to count him as a vagrant”—and settles, wrongly as he admits, on “no visible means of support.” A man taken up as a vagrant went before a magistrate and could be committed to the island: to the workhouse if he was classed idle, to the almshouse if he was classed destitute. The Tombs, where Bartleby was held, was the city’s Halls of Justice, opened in 1838 on the filled bed of the Collect Pond: an Egyptian Revival pile of granite housing courts, police, and detention in one complex, designed for about 300 prisoners. Built on lashed hemlock piles over the pond’s bed, it began to sink within months of opening; grand juries repeatedly condemned it as damp and unhealthy. It was, in the historian Timothy Gilfoyle’s phrase, the physical representation of nineteenth-century criminal justice in the city—and its regime ran on informal arrangement: access, food, and conditions were negotiated, which is how a private grub-man came to sell dinners inside a public prison, and how a prisoner under no disgraceful charge came to wander its yard unexamined until he died in it. Correction History, a tale of the Tombs

Was the trajectory ordinary in its own time? The record supports a divided answer. Each institutional step existed and was routine: men were taken up as vagrants in large numbers, committed to the island, and fed or not fed according to the arrangements they could make. What the record does not show as routine is the story’s pacing. Inside the fiction, every other character—the visiting attorneys, the incoming tenant, the landlord—treats the arrangement as an unheard-of scandal, and the narrator’s months of forbearance, his offers of money, of five other livelihoods, and of his own home, are the story’s device, not its documentary. And the refusal that drives the plot was already, in 1853, a refusal of the nets that existed: the almshouse was named to Bartleby as the fallback and he would not go; the paid dinners were set before him and he would not eat. The fall was ordinary machinery. The man declined every hand the machinery and his employer extended, in a city whose entire apparatus for such a man consisted of classification, commitment, and a yard.

Beat One: The Work Stops

In 2026 the first beat runs faster than in the story, and lawfully. New York employment is at will: an employer may dismiss an employee who declines the work, for that reason alone, without process, notice period, or severance. Bartleby’s refusal to copy is not an offense; neither is his silence. The modern lawyer need not move his offices, need not wait out months of reverie, and need not debate his conscience in print: the employment ends when he says it ends. What the modern record adds is a fork the 1853 office did not contain. An employee who stops working in the manner described—abruptly, totally, without explanation—presents, to an employer and to any clinician who later sees him, a presentation consistent with illness, and the systems that meet him downstream are built to test that possibility before they test anything else. The story’s employer had no instrument between indulgence and the constable. The modern sequence has several, and they begin before any arrest.

Beat Two: The Residence in the Office

The beat on which the story turns—the scrivener discovered living behind his screen, and left there—cannot stabilize in 2026. An employee’s presence in an office is a license, and under New York Penal Law a person remains unlawfully, for trespass purposes, when he is not licensed or privileged to remain; where premises are open, the license ends when a lawful order not to remain is personally communicated by the owner or an authorized person. A dismissed employee told to leave who stays is therefore exposed, on the employer’s word to the police, to removal the same day: the conduct maps to trespass, a violation under Penal Law § 140.05 (knowingly entering or remaining unlawfully on premises), and to criminal trespass in the third degree, a class B misdemeanor under § 140.10, where an aggravating condition such as an enclosed building is present. Separately, an office is not a dwelling: its certificate of occupancy does not permit residence, and an occupant who holds no lease may also be removed by civil proceeding. The 1853 lawyer’s dilemma—that he could not lawfully thrust the man out and so vacated his own chambers—does not survive contact with the modern code. The removal is rerouted, not abolished: it takes an order, a communication, and officers, and it happens in days. New York Court of Appeals, People v. Barnes, on the trespass ladder

Beat Three: The Arrest, and the Charge That No Longer Exists

The story’s arrest is the beat the modern record refuses outright. Bartleby is taken “as a vagrant”—for what he is, not for anything he has done. That charge no longer exists in American law. In Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville, 405 U.S. 156 (1972), the Supreme Court held a classic vagrancy ordinance—punishing, among others, “persons wandering or strolling around from place to place without any lawful purpose or object” and “habitual loafers”—void for vagueness: it failed to give fair notice, it made criminal activities innocent by modern standards, and it placed almost unfettered discretion in the hands of the police. The ordinances of Bartleby’s New York were of the same family. A 2026 Bartleby can be arrested only for conduct: here, remaining in a building after a personally communicated order to leave. Cornell Legal Information Institute, Papachristou v. Jacksonville

The process that follows the conduct charge is built to be short. Since January 1, 2020, New York police are required to issue desk appearance tickets for most misdemeanors—a paper court date and release from the station—rather than hold the person for arraignment; where the person is held, court-system guidance puts arraignment at roughly a day after arrest. At arraignment, the charge decides whether detention is even available: for trespass and most other misdemeanors, which are not qualifying offenses under Criminal Procedure Law § 510.10(4), the judge must release the defendant; money bail and remand are off the table. Desk Appearance Ticket, the 2020 rules explained The story’s months of drift thus compress, in the modern sequence, into hours of custody at most—and the likeliest outcome of the arrest is that Bartleby walks out of the precinct with a ticket, unexamined still, which is where the next beat begins.

Beat Four: The Examination

What 1853 did not possess, at any price, was a procedure for asking what was wrong with the man. The Tombs held him under no disgraceful charge and asked him nothing. The modern system asks, compulsively, at three separate doors. First, the criminal door: under Article 730 of the Criminal Procedure Law, a court must issue an order of examination when it is of the opinion that a defendant may be an incapacitated person—one who, as a result of mental disease or defect, lacks capacity to understand the proceedings or to assist in his own defense. A defendant who will not speak, will not instruct counsel, and will not participate is the paradigm case for the order; two psychiatric examiners report, the court holds a hearing, and a defendant found incapacitated on a misdemeanor is routed to observation and treatment rather than to trial, with the constitutional limit of Jackson v. Indiana (1972) barring indefinite holding for restoration. Second, the hospital door: under Mental Hygiene Law § 9.41, a police officer may take into custody any person who appears to be mentally ill and is conducting himself in a manner likely to result in serious harm to himself or others, and remove him to a hospital or comprehensive psychiatric emergency program for evaluation; under § 9.39, a hospital may admit and retain such a person for up to 15 days on physicians’ findings. Since August 7, 2025, the statute states expressly what case law had built into it: “likely to result in serious harm” includes a substantial risk of physical harm due to an inability or refusal, as a result of mental illness, to provide for one’s own essential needs—food, clothing, necessary medical care, personal safety, or shelter. A man found living in an office, eating ginger-nuts, declining all food and all change, is the fact pattern the amended definition describes. Third, the community door: Kendra’s Law (Mental Hygiene Law § 9.60, enacted 1999) authorizes court-ordered assisted outpatient treatment for a person whose history meets its criteria. None of these doors existed in 1853. Every one of them generates an examination, a finding, and a record. Beat Five: Custody, and the Refused Meal

The Tombs itself is gone four times over: the name passed from the 1838 Halls of Justice to successor jails on and near the site, last to the Manhattan Detention Complex at 125 White Street, whose towers were demolished beginning in 2023 for the borough-based jail program; the city’s pretrial population is now held principally on Rikers Island. Custody in 2026 is not gentle, and the counter-record below states its death toll in full. But the specific death the story records—a detainee refusing food for days, unmonitored, while the only food intervention is a private caterer hired by a visitor—runs against the modern machinery at every point. Food in custody is the custodian’s obligation, not a contractor’s favor. A detainee who stops eating enters a documented protocol: medical monitoring, capacity evaluation, and, where refusal continues toward danger, a court proceeding. New York’s highest court has decided that proceeding’s outcome. In Matter of Bezio v. Dorsey, 21 NY3d 93 (2013), the Court of Appeals upheld an order authorizing prison officials to feed a mentally competent, serial hunger-striking inmate by nasogastric tube after he had lost 11.6 percent of his body weight in four weeks and faced organ failure: the state’s interest in preserving the life of a person in its custody, and in the order of its institutions, outweighed the inmate’s claim to refuse nourishment as a protest strategy. The ruling concerned a sentenced prisoner; its holding is the state’s answer to the question Bartleby poses, and no New York authority permits the opposite—a custodian watching a charge starve without acting. The yard in which a man could lie down unnoticed and die has been replaced by a yard in which his refusal becomes a case file. New York Court of Appeals, Matter of Bezio v. Dorsey

The Nets, Named With Their Instruments

Between the office and the street, the 2026 record interposes a set of named instruments, none of which existed in 1853. Shelter: under the consent decree in Callahan v. Carey, finalized August 26, 1981, New York City must provide shelter and board to each homeless man who applies and meets its terms—a legal right to shelter, grounded in Article XVII of the state constitution, extended to women in 1983 and to families thereafter, and unique among American cities. The decree’s lead plaintiff, Robert Callahan, himself died on the street in the autumn of 1980, before the decree bearing his name was signed; that fact belongs to the counter-record and is printed there. Coalition for the Homeless, the Callahan legacy Income: Supplemental Security Income and Social Security Disability Insurance pay federal cash benefits to people whose disability prevents substantial work; the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program pays for food; Medicaid pays for care, and New York expanded it. Medicine: since 1986, the federal Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act has required Medicare-participating hospitals—nearly all hospitals—to screen and stabilize any person who comes to an emergency department, regardless of ability to pay. Outreach: the city spends $368 million in fiscal 2025 on street outreach, drop-in centers, and low-barrier beds, up from $102 million in fiscal 2019, and its HOME-STAT teams maintain a by-name record of the unsheltered—3,724 people confirmed and being engaged as of September 30, 2025. New York City DHS, the HOME-STAT record In 1853 the entire apparatus was a visitor from the AICP with a notebook, an almshouse on an island, and whatever a man’s employer chose to feel. The Counter-Record

The objective lens cuts both ways, and the record against the nets is documented in the same official hand as the record for them. New York City’s twentieth annual report on deaths among persons experiencing homelessness, covering July 1, 2024 through June 30, 2025, counts 634 deaths. Of those, 329—52 percent—were people classified as non-sheltered; 58 percent of all decedents died in a hospital, and drug-related causes accounted for 251 deaths, 40 percent. The prior fiscal year’s count, 770, was the highest since the city began publishing the report in 2005. The annual HOPE survey estimated 4,504 people sleeping unsheltered on the night of January 28, 2025—about 3.5 percent of the city’s homeless population, the lowest proportion of any major American city, and still, in absolute terms, four and a half thousand people outside on a winter night, in the one American city where a bed is a legal entitlement. The right to shelter is a right to claim. Nothing in the decree, or in any statute the desk can find, makes it a duty to accept. New York City DOHMH and DSS, the twentieth annual report New York City DSS, the HOPE 2025 estimate

Refusal, measured, is not rare. The city Comptroller’s audit of the encampment cleanups conducted between March 21 and November 30, 2022 found 2,308 people present at the sites; 119 of them, 5 percent, accepted the temporary shelter the outreach teams offered. Auditors revisiting 99 cleared sites in April 2023 found homeless activity resumed at 31 percent. What refusal means is itself disputed in the record, and the dispute is printed, not blended. Researchers at the NYU Silver School of Social Work, interviewing 43 people living unsheltered in Manhattan, concluded that the refusals they documented were driven by the machinery’s friction—eligibility rules requiring a person to be seen bedded down in the same place repeatedly and to have lived on the street at least nine months, documentation demands such as a birth certificate, shelters that exclude pets and are perceived as unsafe, and waits of months or years for the permanent housing that is the offer’s real object: in the researchers’ phrase, the people they interviewed were not “service resistant” but rational actors familiar with unkept promises. The city’s own former mayor, announcing expanded outreach in 2018, said it can take dozens or more contacts to persuade a person to come in off the streets. Both accounts agree on the operative fact: each net is opt-in, each opt-in carries friction, and the friction falls hardest on the capacity—initiating, documenting, persisting, appearing—whose impairment is frequently the reason the person is on the street. New York City Comptroller, the encampment audit NYU Silver School of Social Work, the refusal study

Custody’s counter-record is likewise official. The city’s Board of Correction, which investigates every death in Department of Correction custody, counts 15 deaths in 2025; the Vera Institute’s tracker records the same 15 by name and facility, five deaths in 2024, and nine further deaths in custody in 2026 as of its current page, the most recent on November 10, 2026. The deaths in the Board’s reports are deaths by medical event, by suicide, by infection and allergic reaction and seizure—deaths inside a system of clinics, monitors, and investigations, several of them followed by findings of missed appointments, delayed response, and failed prevention. They are not deaths by unnoticed starvation in a yard; the machinery that makes Bartleby’s specific death improbable is the same machinery whose failures these reports document. New York City Board of Correction, the 2025 deaths report Vera Institute of Justice, the jail deaths tracker And the decree’s own plaintiff stands in the record: Robert Callahan, whose name is on the right to shelter, died sleeping rough on the Lower East Side in the autumn of 1980, while his case was being negotiated. The net was built over his body, and it has held, as law, for forty-five years. It did not hold him, because he did not take hold of it, and the law that built it does not compel the taking.

That is the autonomy threshold, and it is a matter of law, not of administrative failure. A competent adult in New York may refuse medical treatment, refuse shelter, refuse benefits, refuse food in his own rooms, and refuse every outreach worker’s offer, and the state’s instruments engage only at defined thresholds: conduct that is a crime, or a clinical finding, under the Mental Hygiene Law, that mental illness makes serious harm—now including the inability to meet essential needs—likely. Below those thresholds the quiet refuser is untouchable, by design. The 2025 amendment lowered the hospital threshold to reach the man who cannot feed himself; it did not and does not reach the man who, on examination, is found competent and simply will not. Bartleby, examined, might be found either. The story gives the examiners nothing to work with but the refusal itself, and the record cannot say which side of the line a silent, peaceable, self-supporting man who prefers not to would fall on. What the record says is that the line exists, that it is policed by courts, and that on the far side of it the 1853 ending remains available in 2026—not in a jail yard, but in the 329 non-sheltered deaths of the last counted year.

The Diagnosis

Beat by beat, the audit lands as follows. Impossible: the arrest for vagrancy as a status, void since 1972; and the death by starvation, over days, of a man in city custody, against a documented feeding protocol and a Court of Appeals ruling that the custodian may feed by tube rather than watch. Rerouted: the dismissal (instant, at will); the office residence (a license revoked by a personally communicated order, removal in days, the residence itself unlawful); the arrest (a conduct charge, a desk ticket or an arraignment inside about a day, release mandatory); the silence (an examination under Article 730, or a hospital evaluation under §§ 9.41 and 9.39, where 1853 had no question to ask). Still possible: the refusal, and everything the refusal can still carry a man to outside custody—the street, the subway, the winter night—in a city that counts its unsheltered dead by the hundred each year. The conceit that no beat of the story could happen today is therefore refused by the record in its strongest form and sustained in a narrower one: what has been abolished is not the fall but the fall’s invisibility. Every beat of the modern sequence generates an examiner, a finding, a docket, a by-name list, an annual report. In 1853 a man could pass from a Wall Street desk to a prison yard leaving almost no paper but a landlord’s note. In 2026 he would leave paper at every step—and the paper, the record shows, is not the same thing as a rescue. The difference between the two centuries is not that the ground has been removed. It is that the ground is now documented, litigated, and reached, when it is reached, past a series of doors each of which a man may decline to enter and most of which he must open himself.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
