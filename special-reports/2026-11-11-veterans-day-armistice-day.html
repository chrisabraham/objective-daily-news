Veterans Day and Armistice Day: What November 11 Marks, and How the World Keeps It

On November 11, 1918, an armistice signed before dawn in a railway carriage in the Forest of Compiègne stopped the fighting on the Western Front at 11:00 in the morning. This report follows what the day became: the two minutes of silence and the corn poppy—Papaver rhoeas, not the opium poppy; the American renaming that Kurt Vonnegut called the loss of a sacred day; the German day of mourning kept on a different date entirely; the Polish independence day that shares the date; and the several separate days on which the world marks the end of the Second World War—because there is no single one.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0720 ET, Wednesday, November 11, 2026.

The File in Brief

The eleventh hour is an arithmetic fact, not a poem. The armistice document signed at Compiègne is headed “signed at 5 o’clock A.M. (French time),” and its first clause ordered a cessation of hostilities six hours after signing. Five plus six is eleven. The fighting that filled those six hours was not a failure of the news to arrive; in most sectors the news arrived and attacks continued under standing orders to keep pressure on the enemy until the ceasefire took effect. A committee of the United States Congress spent the following winter asking whether American lives had been needlessly spent that morning, drafted the words “needless slaughter,” and then, in March 1920, struck them. Men were still being killed in the war’s final minutes: a Canadian private, George Lawrence Price, died at 10:58 by the record his comrades carved; an American private, Henry Gunther, at 10:59 by one National Archives account and 10:58 by another. The desk prints the ranges, because the sources do.

The day was invented quickly and argued about immediately. Within a year the silence had been improvised in Cape Town, proposed in print in London, carried through a South African statesman’s letter to the King’s private secretary, and proclaimed by George V for the whole Empire. The flower that came with it is a botanical point this report states plainly because it is widely gotten wrong: the Flanders poppy of remembrance is the corn poppy, Papaver rhoeas. The opium poppy is a different species, Papaver somniferum. The two are cousins, not the same plant, and the distinction is the whole of the symbol’s history—the corn poppy is the weed that colonized the churned chalk of the battlefields because its seeds can wait in disturbed soil for decades.

The date does not mean one thing. In the United States, November 11 began as Armistice Day, a day the 1938 statute said was “to be dedicated to the cause of world peace,” and became Veterans Day in 1954, honoring the living veterans of all wars. Kurt Vonnegut, born on the day in 1922 and a prisoner of war in Dresden in 1945, wrote the dissent that is still quoted: “Armistice Day was sacred. Veterans’ Day is not.” In France it is a public holiday for the dead of all France’s wars. In Poland it is Independence Day, marking a state reborn the same morning. In Germany it is nothing at all: the German national day of mourning, Volkstrauertag, falls this year on Sunday, November 15, and Germany’s first national day for veterans—created by the Bundestag in 2024, first observed June 15, 2025—is deliberately not a holiday and deliberately not in November. Russia left the war eight months before Compiègne and keeps its First World War dead on August 1; its great day is May 9. And the question this report was asked—what is the World War II day?—has no single answer, because the second war ended in pieces: May 8 in most of Europe, May 9 in Moscow, August 15 in Britain, September 2 in the United States, September 3 in China and, since 2020, in Russia too.

1. The Eleventh Hour

The German delegation crossed the lines on the night of November 7–8, 1918, and was given seventy-two hours. It was headed by Matthias Erzberger, a Centre Party politician and Secretary of State, with Count Alfred von Oberndorff of the Foreign Ministry, Major-General Detlof von Winterfeldt for the army and Captain Ernst Vanselow for the navy. The convoy crossed the front in five cars on the evening of November 7 and was driven for some ten hours through the devastated north of France to Foch’s private train, standing in a siding in the Forest of Compiègne at Rethondes. The carriage was dining car No. 2419D of the Compagnie Internationale des Wagons-Lits. Marshal Ferdinand Foch, the Allied commander-in-chief, appeared in person only twice in the three days—at the opening, and at the signing. Admiral Rosslyn Wemyss, the British First Sea Lord, was the second Allied signatory. The coldness was policy. Erzberger asked for an immediate ceasefire while the terms were considered; in Joseph Persico’s widely quoted reconstruction he pleaded, “For God’s sake, Monsieur le Maréchal, do not wait for those seventy-two hours. Stop the hostilities this very day.” Foch refused. The desk labels that sentence as reported dialogue from a secondary history; no stenographic transcript was located.

The terms were a dismantling. As printed in the signed text preserved in the United States State Department’s Foreign Relations volumes, the thirty-four clauses required: evacuation of Belgium, France, Luxembourg and Alsace-Lorraine within fifteen days; Allied occupation of the left bank of the Rhine with bridgeheads thirty kilometres deep at Mainz, Koblenz and Cologne; surrender of 5,000 artillery pieces, 25,000 machine guns, 3,000 trench mortars and 1,700 aircraft; surrender of every submarine and internment of the High Seas Fleet’s modern units; delivery of 5,000 locomotives, 150,000 railway wagons and 5,000 motor lorries; renunciation of the Treaties of Brest-Litovsk and Bucharest; repatriation of Allied prisoners without reciprocity; and continuation of the naval blockade of Germany. While the delegation waited, the state that had sent it ceased to exist: on November 9 the Kaiser’s abdication was announced and a republic proclaimed in Berlin; on November 10 Wilhelm II crossed into the Netherlands, and Friedrich Ebert’s government instructed Erzberger to sign. The men who had arrived as envoys of an empire signed for a republic. Erzberger was vilified for it at home and assassinated by right-wing gunmen in 1921.

The signing time is a range, and the report prints it as one. The document itself is headed “signed at 5 o’clock A.M. (French time),” and the British government communiqué Lloyd George read out said the same: “The armistice was signed at five o’clock this morning, and hostilities are to cease on all fronts at 11 a.m. to-day.” The Imperial War Museums give 05:00. The Australian War Memorial gives 05:10 in its main account and 05:15 in a second account by the historian Peter Hart; Persico also gives 05:10, “backed up officially to 5 a.m.” Compilatory sources run as late as 05:45. The honest statement is that the official time is 05:00, the best-supported time for pen on paper is about 05:10, and the full documented range is 05:00 to 05:45. Any account that prints one minute bare is claiming more than the record holds.

Three numbers are habitually confused, and they are different numbers. The seventy-two hours were Foch’s deadline to accept the terms. The six hours were Clause I’s interval between signing and effect: “Cessation of hostilities by land and in the air six hours after the signing of the armistice.” The thirty-six belongs to Clause XXXIV, which set the armistice’s duration at thirty-six days, renewable, and denounceable on forty-eight hours’ notice. There was no thirty-six-hour notice period. Foch’s implementing signal, preserved in the 2nd Australian Division’s copy and timed 06:35, read: “Hostilities will cease on the whole front on the 11th November French Time 11 Oclock. The Allied troops will not cross until further orders the line reached on that date at that time.” In Berlin, on Central European Time, the hour was noon. A story that Admiral Wemyss chose 11:00 for its poetry over a preferred 14:30 circulates in a community-contributed Imperial War Museums page; the desk found no better source for the motive and prints it as allegation only. The arithmetic needs no poetry: a 05:00 signature and a six-hour clause produce 11:00 on their own.

2. The Last Morning

The fighting continued because it was ordered to continue. The Commonwealth War Graves Commission’s official account states the rationale plainly: “Despite signing the Armistice in the early hours of the day, Allied commanders were still keen to keep the pressure on Imperial Germany militarily. Should fighting resume for any reason, the Allies decided they should be holding the best ground.” Foch’s standing instruction of November 9 was to maintain pressure until the ceasefire took effect. General Pershing’s notification to his commanders after the signing announced the hour; it did not order a halt before it. A divisional order quoted by Persico ran: “Hostilities will cease on the whole front at 11 hours today, French time. Until that hour, the operations previously ordered will be pressed with vigor. At 11 hours our line will halt in place, and no man will move one step forward or backward.” The morning’s attacks were therefore not, in the main, breakdowns of communication. They were the war, run to the last minute of its timetable.

The named operations carry their own records. On the Meuse, Major-General Charles P. Summerall ordered the 5th Division and attached Marines to force river crossings on the night of November 10–11, over pontoon bridges under machine-gun and shell fire; Persico counts more than 1,100 casualties in the final hours, a figure the desk prints as his count, no official V Corps tally having been located. At Stenay, Major-General William M. Wright carried out a planned attack with the 89th Division, which suffered 365 casualties including 61 dead; Wright’s own stated reason, from his diary as quoted by Persico, was that the division “had been in the line a considerable period without proper bathing facilities, and since it was realized that if the enemy were permitted to stay in Stenay, our troops would be deprived of the probable bathing facilities there.” Detractors alleged he wanted Stenay because it had been Crown Prince Rupprecht’s headquarters. Wright was succeeded in command the next day; whether the relief was punishment is alleged, not documented, and the desk does not print it as fact. At Mons, General Sir Arthur Currie received the news at 06:00 and Canadian troops completed the capture of the city that morning, at a cost commonly cited as 280 casualties to the Canadian Corps across November 10–11. Currie denied ever ordering an assault: “No order by me, verbal or otherwise, ordered an assault on Mons and Mons was never assaulted.” In the French sector, the 163rd Division attacked across the Meuse at Vrigne-Meuse under orders already in motion; figures of 75, 91 and “hundreds” for its November 11 dead all circulate, and the desk could not reconcile them.

The famous casualty figure is one historian’s estimate, and must be cited as one. Joseph E. Persico, in Eleventh Month, Eleventh Day, Eleventh Hour (2004), reconstructed from unit records an estimate of 10,944 casualties of all kinds on all sides on the Western Front on November 11, 1918, of whom 2,738 were killed—most of them in the six hours between signing and ceasefire, and at a rate he noted exceeded the war’s daily average. The Associated Press rounds the same estimate to 10,900. No official all-armies total exists. For the Commonwealth alone, the War Graves Commission’s records show 863 Commonwealth soldiers died on November 11, 1918—a figure narrower in every dimension: deaths only, one side’s forces only, counted by date of death, and including men who died that day of wounds received earlier. The two numbers measure different things and cannot be placed on the same axis. The American figures disputed before Congress illustrate the same problem from inside one army: the Army’s clerks supplied 268 Americans killed in action and 2,769 seriously wounded between midnight and 11:00; Persico’s reconstruction from the committee’s own papers puts American dead at at least 320 and total American casualties above 3,500, noting that the official return excluded divisions fighting under British and French command and conflicted with unit reports—one division officially reporting zero killed on a morning when one of its brigade commanders reported 191 killed and wounded. What is not in dispute, in any national account, is the order of magnitude of the morning: artillery and infantry attacks at operational tempo until 11:00, and the dead in the thousands in total casualties.

The named last deaths are documented, and their stopwatch order is not. Private George Edwin Ellison, 5th (Royal Irish) Lancers, a forty-year-old former Leeds miner who had fought from Mons in 1914, was killed on patrol outside Mons at 09:30 by the best-supported account, ninety minutes before the ceasefire. He lies in St Symphorien Military Cemetery, where his grave faces that of Private John Parr, the first British soldier killed in action on the Western Front, on August 21, 1914—a coincidence of a cemetery the German army created for the Mons dead of 1914, not a designed pairing. Private George Lawrence Price of the 28th Battalion, Canadian Infantry, was shot by a German sniper at Ville-sur-Haine as he stepped back into a street his patrol had just searched, and died at 10:58, the time his comrades’ 1968 plaque records and the War Graves Commission prints. The desk notes the conflict rather than hiding it: his battalion’s war diary gives 10:50 and a machine gun. Private Henry Nicholas Gunther of the 313th Infantry, a Baltimore bank clerk and the son of German immigrants, demoted from sergeant after a censored letter home, charged a German machine-gun roadblock near Chaumont-devant-Damvillers whose gunners—who knew the armistice was minutes away—tried to wave him off; he fired, they returned a short burst, and he died instantly. Pershing’s Order of the Day on November 12 named him the last American killed in the war. The National Archives gives his death at 10:59 in its burial-card article and at 10:58, quoting a war diary, in its centenary article; on the second timing Gunther and Price are simultaneous. The desk prints Gunther as generally recognised as the last, and the superlative as a convention of the record, not a stopwatch certainty. The last French soldier killed, Augustin Trébuchon of the 415th Infantry Regiment, a shepherd and regimental runner, died at Vrigne-Meuse carrying a message; accounts put his death between 10:45 and 10:55. A figure of 09:15 attached to Trébuchon in some retellings was found in no source the desk consulted and is not printed. French graves of November 11 dead in the Vrigne-Meuse sector bear the date November 10; the graves are documented, and an official order to backdate—and the motive for one, shame or clerical practice—remains alleged in both directions. No last German death of the morning is reliably documented by name and time.

Congress asked, and then Congress withdrew the question. On November 5, 1919, Pershing told the House Committee on Military Affairs that “the mere discussion of an armistice would not be sufficient grounds for any judicious commander to relax his military activities.” Representative Alvan Fuller forwarded a constituent letter from a former artillery operations officer lamenting “the little crosses over the graves of the colored lads who died a useless death on that November morning” and asked for “a real frank, full answer to the question as to whether American lives were needlessly wasted.” Pershing replied that he had followed Foch’s November 9 orders and had no knowledge of the signing before 06:00; Persico calls that claim disingenuous, and the desk prints both characterisations attributed. A separate body, Subcommittee 3 of the House Select Committee on Expenditures in the War Department, chaired by Royal C. Johnson—himself a decorated veteran of Gunther’s own regiment—investigated the Armistice Day losses through the winter. By the end of January 1920 Johnson’s draft concluded that “needless slaughter” had occurred, and the full committee initially adopted it. Daniel Flood filed a minority report charging defamation of the commanders. On March 3, 1920, under pressure not to hold up the committee’s report, Johnson struck every imputation that American lives had been needlessly sacrificed. The verified outcome of the only official inquiry into the last morning is therefore: hearings held, a verdict drafted and adopted, the verdict withdrawn, and no commander sanctioned.

3. The War’s Other Endings

November 11 is a Western Front date because the war ended in pieces. Russia left first: the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, signed March 3, 1918, took the new Bolshevik state out of the war eight months before Compiègne, at the price of vast western territories. Bulgaria signed the Armistice of Salonica on September 29, 1918. The Ottoman Empire signed at Mudros on October 30. Austria-Hungary signed at Villa Giusti on November 3, effective at 15:00 on November 4—which is why Italy keeps November 4, not November 11. In East Africa, German forces under Lettow-Vorbeck, who had heard nothing, fought on until November 25. An armistice is a suspension of fighting, not a peace: the Compiègne instrument ran thirty-six days at a time and was renewed while the blockade continued. The peace with Germany, the Treaty of Versailles, was signed June 28, 1919, in the Hall of Mirrors. The United States, whose Senate refused Versailles, ended its own war separately—by the Knox–Porter joint resolution of July 2, 1921, and the Treaty of Berlin of August 25, 1921. The ratifications of that treaty were exchanged on November 11, 1921, the day the American Unknown Soldier was buried at Arlington. The Western Front’s date became the world’s date by usage, not by fact; Section 12 of this report takes up the countries for whom the date means something else entirely.

4. The Invention of Remembrance

The first anniversary was proclaimed, not legislated. On November 11, 1919, President Woodrow Wilson issued an “Address to Fellow-Countrymen”—not a numbered proclamation, and it created no holiday—recalling that a year earlier “our enemies laid down their arms” and that the achievement had been bought at a cost the country was still counting. The federal holiday came nineteen years later (Section 7). In London, George V’s message appeared in The Times of November 7, 1919—the Australian War Memorial dates the Commonwealth message November 6; both are printed here because both stand in the record—asking that at the eleventh hour there be, throughout the Empire, a complete suspension of ordinary business and a silence of two minutes. The London observance of 1919 was improvised around a monument that did not yet exist in permanent form.

The silence has three documented parents, and the Imperial War Museums credit none of them. The earliest documented practice is Cape Town’s. After news of the German spring offensive reached South Africa, the mayor, Sir Harry Hands—whose own son had been killed in April 1918—instituted a midday pause: a notice in the Cape Times of May 13, 1918 announced three minutes from May 14, one for the fallen, one for prayer for those still fighting, one for the living; within days it was cut to two minutes, and it ran daily, signalled by a noon gun and maroons, with trams stopping and men baring their heads. The transmission to London is documented: Sir Percy FitzPatrick, the South African author and statesman, wrote to Lord Stamfordham, the King’s private secretary, proposing that the Cape Town practice be taken up Empire-wide on the anniversary; the letter survives in the Royal Archives. The third claimant, the Australian journalist Edward Honey, is documented only as a publication: an article in the London Evening News of May 8, 1919 proposed a silence of five minutes. The Imperial War Museums’ account names no originator. The desk’s statement is the one the evidence supports: the practice is documented first in Cape Town, the proposal’s route to the King is documented in FitzPatrick’s letter, Honey proposed a longer silence in print independently, and the two minutes the world kept are Cape Town’s length, not Honey’s.

The Cenotaph was a temporary stage prop that the public refused to give up. Edwin Lutyens designed a wood-and-plaster cenotaph—the word means an empty tomb—for the Peace Day parade of July 19, 1919. Crowds left flowers and wreaths at its base in such volume that it was rebuilt in Portland stone on the same Whitehall site and unveiled by George V at 11:00 on November 11, 1920, as part of the funeral procession of the Unknown Warrior. The British Unknown Warrior himself had been chosen at Saint-Pol-sur-Ternoise—not Saint-Omer, as many accounts print—where four bodies exhumed from four battlefields were laid out and one was selected, blind, by a brigadier; he was buried in Westminster Abbey on November 11, 1920. A correction the desk makes because the error is common: the Unknown Warrior received no Victoria Cross. The Cross enters the story twice around him—a guard of honour of one hundred Victoria Cross holders stood over his grave, and he was awarded the American Medal of Honor, presented by General Pershing on October 17, 1921. The reciprocal award closed the circle on November 11, 1921, when Admiral Beatty presented the Victoria Cross to the American Unknown Soldier at his burial in Arlington.

France and America built the same idea on different days. The French Unknown Soldier was presented beneath the Arc de Triomphe on November 11, 1920, but buried there on January 28, 1921; he had been chosen the day before the presentation at Verdun by a young soldier, Auguste Thin, who laid a bouquet on one of eight coffins. The eternal flame at the tomb was first lit by the Minister of War, André Maginot, on November 11, 1923, and has been rekindled daily at 18:30 ever since, including through the Occupation. France’s own November 11, 1919 had been a single discreet ceremony in a chapel of the Invalides; the French public holiday dates from the law of October 24, 1922. At Arlington on November 11, 1921, the American Unknown was buried with full honours; Woodrow Wilson, broken in health, rode in the Washington procession—whether he reached the amphitheatre itself is disputed in the sources, and the desk leaves it open.

5. The Flower

First, the plant, because the day’s symbol is persistently misdescribed, including in the commissioning conversation for this report, as “the flower from the opium plant.” It is not. The Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, identifies the remembrance poppy as Papaver rhoeas, the corn poppy—also called the field poppy, Flanders poppy and red poppy—a common annual weed of cornfields and disturbed ground across Europe. The opium poppy is Papaver somniferum, a separate species, the plant of the opium trade and the source plant of morphine and codeine. The two are congeners within the genus Papaver; they are not the same species, and the red flower on a November lapel is the corn poppy. The desk states the correction carefully and stops where the botany stops: the corn poppy is not a drug crop and is not the opium plant, but it is a member of a chemically active genus, and this report makes no unqualified claim that the plant contains no alkaloids of any kind—a pharmacopoeial entry located in research asserts the presence of thebaine-type compounds, and the qualified statement is the safe one. What made rhoeas the flower of the battlefields is its seed. Kew documents that corn poppy seeds can lie dormant in soil for eighty years or more and germinate when the ground is disturbed; Veterans Affairs Canada adds the chemistry of the trenches—the chalk and lime of shell-torn, rubble-strewn ground suited the plant exactly. The poppies of 1915 were not planted. They were what the ploughed graveyard of the Western Front grew by itself.

The poem came first, and it was published anonymously. Lieutenant-Colonel John McCrae, a Canadian physician serving with the Canadian Field Artillery, wrote “In Flanders Fields” at Ypres in the days after the death of his friend Lieutenant Alexis Helmer, killed on May 2, 1915; the poem was composed, by the standard account, on or about May 3, among graves already marked with wooden crosses and already growing poppies. Punch published it, unsigned, on December 8, 1915: “In Flanders fields the poppies blow / Between the crosses, row on row.” The poem’s last verse is an instruction—“Take up our quarrel with the foe: / To you from failing hands we throw / The Torch; be yours to hold it high”—and the organisations that took the poppy up understood themselves as answering it. McCrae did not live to see the symbol: he died of pneumonia in January 1918.

Two women carried the flower from the poem to the lapel. Moina Michael, an American professor serving with the YMCA in New York, read the poem in November 1918 and answered it with a poem of her own, “We Shall Keep the Faith,” and a vow to wear a red poppy; she is the documented origin of the poppy as an emblem worn. Anna Guérin, a Frenchwoman who had seen the poem’s effect in America, is the documented origin of the poppy as an article sold for veterans: she organised the manufacture of artificial poppies in France—made, in the first instance, by war widows and orphans—and carried the Poppy Day idea to Britain, Canada, Australia and New Zealand in person in 1920–21. The veterans’ organisations adopted in sequence: the American Legion named the poppy its official flower on September 27, 1920, at its Cleveland convention; Canada’s Great War Veterans’ Association adopted it in July 1921—the Legion’s poppy history and Veterans Affairs Canada differ by a day, July 5 or July 6, and the desk prints the month—and held its first Poppy Day on November 11, 1921; and in London, Major George Howson and the Royal British Legion, founded that May, adopted Guérin’s proposal for the first Poppy Appeal of November 11, 1921, which raised £106,000. The desk prints that figure alone; modern equivalents offered by sources range so widely—£1.3 million to £30 million in the material consulted—that any one of them would be a false precision.

The making of the poppies is part of the symbol, and its history is specific. From the start, the British appeal’s poppies were to be made by disabled ex-servicemen. The Poppy Factory was founded on June 5, 1922—on the Old Kent Road in London, not in Richmond, whither it moved in 1926—employing disabled veterans to make the wreaths and poppies. The present-day division of labour, verified for this report, is that the Richmond factory makes the wreaths and ceremonial work, while the bulk of the lapel poppies are machine-made at Aylesford in Kent. In 2023 the Legion replaced the plastic poppy with a plastic-free, 100-per-cent paper poppy, launched on October 26, 2023. The money remains large: the Legion’s Annual Report records £41.9 million for the 2023 appeal, an announced £51.4 million for the 2024 appeal, and £56.6 million reported for the 2025 appeal; the desk prints the three figures with their years and measures attached, because the appeal’s totals are reported on different bases in different documents and an unattributed single figure would mislead. In Scotland the poppy is its own: four petals and no leaf, made at Lady Haig’s Poppy Factory in Edinburgh, under Poppyscotland, which merged into the Legion in 2011 while remaining a distinct Scottish charity. In New Zealand the first Poppy Day, in 1922, fell not in November at all but on Anzac Day, April 24, because the shipment of French-made poppies arrived late—a logistics accident that anticipated the southern pattern described in Section 9. In the United States the flower’s anchor is the other war-dead day: the American Legion’s Poppy Day and the Veterans of Foreign Wars’ Buddy Poppy—distributed nationwide from before Memorial Day 1922, its trademark registered in February 1924, made historically by disabled veterans in hospitals—belong to Memorial Day in May, though both organisations also distribute poppies on Veterans Day. No record of trademark litigation over the Buddy Poppy was found in this research, and none is claimed.

6. The Other Flowers and the Quarrels

France’s flower is blue, and older as an organised symbol than the British poppy appeal. The bleuet—the cornflower—was first made at Les Invalides in 1916, in workshops organised by Suzanne Lenhardt, the hospital’s head nurse and a war widow (official French sources divide on the spelling, Leenhardt or Lenhardt; the desk uses the form in the charity’s own history), and Charlotte Malleterre, where wounded soldiers made tissue cornflowers for sale for their own benefit. The official chronology of the Œuvre nationale du Bleuet de France runs: association in 1934, a nationwide collection in 1935, a second collection day on May 8 added in 1957, and absorption into the national veterans’ office, ONACVG, in 1991. The blue is officially explained three ways at once: the cornflowers that grew, like the poppies, in the battlefield mud; the blue of the tricolour; and the bleu horizon, the blue-grey uniform of the young conscripts, the bleus, who arrived at the front as the cornflowers flowered. Two flowers, one flora: the Allies’ symbols were both, in origin, the weeds of the same fields, sorted by nation.

Serbia’s flower is a resurrection plant, chosen by politicians in 2012. Serbia made Armistice Day a state holiday in 2012, and its emblem pairs Natalie’s ramonda, Ramonda nathaliae, with the green-and-black ribbon of the Albanian Commemorative Medal. The botany is genuinely remarkable and documented in the botanical literature: the ramonda is a resurrection plant, able to survive near-complete desiccation and revive when watered, and it was adopted as a figure for Serbia’s own recovery after 1918—a country that lost, on the attributed range the desk found, between 1.1 and 1.3 million people, the sources in conflict on the toll of the state that suffered proportionally among the worst of the war. But the symbolism’s youth is documented too: the Belgrade scholar Senka Kovač records that the emblem was constructed by politicians in 2012, not inherited from 1918. The popular claim that the plant loses 95 per cent of its water is compilatory only; the scholarship supports near-complete desiccation and recovery over days, not the hours of some retellings.

The white poppy’s history is a three-step, and each step has a date. White poppies were first sold in 1933 by the Co-operative Women’s Guild, whose members—many of them women who had lost men in 1914–18—wanted a symbol of remembrance joined to a pledge against war. The Peace Pledge Union became involved in distribution in 1934 and took over production in 1936, and has made them since. The PPU’s own statement of meaning is threefold and is quoted here from its materials: remembrance of all victims of war, including civilians and the dead of all nations; a commitment to the abolition of war; and a challenge to what the Union calls the glamorisation of military values in the red poppy’s presentation. The scale is asymmetric and the PPU’s own figures say so: 122,385 white poppies in 2018, a figure the Union still gives as its record, against some forty million red poppies—roughly one white for every three hundred and thirty red. A claimed 2024 white-poppy record of 130,000 rests on a single Substack source, is contradicted by the PPU’s own current text, and is not printed as fact.

The Royal British Legion’s position has reversed across ninety years, and the documents show it. In 1934 a Legion conference motion proposed that members be “discouraged by all possible means” from wearing the white poppy. In 2018 the Legion published that it supported the right to wear “any colour (including white) or no poppy,” adding that “NO ONE should be targeted” for the choice. Its current coloured-poppies position respects other colours while asking that the red poppy be worn alongside them, on the ground that the red poppy is the appeal’s fundraising instrument and the Legion’s own emblem. The other colours carry their own documented histories. The black poppy—BlackPoppyRose, founded in 2010 by Selena Carty—commemorates the contribution of Black, African and Caribbean servicemen and servicewomen, which its founders argue the standard narrative under-records; no sales or distribution figure for it exists in any source the desk found. The purple poppy, for animals in war, was created by Animal Aid in 2006 and withdrawn by Animal Aid itself in 2015, in its own words because animals are “victims, not heroes”—whereupon Murphy’s Army took the symbol up in 2016 in exactly the honour-and-service framing its creator had rejected, and maintains it still, in its tenth-anniversary year in 2026. The campaign’s framing figure of eight million horses and donkeys dead in the First World War is sourced nowhere primary in the material consulted and is printed here as the campaign’s figure, not as a count.

The phrase “poppy fascism” has a named author and a documented exchange. In 2006 the Channel 4 newsreader Jon Snow explained, in print, why he does not wear a poppy on air: he draws a line between his private observance—he attends remembrance events and wears a poppy off air—and what he called the creeping obligation to wear symbols on screen, writing that there is “rather a lot of poppy fascism” about. The Legion’s contemporaneous response was the opposite of the phrase’s implication: wearing is voluntary, and he was entitled to his opinion. In 2013 the ITV presenter Charlene White set out the same professional-impartiality reasoning in a published statement and was met with racist and sexist abuse, documented by The Guardian—the documented abuse, not the poppy, being what her employers then had to answer. The footballer James McClean, from Derry, has since 2014 declined to wear the poppy on his club shirt; his 2014 letter to his then chairman, Dave Whelan, is the fullest first-person statement in the file and is quoted rather than paraphrased: the poppy, he wrote, commemorates for many not only the two world wars but other conflicts; had it been solely for those who died in the world wars, he would wear it—but, being from Derry, he could not wear a symbol associated in his community’s memory with the British Army’s actions there, including Bloody Sunday. McClean has been fined by no authority for the refusal; he has been abused for it annually, and his clubs have issued the same explanation annually.

The football-shirt dispute was settled by rule, not by sentiment. FIFA’s prohibition on political, religious or personal symbols on kit collided with the home nations’ poppies in 2011, when a compromise—poppies on armbands, after intervention by the Prime Minister and Prince William—set the pattern. In December 2016 FIFA fined the four home associations for Armistice-period displays: England CHF 45,000, Scotland CHF 20,000, Wales CHF 20,000, Northern Ireland CHF 15,000. On September 26, 2017, the game’s lawmaker, IFAB, clarified the law to permit such symbols where the opposing team and the competition organiser agree—the armband route, not an unconditional shirt right. Ireland’s part in the row is widely misreported and the desk corrects it: the FAI’s CHF 5,000 fine was not for an Easter Lily. It was for a “1916–2016” Easter Rising centenary insignia worn against Switzerland on March 25, 2016, pursued retroactively after the comparison was raised in the poppy dispute.

Ireland is where the flower’s meaning splits along the state’s own line. The poppy is worn in the Republic by some—there are official wreaths, and a Taoiseach has laid a laurel wreath at Enniskillen since Enda Kenny in 2012—but it is not the island’s shared symbol, and in Northern Ireland the ceremony has become partially shared while the flower has not: unionist representatives lay poppy wreaths, nationalist representatives lay laurel wreaths, a distinction documented again in the coverage of this year’s Remembrance Sunday, at which the Sinn Féin Lord Mayor of Belfast, Tracy Kelly Donnelly, laid laurel as Michelle O’Neill had first done, as First Minister, in 2024. Enniskillen is the wound inside the subject, and the desk corrects the record it was briefed with: on November 8, 1987, a Provisional IRA bomb at the town’s remembrance service killed eleven people on the day, among them Marie Wilson, aged twenty. The twelfth victim was Ronnie Hill, who lapsed into a coma two days later and died on December 28, 2000, never having recovered. Marie’s father, Gordon Wilson, survived; injured, he gave the interview that made him the bombing’s witness—“I bear no ill will. I bear no grudge”—became a senator in the Republic and a campaigner for reconciliation, and died in 1995. He is not in the toll, and accounts that place him there erase both the twelfth victim and the point of Wilson’s life.

7. The American Divergence

The American day began as an armistice day, in the strict sense: a day about the ending of a war, and about peace as a programme. The 1926 concurrent resolution of Congress recorded that twenty-seven states had already observed the day and invited the President to proclaim November 11 yearly, with exercises, in its phrase, designed to perpetuate peace through good will and mutual understanding. The Act of May 13, 1938 (52 Stat. 351) made it a federal legal holiday in words the desk quotes in full because everything that follows is a departure from them: November 11 was made “a day to be dedicated to the cause of world peace and to be hereafter celebrated and known as Armistice Day.” By 1938, the Congressional Research Service records, every state in the forty-eight-state Union observed it—forty-four by statute and four by gubernatorial action. A figure of thirty-eight states, which circulates in summaries, is supported by no source this research found and appears in the unverified ledger at the end of this report.

The renaming came from a Kansas shoe-repair shop, by the official account, and from an Alabama city by a competing one. Alvin J. King of Emporia, Kansas, whose nephew—John Cooper, killed in the Second World War; some records say stepson, and the records also divide on whether Cooper fell in Belgium or at Habkirchen in Germany—had not come home, campaigned from 1953 to widen the day to all veterans. Emporia’s observance that year was called “All Veterans Day”: the Emporia Gazette recorded some ninety per cent of the town’s stores closed and the schools shut, with King as general chairman. Emporia’s congressman, Ed Rees, carried the bill to Washington. The competing claim belongs to Raymond Weeks of Birmingham, Alabama, who proposed a national veterans’ day to General Eisenhower in 1945 and organised Birmingham’s first veterans’ parade in 1947; in 1982 President Reagan presented Weeks with the Presidential Citizens Medal, and Birmingham styles its parade the nation’s oldest. The desk prints both claims because both are documented; the Emporia line is the one embedded in the legislative history, and the Birmingham line is the one embedded in Alabama’s.

The statute is Public Law 83-380, and it was approved on June 1, 1954—not May 26, a date propagated by copies of copies and corrected here from the statute itself. Proclamation 3071, of October 8, 1954, called the first Veterans Day for Thursday, November 11, 1954, and the desk quotes it because Section 8 turns on its language: “On that day let us solemnly remember the sacrifices of all those who fought so valiantly, on the seas, in the air, and on foreign shores, to preserve our heritage of freedom, and let us reconsecrate ourselves to the task of promoting an enduring peace so that their efforts shall not have been in vain.” The peace dedication of 1938 was not struck out in 1954; it was carried into the new day in the President’s own words, and Harvey V. Higley, the Veterans Administrator, chaired the first national committee. What changed was the object of the day: from an event—the armistice, and the peace it was to have inaugurated—to a class of persons, the living veterans of all American wars, the Second World War and Korea having multiplied that class beyond what a First World War day could hold. Memorial Day, in May, kept the dead; the VA’s own standing explainer exists because, in its words, many Americans confuse the two.

The day was moved once, and the move failed on the record. The Uniform Monday Holiday Act of 1968 placed Veterans Day on the fourth Monday of October, and the first Monday observance, October 25, 1971, was held, in the contemporary phrase the desk found in the congressional history, “with much confusion.” States refused in numbers: by 1975 forty-six states had stayed on November 11 or returned to it, and only Mississippi and South Dakota had ever followed the federal date throughout. President Ford signed Public Law 94-97 on September 20, 1975—not September 18, as often printed—restoring November 11 as the federal observance, effective 1978. The date is the rare case in the file of a symbolic question settled by administrative fact: the eleventh of November is the anniversary, the states said so by statute, and the federal government followed them back.

The present American day is a federal holiday and a commercial occasion, and the two facts coexist without a national ledger. November 11, 2026 is one of the eleven federal holidays on the Office of Personnel Management schedule; falling midweek, it carries no in-lieu shift this year. Whether it is a state holiday is each state’s own question—compilations conflict, definitions differ, and the desk prints no fifty-state total. The 73rd National Veterans Day Observance is scheduled for 11:00 this morning at the Memorial Amphitheater in Arlington National Cemetery, with one documented change for 2026: attendance at the wreath-laying is limited to official participants. The President’s Veterans Day proclamation for 2026 was signed on November 10; the desk records its existence and date and does not adopt its performance claims about the Department of Veterans Affairs, which are the administration’s to make and others’ to audit. Commercial practice—retail sales events and free meals for veterans, announced chain by chain—is real and is described here as practice, not totals: no audited national count of Veterans Day sales or meals exists in the sources consulted, the National Retail Federation publishing no Veterans Day survey comparable to its other holiday surveys. The greeting argument—whether “Happy Veterans Day” is a kindness or a category error—runs annually in veterans’ own writing, and the benefits-versus-rhetoric critique has an institutional form: the Iraq and Afghanistan Veterans of America built its name on rating Congress against veterans’ legislation rather than against its speeches. The desk reports the argument’s existence and its named carriers; it found no poll it could print on how veterans in general receive the greeting.

8. Vonnegut and the American Argument

The sharpest American sentence on the renaming was written by a man born on the day. Kurt Vonnegut Jr. was born on November 11, 1922, in Indianapolis. He served as an infantry scout in the 106th Infantry Division, was captured in the Battle of the Bulge in December 1944—the biographies divide between December 14 and December 19, and the desk prints no single day—and was held as a prisoner of war in Dresden, where he survived the firebombing of February 13–15, 1945 in an underground slaughterhouse meat locker, Schlachthof Fünf. The experience became Slaughterhouse-Five (1969). The Armistice Day passage is in the Preface to Breakfast of Champions (1973)—a preface, not a numbered chapter, signed with his pen name for the purpose, “Philboyd Studge”—and the desk quotes the printed book, because the version in common circulation, including the version in this report’s own commissioning brief, does not match it. The book’s words are these: “When I was a boy, and when Dwayne Hoover was a boy, all the people of all the nations which had fought in the First World War were silent during the eleventh minute of the eleventh hour of Armistice Day, which was the eleventh day of the eleventh month. It was during that minute in nineteen hundred and eighteen, that millions upon millions of human beings stopped butchering one another.” And then: “Armistice Day has become Veterans’ Day. Armistice Day was sacred. Veterans’ Day is not. So I will throw Veterans’ Day over my shoulder. Armistice Day I will keep. I don’t want to throw away any sacred things.” No second, independent Vonnegut statement of the point was verified: the only Palm Sunday connection in the sources is his later self-grading of Breakfast of Champions at a “C,” and a line often quoted as his—that the new day encourages going off to kill and be killed—is a Veterans for Peace reteller’s gloss of 2018, not Vonnegut’s text. The desk prints the retraction of the gloss in the same type size as the quotation, because the misquotation is itself part of the day’s modern history.

Vonnegut’s argument has an organisational heir, and its claim is checked against the 1954 texts above. Veterans For Peace has run a “Reclaim Armistice Day” campaign since a 2008 national resolution: bells rung eleven times at 11 a.m. on November 11, and the day observed under its original name. Its argument is that Congress in 1926 prescribed exercises to perpetuate peace, that the 1938 act dedicated the day to the cause of world peace, and that the 1954 renaming, in the organisation’s word, “flipped” the day from peace into militarism. The campaign is current: an Alaska chapter’s bell-ringing notice for today stands in the 2026 record. The desk prints the claim as the organisation’s position, beside the documentary complication the organisation’s account omits—Eisenhower’s Proclamation 3071 carried the peace rededication into Veterans Day expressly, and the 1954 act’s own framing kept the day’s dedication to world peace. What changed in 1954, on the texts, is not the dedication but the dedicatee: a day for peace became a day for veterans, with the peace language retained as a purpose clause. Whether that retention is a continuity or a courtesy is the symbolic dispute, and it is not this report’s to settle. The factual dispute is narrower and is settled here: the texts say what they say, and both sides of the argument quote them selectively.

9. Britain and the Commonwealth

Britain keeps the day twice, and the larger keeping is on the Sunday. Remembrance Sunday—the Sunday nearest November 11—fell this year on November 8, three days ago, and the national service at the Cenotaph in Whitehall was held: the two minutes’ silence at 11:00, then the Last Post, then the Rouse—in that order, the Whitehall order; accounts that print Last Post, silence, Reveille are describing local and some Commonwealth forms, not the national service. The King laid his wreath—Charles III first laid the Cenotaph wreath as monarch on November 13, 2022, not 2023 as sometimes printed—followed by the royal family, the government, the opposition and the Commonwealth, and the March Past of veterans, which official accounts round at ten thousand. Twelve veterans of the Second World War marched or were counted in this year’s coverage, against twenty in 2025, as reported—the desk prints the figures as reported, the arithmetic of the surviving generation being a number with a cause, a manner and a baseline: the youngest of them are in their late nineties, and the cohort halves on a short clock. November 11 itself is observed in Britain with a silence, revived as a national practice only in 1995 after the Legion’s campaign—the weekday silence had lapsed after the Second World War—and it is a public holiday nowhere in the United Kingdom. Wales marks the day within the Whitehall pattern and its own national service in Cardiff; Scotland’s poppy is distinct (Section 5) and its services its own; Northern Ireland is treated in Section 6, where the wreaths differ by flower.

Canada is the Commonwealth country for which November 11 is statutory in most of the map and total in the culture. Remembrance Day is a statutory holiday in British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island—which is statutory, a point briefs on this subject get wrong—and Newfoundland and Labrador, and in all three territories; Ontario and Quebec do not make it statutory, and Manitoba and Nova Scotia govern it under their own Remembrance Day Acts. The poppy campaign is the Legion’s, on the McCrae inheritance (Section 5). Canada’s Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, at the National War Memorial in Ottawa, dates from May 28, 2000, the body brought from Cabaret-Rouge cemetery near Vimy—eighty years after the idea, a measure of how the Commonwealth’s invention of 1920 went on being completed.

In Australia and New Zealand, November 11 is observed and April 25 is kept. Australia’s silence at the eleventh hour is one minute, not two—fixed in its modern form by the Governor-General’s proclamation of October 30, 1997. It was two minutes in 1919; when and why it was shortened between 1945 and 1997 is not established in the sources consulted, and the desk invents no cause. New Zealand buried its Unknown Warrior in Wellington on November 11, 2004. But in both countries the larger national day of war remembrance is Anzac Day, April 25, the anniversary of the Gallipoli landing—a public holiday in both, where Remembrance Day is not—because the dominions’ founding military memory is a landing of their own, not the armistice of a European war. The Commonwealth pattern is thus threefold: Britain keeps the Sunday and the weekday silence; Canada keeps the weekday as a holiday in most provinces; the antipodes keep the weekday briefly and another day entirely at full weight.

10. France and Belgium

In France, le 11 novembre is a public holiday, and in 2012 it became a larger one. The holiday dates from the law of October 24, 1922. The law of February 28, 2012 widened the day’s object from the armistice generation to all who have died for France—tous les morts pour la France—in all conflicts, a change parallel to the American widening of 1954 but in the opposite direction of regard: France widened the day among its dead, America among its living. The monuments are the day’s furniture in every town: well over 36,000 monuments aux morts were erected between 1919 and 1926, one in nearly every commune—the desk prints the count in that form, the rounder versions conflating two different enumerations. At the Arc de Triomphe the flame lit by Maginot in 1923 is rekindled at 18:30 daily, a ceremony that continued through the Occupation and continues this evening. At Compiègne the clearing at Rethondes holds a museum and the carriage’s story, which is the next paragraph’s subject because France’s enemies made it one.

The carriage was used twice, the second time as an instrument of revenge. Dining car 2419D, in which the 1918 armistice was signed, was displayed in Paris and then in a purpose-built hall at Rethondes. In June 1940, after France’s defeat, Hitler had the car brought back to the same clearing and the same spot, and the French delegation signed the second armistice in it on June 22, 1940; the car was then taken to Germany as a trophy. Its end is a two-version record, and the desk preserves both: the majority account has it burned or demolished by SS troops near Crawinkel in March or April 1945; a competing account has it destroyed in an air attack near Ohrdruf in April 1944. The carriage in the museum at Compiègne today is a sister car of the same series, No. 2439D—not 2430D, a transposition in circulation—presented as the replacement it is. In November 2018 President Macron and Chancellor Merkel stood in the clearing together and unveiled a plaque; the recommended form of words, on the record consulted, is that Merkel was the first German leader since the Second World War to stand at the site in that way. No separate joint declaration issued from Compiègne; the next joint French-German text of the season was the Treaty of Aachen, signed January 22, 2019.

The Pétain remark is the modern French fight over the day, and it is printable only with its sequence intact. On November 7, 2018, at Charleville-Mézières, during the centenary tour, President Macron said it was “entirely legitimate” to honour the marshals of the Great War and that “Marshal Pétain was a great soldier in World War One.” The backlash was immediate and named: the CRIF, the representative council of French Jewish institutions, for whom Pétain is the head of the Vichy regime that deported Jews; and Jean-Luc Mélenchon from the left. By the same evening the Élysée had walked the remark back to a narrower position—the honour attached to the five marshals buried at the Invalides collectively—and on November 8 Macron stated that it had “never been a question of celebrating him individually.” The desk prints the sequence whole, because printed in part it supports either of two opposite stories. The older French repair of the same kind is Craonne: on November 5, 1998, Prime Minister Lionel Jospin spoke at the village above the Chemin des Dames for the soldiers shot after the 1917 mutinies—554 death sentences passed, 49 executions carried out on the usual count, from 3,427 courts-martial—and reintegrated them into the national memory. That was a reintegration into memory, not a legal rehabilitation, and the distinction is the French state’s own. The live French dispute of 2026 is smaller and procedural: a ministerial circular of July 2, 2026 making a school remembrance ceremony obligatory, opposed by the SNES-FSU teachers’ union under the slogan Refusons l’embrigadement—“refuse the regimentation”—the pacifist argument of the French left, in its current form, being not against the dead but against the state’s choreography of children around them.

Belgium keeps the holiday and the nightly ceremony. November 11 is one of Belgium’s ten statutory public holidays, under the Royal Decree of April 18, 1974. At Ypres, the Last Post has been sounded under the Menin Gate at 20:00 every evening since 1928, by the Last Post Association’s buglers, through occupation (when it was maintained in exile and resumed on the day of liberation) and without interruption in the modern era; the association’s own texts divide on whether the first sounding was July 1 or July 2, 1928, and the desk prints the year with the conflict noted. Ypres is where McCrae wrote the poem and where Helmer died; the Menin Gate carries the names of more than fifty-four thousand Commonwealth soldiers of the Ypres salient with no known grave. Belgium’s observance is thus the least national in this report and the most local: a town that was destroyed keeping a nightly appointment at its own gate, for the dead of other people’s armies, on a holiday its own state also keeps.

11. Germany: The Losing Power

Germany does not mark November 11, and the reason is structural, not an oversight. The Federal Republic is the state that, within living memory of the day’s invention, started the Second World War and committed the Holocaust. A German national cult of military heroism built on the First World War’s ending would have had to run through 1918’s defeat, 1919’s revolution and the stab-in-the-back legend that poisoned the Weimar Republic—and, after 1945, would have had to honour, in the first instance, the Wehrmacht. What Germany built instead is the most fully documented alternative in this report: a day of mourning for the dead of all nations and for the victims of Germany’s own tyranny, held in November but not on the eleventh, and, since 2024, a separate, deliberately unheroic day for living veterans in June.

Volkstrauertag, the National Day of Mourning, began as a proposal of 1919 from the war-graves movement. The Volksbund Deutsche Kriegsgräberfürsorge—the German War Graves Commission, founded in 1919 to do for German graves what the Imperial War Graves Commission did for the Empire’s—proposed a national day of commemoration for the fallen in 1919, and the day was observed through the 1920s, customarily in early spring, as a day of mourning pointedly without military display. The Nazi regime seized the form and inverted the content: in 1934 the day was renamed Heldengedenktag, Heroes’ Memorial Day, and in 1939 it was moved to March 16, the anniversary of the reintroduction of conscription—that is, detached from mourning and attached to rearmament. After 1945 the Federal Republic re-created the day on the Volksbund’s original lines, moved it to its present position—the second Sunday before the first Advent—in the early 1950s, and made its content the victims of war and tyranny of all nations. The date is computable and the desk computes it: Advent Sunday falls this year on November 29, 2026; two Sundays before it, Volkstrauertag 2026 is Sunday, November 15, four days from now.

The modern day’s texts enact the perpetrator-state premise. The central ceremony is held in the Bundestag, organised by the Volksbund, and its centrepiece is the Totenehrung, the Federal President’s tribute to the dead, a tradition begun by Theodor Heuss in 1952. Before it, the President lays a wreath at the Neue Wache in Berlin, rededicated on Volkstrauertag, November 14, 1993, under Chancellor Kohl as the Federal Republic’s central memorial—dedicated not to soldiers but “to the Victims of War and Tyranny,” containing an enlarged copy of Käthe Kollwitz’s pietà, a mother holding her dead son, by an artist who lost her own son in October 1914. The Totenehrung’s text places inside one act of remembrance the German soldiers, the German civilian dead, the expellees—and the peoples Germany persecuted and murdered, named by nation, by imputed race, by minority status and by disability. Flags fly at half-mast. There is no march past. The architecture is the argument: in the German form, soldiers are remembered among the victims of war, not celebrated above them.

The Bundeswehr’s own birthday sits one day off the date, for an unrelated reason that the desk states because the coincidence invites error. The Bundeswehr was founded on November 12, 1955, when the first volunteers received their appointments. The documented reason for the date is the two-hundredth birthday of the Prussian reformer Gerhard von Scharnhorst, born November 12, 1755. No source located in this research connects the date to November 11, and the desk prints no connection: West Germany’s army was born ten years after the war, in a state that had abolished the tradition it was reforming, and its date looks to 1755, not to 1918.

The veterans question arrived in Germany only in 2024, and it arrived in June. On April 25, 2024, the Bundestag resolved—on a motion carried by the SPD, Greens and FDP with CDU/CSU support (Drucksache 20/11138)—that Germany would keep a national Veteranentag, a Veterans’ Day, on June 15 each year, honouring the living veterans of the Bundeswehr, above all those of the foreign deployments since the 1990s, in which more than ten million Germans have served and from which some have not returned. The form matters as much as the date: it is a parliamentary resolution, not a statute and not a public holiday. The desk notes the structural consistency its organisers argue themselves—the Bundeswehr is a Parlamentsarmee, a parliament’s army, which may not be sent on an armed mission abroad without a Bundestag mandate, and its recognition was therefore given by parliament, in parliament’s form. The first Veteranentag was observed on Sunday, June 15, 2025, with its central event at the Reichstag; the separate Tag der Bundeswehr, the armed forces’ open day, was June 28, 2025, and the two should not be conflated. The second Veteranentag fell on June 15, 2026, its central Berlin celebration held on June 21, 2026. Support and criticism divide as the day’s design predicts: the Bundeswehrverband and the governing-coalition sponsors present it as overdue civic recognition for a force long asked to serve without a public; critics in the peace movement and on the left object to any normalisation of military honour in German civic life, and some veterans’ voices have asked whether a resolution without the weight of a holiday is recognition or a courtesy. The desk prints the dispute as it stands. What no party proposed, at any point, was November 11: for Germany the eleventh of November is the anniversary of a defeat its own extremists turned into a founding lie, and the country that lost the war keeps its dead on the fifteenth this year and its veterans in June.

12. Russia, Poland and the Other Meanings of the Date

Russia is the standing refutation of the idea that November 11 is a world day. The Russian Empire’s war ended not at Compiègne but by revolution: the October Revolution of November 1917, the armistice of December 1917, and Brest-Litovsk on March 3, 1918, followed by civil war that killed more Russians than the world war had. The Soviet state then marginalised the 1914–18 war for seventy years—an imperialist war in its doctrine, its cemeteries neglected or destroyed. The claim that Stalin personally ordered the demolition of Moscow’s Fraternal Cemetery in 1932 is, on the scholarship consulted, an urban legend, and is labelled alleged. The revival is post-Soviet and dated: a federal law of December 2012 made August 1—the anniversary of Germany’s 1914 declaration of war on Russia—a day of remembrance for Russian soldiers killed in the First World War, and on August 1, 2014, President Putin unveiled the war’s first national monument on Poklonnaya Hill in Moscow and said, in the Kremlin’s transcript, that “this victory was stolen from our country.” But Russia’s great day of war memory is May 9, Victory Day over Nazi Germany (Section 13), and August 1 is a date in the calendar, not a day in the streets. The country that buried perhaps two million soldiers in the first war keeps them at a fraction of the weight it gives the second—because the second war, in Russian memory, is the one the state survived and won, and the first is the one that destroyed the state that fought it.

Poland’s November 11 is not about the war’s ending at all; it is about the state the ending made. Józef Piłsudski was released from German imprisonment at Magdeburg on November 8, 1918, arrived in Warsaw on November 10, and on November 11 the Regency Council transferred command of the Polish forces to him—the act Polish memory takes as the hinge of independence regained after 123 years of partition. The Institute of National Remembrance’s chronology is the desk’s source, and its sequence is the point: Poland’s date is fixed by Warsaw’s events, and its coincidence with Compiègne is real but secondary. The holiday was established in 1937, abolished under the communist state in 1945, and restored by the law of February 15, 1989, months before the regime that had abolished it fell. In Warsaw today the day carries both an official commemoration and a large nationalist march, and the argument over which of those owns the date is a live one in Polish politics—the same date a few hundred kilometres west being, in France, a day of mourning at communal monuments, and in Germany an ordinary Tuesday.

The remaining divergences complete the map. Italy keeps November 4, the day the Villa Giusti armistice took effect in 1918, as its day of national unity and of the armed forces. Serbia keeps November 11 itself as Armistice Day, a state holiday since 2012, with the ramonda of Section 6. Latvia’s November military day, Lāčplēsis Day on November 11, commemorates not 1918 at all but the 1919 victory over the Bermondt forces at Riga—a different war, the independence war, sharing the date. Czechia marks November 11 as a significant day, not a public holiday, in its veterans’ form; even the start of that observance is disputed in the sources between 2001 and 2004. The date, taken whole, is a Western Front armistice in France, Belgium and the Commonwealth; a veterans’ day in the United States and Czechia; an independence day in Poland; a 1919 victory in Latvia; an ordinary day in Germany and in Russia. The claim that November 11 is a universal day of remembrance does not survive the calendar it claims to describe.

13. The Second War’s Several Endings

There is no World War II day, and the reason is the same as November 11’s: the second war also ended in pieces, in two theatres, under two instruments, across three time reckonings. In Europe the surrender was signed twice. At Reims, in the early hours of May 7, 1945—02:41 Central European Time—General Jodl signed the unconditional surrender of all German forces, to take effect at 23:01 on May 8. The Soviet Union required a second signing, in Berlin, because the Reims instrument had been signed by a Soviet officer of insufficient rank and in a city the Red Army had not taken: at Karlshorst, late on May 8, Field Marshal Keitel signed again, in a ceremony that ran past midnight in Moscow. Hence May 8 in the West and May 9 in Russia—not a propaganda invention but a clock: when the Karlshorst instrument was complete, it was already May 9 east of the line, and Soviet and now Russian Victory Day has been May 9 ever since.

Germany’s own May 8 is the slowest-evolving date in this report. In the Federal Republic’s first decades May 8 was kept, when kept at all, as the anniversary of defeat and collapse. The reframing has a text and an author: on May 8, 1985, the fortieth anniversary, Federal President Richard von Weizsäcker told the Bundestag that May 8 was “a day of liberation,” which “liberated all of us from the inhumanity and tyranny” of the National Socialist system—a sentence the desk quotes in its verified fragments because the speech’s whole is long; the speech also refused, in its other passages, to let liberation language dissolve German responsibility, and both halves are the record. The desk also corrects a common claim: Berlin’s May 8 public holiday is not permanent. The Land of Berlin made May 8 a holiday twice, as one-offs—in 2020 for the seventy-fifth anniversary and in 2025 for the eightieth—and the standing calendar does not include it. In the east, the German Democratic Republic kept May 8 as the Day of Liberation as a matter of state doctrine, a history that complicates any claim that the liberation framing began in 1985.

France’s May 8 traces a full political arc in one date. It was made a public holiday in 1953; de Gaulle downgraded its observance in 1959; President Giscard d’Estaing abolished the official commemoration in 1975, in the name of Franco-German reconciliation and a forward-looking Europe; and President Mitterrand restored it by the law of October 2, 1981. France thus abolished and restored its victory day within a generation, on arguments—reconciliation versus memory—that are the European argument in miniature.

The Pacific ending is a scatter of dates because it was a scatter of events. The announcement came on August 14 in Washington and August 15 in Tokyo, after the imperial rescript; the instrument was signed aboard USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay on September 2, 1945, which is the official American V-J Day. Britain keeps August 15. China keeps September 3, the day after the signing, as its victory day. Russia moved its Pacific date twice by law: to September 2 in 2010, and then, by President Putin’s law of April 24, 2020, to September 3—the Chinese alignment, adopted in the year of the seventy-fifth anniversary, and renamed in 2023. In the United States one state keeps its own: Rhode Island’s August holiday, whose statute name is Victory Day and never legally was “V-J Day,” survived another set of rename-or-repeal bills in 2025, the bills failing as their predecessors had. The second war therefore has at least six national ending-days in current official use—May 8, May 9, August 15, September 2, September 3 and Rhode Island’s August Monday—and November 11’s singularity, such as it is, is precisely what the first war had and the second never acquired: one instrument, one hour, one front that the world’s memory agreed to treat as the whole.

14. The Centenary and the Present Argument

The centenary weekend of November 2018 is the best-documented modern test of what the day now carries, because it put every tension in this report on one stage. In Paris on November 11, 2018, some seventy heads of state and government stood at the Arc de Triomphe, and President Macron used the ceremony for the phrase that defined it: “Patriotism is the exact opposite of nationalism. Nationalism is a betrayal of patriotism.” At Compiègne the day before, Macron and Chancellor Merkel stood in the clearing together (Section 10). The American absence that defined the weekend’s argument was elsewhere: President Trump did not attend the ceremony at the Aisne-Marne American Cemetery at Belleau Wood on November 10. The record of that episode stands in three layers, and the desk prints all three because printing any one alone would be a position. First, the official explanation at the time: the White House attributed the cancellation to weather—rain that grounded the helicopter and made the motorcade impracticable on the schedule—and Chief of Staff John Kelly and Chairman of the Joint Chiefs Joseph Dunford attended in the President’s place. Second, the contemporaneous criticism, attributed: the British MP Nicholas Soames called the President “pathetic inadequate” and “not fit” for the occasion; the speechwriter David Frum and the former Obama adviser Ben Rhodes made the point in the form Rhodes fixed—“There is always a rain option. Always.” Third, the later dispute: in September 2020 The Atlantic reported, from anonymous sources, that the President had spoken contemptuously of the buried Marines; the President denied the account in terms, on the record, repeatedly. On October 2, 2023, John Kelly—by then the former chief of staff—confirmed on the record to CNN the substance of characterisations of the President’s attitude to the war dead; the Trump campaign dismissed the confirmations in 2023 as “debunked.” The desk’s statement is the layers themselves: the weather explanation is the official account of 2018; the contempt account is alleged by anonymous-sourced reporting, denied by its subject, and confirmed in part by a named principal on the record five years later. Readers are given the record, not a verdict.

Outside the centenary, the perception of the day divides along lines this report can name without caricature, because the carriers are organisations with published positions. The peace movement’s organised form is the Peace Pledge Union’s white poppy (Section 6) and, in the United States, Veterans For Peace’s reclamation campaign (Section 8); its argument is that a day founded in 1919 as a silence for the dead and, in the American statute of 1938, a dedication to the cause of world peace has been converted into a celebration of military service and, in the PPU’s formulation, of military values. The veterans’ establishment’s organised form is the Royal British Legion and the American Legion; their published positions are that the red poppy and the American day are explicitly non-political, non-glorifying instruments—of fundraising for the living in the British case, of honour to service in the American—and the Legion’s documented movement from discouraging the white poppy in 1934 to defending any colour or none in 2018 is the establishment’s own record of accommodation. The nationalist argument appears in Poland (Section 12), where the day’s ownership is contested between the state and the march; in France (Section 10), where the fight is over Pétain and over the state’s choreography of schoolchildren; and in Germany (Section 11), where the dispute runs the other way—whether a parliamentary veterans’ day in June is too much military honour or too little. The factual disputes in this report—times, dates, tolls, texts—have been settled or left open on evidence in the sections above. The symbolic disputes—whether the symbols honour the dead, serve the living, sanctify the state or recruit for the next war—are disputes about what the documented things mean, and this report’s practice has been to attribute each meaning to the named party that holds it and to print the strongest opposing document beside it. No other settlement is available to a newspaper.

15. November 11, 2026: The Day as It Stands

The desk closes the file with the day itself, kept to what could be verified on the morning it describes. Today is a federal holiday in the United States, with the 73rd National Veterans Day Observance scheduled at 11:00 at Arlington and the President’s proclamation signed yesterday; both are stated as scheduled, this report being written before the hour. In France and Belgium today is a statutory public holiday; the flame at the Arc de Triomphe will be rekindled at 18:30 as it is every day, and this evening’s Paris ceremony is carried in the wire record as of this morning—the Associated Press’s live item is timestamped today, November 11, 2026, at 04:54 Eastern time—and is reported here as scheduled and under way, not as completed. In Britain the weekday silence at 11:00 will be observed at workplaces and stations; the national service was Sunday’s, three days ago, and is reported in Section 9 as held, on timestamped post-event coverage. In Canada the day is statutory in six provinces and three territories and observed everywhere. In Poland today is Independence Day. In Germany today is an ordinary Wednesday: mourning comes on Sunday, November 15, and the veterans were honoured in June. In Russia today is an ordinary Wednesday also.

The flowers, today: the red corn poppy in Britain and across the Commonwealth, in its plastic-free paper form in the United Kingdom; the blue cornflower in France, for the November collection as for May’s; the ramonda in Serbia; white poppies in their documented minority in Britain; laurel, not poppy, from nationalist hands in Belfast. What remains contested: in Britain, a live dispute of this autumn between the Peace Pledge Union and the Royal British Legion over a proposed renaming at the National Memorial Arboretum, unresolved as this report is filed; in France, the obligatory school ceremony; in Poland, the ownership of the day; in the United States, the annual argument recorded in Section 7. What has faded: the weekday silence in Britain, dead for fifty years and revived within living memory; the name Armistice Day in America, surviving in a veterans’ peace campaign and in a novelist’s preface; the German army of 1918 itself, whose state abolished the day twice over. What has not faded is the arithmetic that began the file: a signature in the small hours, a clause of six hours, and a generation that agreed to stop, at the same minute, every year, and to call the stopping sacred—whether it kept the agreement in Vonnegut’s sense being the argument the eleventh of November now annually re-opens.

16. Claims That Could Not Be Verified

The following claims were examined for this report and could not be established to the desk’s standard. They are printed so that their absence from the sections above is a matter of record, not of oversight. (1) A time of 09:15 for the death of Augustin Trébuchon: found in no source consulted; the attested range is 10:45–10:55. (2) A named, timed last German death of November 11, 1918: no reliable documentation exists. (3) That Admiral Wemyss chose the 11:00 hour for its poetry: a single community-contributed source; allegation only. (4) The figures of 10,944 casualties and 2,738 killed on the last day are Joseph Persico’s reconstruction from unit records—the most careful available, and an estimate, not an official count; no official all-armies total exists. (5) A figure of thirty-eight American states observing Armistice Day by 1938: the documented figures are twenty-seven (1926) and forty-eight (1938). (6) That Veterans Day is a state holiday in all fifty states in 2026: compilations conflict and definitions differ; no total is printed. (7) Aggregate national figures for Veterans Day retail sales or free meals served: none exist in audited sources. (8) A 2024 white-poppy record of 130,000: a single Substack source, contradicted by the Peace Pledge Union’s own text, which gives 122,385 (2018) as its record. (9) Any sales or distribution figure for the black poppy: none exists in the sources found. (10) The full text of Harry Leslie Smith’s Guardian article of November 8, 2013—its existence, title and thesis are documented, and Smith’s announced refusal to wear the poppy thereafter is a matter of record, but the article’s text was not retrieved, and no line from it is quoted in this report. (11) The Royal British Legion’s alleged definitional shift of 2019, characterised by the Peace Pledge Union: the Legion’s 2019 text was not retrieved, and the characterisation is printed as the PPU’s. (12) The 2019 Ontario and Quebec school-board poppy cases referred to in commissioning material: not verified, and omitted. (13) The spelling of the bleuet founder’s name: official French sources divide between Lenhardt and Leenhardt. (14) Whether Woodrow Wilson reached the Arlington amphitheatre in 1921. 17. The Contradictions Ledger

The desk’s practice is to preserve conflicts between reputable sources rather than smooth them. The material ones in this file are these. Signing time: 05:00 in the document itself and the British communiqué; 05:10 in the Australian War Memorial’s main account and in Persico; 05:15 in a second AWM account; 05:45 in compilatory sources. Gunther’s death: 10:59 in the National Archives’ burial-card article; 10:58 in its centenary article quoting a war diary. Price’s death: 10:58, sniper, in the War Graves Commission and his comrades’ plaque; 10:50, machine gun, in his battalion’s war diary. Canada’s poppy adoption: July 5 or July 6, 1921, between veterans’ sources. Poppy Appeal totals differ by year and measure—£41.9 million (2023 appeal, Annual Report), £51.4 million announced for 2024, £56.6 million reported for 2025—and are printed only with their years attached. The Compiègne carriage’s destruction: burned or demolished by SS troops near Crawinkel in spring 1945, in the majority account; destroyed in an air attack near Ohrdruf in April 1944, in the competing one. The Menin Gate Last Post was first sounded July 1 or July 2, 1928, between the association’s own texts. Alvin King’s nephew is a nephew in most accounts and a stepson in some; John Cooper fell in Belgium or at Habkirchen, Germany, between records. George V’s silence message is dated November 6 by the Australian War Memorial and November 7, in The Times, in London. Berlin’s May 8 holiday was one-off in 2020 and 2025; accounts describing it as permanent are in error. The corn poppy is not the opium poppy—that point is not a contradiction but a settled correction, printed here because it is the error this report was commissioned in part to repair: Papaver rhoeas, the corn poppy, is the flower of Flanders and of the lapel; Papaver somniferum is another plant, and no remembrance poppy ever worn was picked from it.
