A Launch Briefing—The Vehicle, the Flight Record, Friday’s Plan, the Two Catches, the Starlink Payload, the Economics, the Moon and Mars, the Field, the License, and the Risks

Objective

Starship Flight 15: The First Attempt to Catch Both Stages of the World’s Largest Rocket — Technology Report

SpaceX is targeting Friday, November 13, 2026, for the fifteenth flight of Starship, the tallest, heaviest, and most powerful rocket ever flown, and the flight is a target and not a settled event: the Federal Aviation Administration license modification that would permit its central maneuver had not been issued as of Tuesday, November 10. The plan, as SpaceX published it on November 7, is to launch from Orbital Launch Pad 2 at Starbase, Texas, in a 75-minute window opening at 5:45 p.m. EST, catch the Super Heavy booster in the launch tower’s arms at 6 minutes 35 seconds after liftoff, deploy 26 Starlink V3 satellites, keep the ship in orbit for about 22 hours and roughly 14 revolutions, and then attempt the first catch of an orbital upper stage in history at the same tower, an attempt gated on health conditions the FAA has approved in advance and that the ship’s own commander, Elon Musk, prices at 50 to 60 percent. The ship does not launch into orbit: it ascends on a suborbital trajectory ending in a cleared patch of the Indian Ocean, enters orbit only if it is healthy at 26 minutes, and is offered three scheduled ocean disposals across the day before it commits to the Texas corridor. This briefing files the vehicle and the one size record it has not taken—its demonstrated payload to orbit is about 44 tons, against a planned figure of more than 100 that has not been flown; the fourteen-flight record that leads to Friday, including the first orbital flight on September 28, 2026, and the booster engine-relight failures of Flights 12 and 13 that are the program’s live technical problem; the catch mechanics and the reason the vehicle carries no landing legs; the payload and what 26 third-generation Starlinks mean; the economics, in which every figure is labeled with its claimant, from an estimated $100 million per test flight today to the company’s stated target of $100 to $200 per kilogram; the Moon and Mars architecture that stands behind the flight; the competitive field in November 2026, in which the list of organizations that have recovered an orbital upper stage is empty; the licensing record; and the risks, ranked by the record rather than by drama.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0959 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026.

The File in Brief

The flight is a target, and the paper that decides it was still unsigned on Tuesday morning. SpaceX published its Flight 15 mission page on November 7, 2026: the fifteenth flight of Starship is “preparing to launch as soon as Friday, November 13, pending regulatory approval,” from Orbital Launch Pad 2 at Starbase, Texas, in a 75-minute window opening at 4:45 p.m. Central time—5:45 p.m. EST, 22:45 UTC—with backup airspace coverage running November 14 through 19. The Federal Aviation Administration license modification the flight requires is not a routine one: it must authorize, for the first time, the return of the upper stage to the launch site for a tower catch, on a reentry path that crosses northern Mexico and southern Texas and overflies populated areas. Reporting from November 5 through November 10 records no issuance, and SpaceX’s own page still carried the word “pending” at collection time; the license for Flight 14 was issued two days before that flight, so a Thursday or Friday issuance would match the program’s pattern, and nothing in this briefing treats Friday as settled. What follows is the record of the machine, the fourteen flights behind it, the plan as published, and the risks as the record ranks them—filed on Tuesday, four days before the window, and standing unrevised whatever Friday brings.

The vehicle is the largest rocket ever flown by three of the four measures that matter, and the fourth measure is the one the headlines usually get wrong. Starship in its Block 3 form stands 124.4 meters—408 feet—taller than Saturn V’s 110.6; it lifts off at a mass of about 5,300 to 5,533 tons, against Saturn V’s roughly 2,970; and its liftoff thrust, printed as a range because two rating conventions produce two figures, runs from 80.8 to 90.6 meganewtons, more than twice Saturn V’s 34.5 by either reading. It is the tallest, heaviest, and most powerful rocket ever flown. Payload is the measure it has not taken: the figure of more than 100 tons to low Earth orbit is SpaceX’s design target for the fully reusable configuration, a planned figure that has not been demonstrated, while the largest mass the system has actually placed in orbit is about 44 tons of Starlink satellites on Flight 14—below the roughly 118 to 140 tons Saturn V delivered in expendable service. This briefing prints the 100-ton figure only as a target, and the 44-ton figure as the record, every time the subject arises.

Friday’s plan is two catches at one tower, twenty-two hours apart, around a payload deployment and the longest mission the program has attempted. The Super Heavy booster, after separation and a boostback burn, is to be caught by Pad 2’s tower arms at T+6 minutes 35 seconds—the first booster catch attempt since Flight 8 on March 6, 2025, the first of a Block 3 booster, and the first catch of any kind at Pad 2. The ship is to deploy 26 Starlink V3 satellites of the Group 31-2 batch between T+35 minutes and T+1 hour 18 minutes, coast through roughly 14 orbits over about 21 hours, deorbit on a single Raptor engine at T+21 hours 36 minutes, reenter over the Pacific, glide across northern Mexico and southern Texas, and be caught by the same tower’s arms at T+22 hours 32 minutes 34 seconds—on Saturday afternoon, Texas time, if Friday’s window opens on schedule. No upper stage of any orbital rocket has ever been caught, or recovered this way, by anyone. A successful double catch would be, in SpaceX’s own framing, “the first full recovery of an orbital launch system.”

The plan is built as a ladder of revocable commitments, and the ladder, not the catch, is its real architecture. The ship does not launch toward orbit. It ascends on a passively safe suborbital trajectory aimed at a cleared area of the Indian Ocean, and only a ship judged healthy performs the orbital insertion burn at T+26 minutes 10 seconds; a sick ship simply comes home the old way. Across the long coast, three scheduled checks—at about T+1 hour 28 minutes over a disposal option near Hawaii, at T+7 hours 16 minutes near Chile, at T+20 hours 42 minutes near Hawaii again—offer controlled ocean disposals before the ship commits. Three of the satellites it deploys carry cameras to photograph its heat shield in orbit, and that imagery feeds the final decision: SpaceX’s published language is that flight controllers “will only permit Starship to return to Starbase if the vehicle’s status meets our internally developed, and FAA-approved, conditions for a return to launch site and catch.” Only after the deorbit burn is the ship committed to the land corridor, and even in entry the profile retains a burn-up option over the ocean and a contingency water landing in the Gulf. Elon Musk’s stated estimate for the ship catch itself, repeated since September, is “at least 50 or 60 percent”—printed here as his estimate, because that is what it is.

The fourteen-flight record behind Friday is better on the ship’s side than on the booster’s, and its live problem has a name: engine relights. The program’s totals stand at 14 flights, nine successes and five failures on the record’s own accounting; three booster catches in four attempts, all at Pad 1 in 2024 and early 2025; one booster reflight; no ship catch attempted. Flight 14, on September 28, 2026, was the first orbital flight: the ship reached about 275 kilometers despite losing one of its three vacuum engines early, deployed all 26 of its Starlink V3 satellites, and then, on a conservative health judgment, cut a planned six-orbit coast to under two orbits and splashed down in the Pacific about three hours after launch. The last three ships have all completed reentry and controlled water landings. The boosters have run the other way: on Flight 12, in May, only 20 of 33 engines relit for boostback and the booster struck the Gulf at high speed; on Flight 13, in July, the landing burn lit 10 of 13 engines and lost five more before a hard impact. Every Block 3 flight so far has had at least one engine anomaly, and Friday asks a Block 3 booster to complete the landing sequence not into the forgiving Gulf but into the arms of the only tower currently flying the type.

The economics are a ladder of labeled claims, and this briefing prints the ladder rather than any rung of it as fact. A Starship flight today, on outside reporting’s estimate, costs roughly $100 million, nothing recovered, which at Flight 14’s 44 tons is over $2,000 per kilogram—worse than Falcon 9 on the same arithmetic. Analyst models of early operations, assuming booster reuse works and ship recovery is validated, produce $15 to $30 million per flight and roughly $100 to $200 per kilogram at full payload; a skeptical school holds at $500 to $1,000 per kilogram through mid-decade on refurbishment and cadence grounds; SpaceX’s stated long-term design target is $100 to $200 per kilogram; and the founder’s floated aspirations, about $10 million per flight and in the limit about $10 per kilogram, are printed in this file as aspirations, the 2019 version of which—about $2 million a flight—operations have not borne out. SpaceX publishes no Starship price and no rate card. The physics under the argument is not disputed: a recovered stack spends propellant and inspection where an expended one spends 39 Raptor engines, two airframes, and a heat shield—and the ship, whose recovery Friday tests for the first time, is where the recurring manufacturing cost concentrates.

Behind the flight stands everything else the machine is for, and all of it waits on the same two undemonstrated capabilities: routine full recovery and orbital refueling. Artemis, restructured in February 2026 after Artemis II’s crewed lunar flyby in April, now plans a crewed Earth-orbit test in mid-2027 and the first crewed landing since Apollo 17 on Artemis IV in 2028, on a lander—SpaceX’s or Blue Origin’s—whose readiness is to decide the assignment; Starship’s lander contract is $2.89 billion, and its architecture requires a propellant depot filled by tanker flights whose published count per landing runs from about 6 to about 22 with no official figure fixed, ahead of a ship-to-ship cryogenic transfer demonstration NASA charts for the first quarter of 2027 that no one has ever performed. Friday’s 22-hour coast is itself part of that preparation: the longest a Starship has stayed in orbit is about three hours, and the boil-off data from a full day aloft does not yet exist. The competitive field, meanwhile, is a study in partial arrival: four organizations have recovered an orbital-class booster—SpaceX, Blue Origin, and, in 2026, China’s CASC and LandSpace—two have reflown one, and the list of those that have recovered an orbital upper stage is empty, while the closest Western rival sits grounded with a wrecked pad and the second sits grounded on a booster nozzle failure, leaving Western heavy lift, this quarter, effectively one company’s.

1. The Vehicle: Tallest, Heaviest, Most Powerful—and the One Measure It Has Not Taken

Starship is a two-stage, fully reusable super heavy-lift launch system, built of stainless steel, burning subcooled liquid methane and liquid oxygen in Raptor engines. The first stage, Super Heavy, is 72.3 meters tall and 9 meters in diameter in Block 3 trim, with 33 Raptor 3 sea-level engines—20 fixed in an outer ring, 13 gimbaling in the center for steering—three enlarged grid fins for aerodynamic control during descent, and no landing legs. The second stage, called Starship or simply the ship, is both upper stage and spacecraft: about 52.1 meters tall on the same 9-meter diameter, six Raptor 3 engines of which three are vacuum-optimized with large bells, a payload bay sized for stacked flat-packed satellites or cargo, four aerodynamic flaps that steer a belly-first descent, and a windward heat shield of about 18,000 hexagonal silica ceramic tiles over an ablative backup layer, built to survive reentry heating of roughly 1,400 degrees Celsius and to be reflown with minimal maintenance. The stages separate by hot staging: the ship lights its engines while still attached and pushes itself away, at about T+2 minutes 21 seconds on Friday’s timeline. The vehicle page SpaceX maintains is clipped below.

Block 3, the version flying Friday, is the third and largest iteration, and its enabling change is the engine. The stack stands 124.4 meters—408.1 feet—on stretched propellant tanks in both stages; propellant capacity across the generations has run from about 4,600 tons in Block 1 to about 4,900 in Block 2 and more again in Block 3. The Raptor 3 produces about 280 tonnes-force of sea-level thrust per engine against the Raptor 2’s 230, at a chamber pressure of about 350 bar, with its plumbing and sensors integrated into the engine body so it needs no separate heat shield of its own. Block 3 also redesigns the booster’s methane feed architecture, replaces its single quick disconnect with two separated connection points, enlarges the grid fins to three, and—on trade reporting, printed at that grade—doubles those fins as the tower’s catch points. The booster’s forward dome now takes the hot-staging blast directly, shielded by a non-structural steel layer and tank pressure, where earlier designs jettisoned a vented interstage.

The biggest-rocket question, which the commission put to the desk directly, has a precise answer, and it is measure by measure. Height: Starship Block 3 at 124.4 meters is the tallest rocket ever flown, ahead of Saturn V at 110.6 meters and NASA’s Space Launch System at about 98. Liftoff mass: about 5,300 to 5,533 tons, against Saturn V’s roughly 2,970—the heaviest object ever flown to space, a distinction a Starship ship already held on Flight 2 in November 2023. Thrust: the Block 3 stack prints two ways, 80.8 meganewtons in block-specific references and about 90.6 computed from 33 Raptor 3s at their published per-engine rating; the gap is a rating-convention gap—installed average against per-engine maximum—not a dispute about the hardware, and either figure is more than twice Saturn V’s 34.5. Payload to orbit is the fourth measure and the exception: Saturn V delivered roughly 118 to 140 tons to low Earth orbit in expendable service, and Starship’s largest demonstrated delivery is about 44 tons. The sentence “Starship can lift roughly 100 tons, far more than any other operational rocket,” in circulation this week, is therefore filed in this briefing at the grade it earns: the 100-ton figure is the reusable configuration’s design target for a vehicle on its fifteenth test flight, not a demonstrated capability, and the honest sentence is that Starship is the largest and most powerful rocket ever flown by height, mass, and thrust, while its payload record, as distinct from its payload target, does not yet exceed Saturn V’s.

The propellant choice is a Mars decision taken on the ground in Texas. Methane and oxygen—methalox—can in principle be manufactured on Mars from water ice and atmospheric carbon dioxide by the Sabatier reaction, which the entire Mars architecture depends on, and methane burns cleaner than kerosene in reusable engines, reducing the coking that complicates reflight. Both reasons are SpaceX’s stated rationale, and both are printed here as rationale; the Sabatier plant that would make Mars propellant exists in no flown form, and the engines’ cleanliness across repeated flights is a record still being written, flight by flight, in section 2.

2. The Flight Record: Fourteen Flights, Five Failures, One Orbital Debut

The record grades each flight against its stated test objectives, and on that accounting the program stands at nine successes and five failures in fourteen flights. The desk prints the accounting with its rule attached, because the rule is generous to the program—a flight that loses a vehicle after meeting its objectives is not a failure on it—and because the flight-by-flight facts below let a reader apply a stricter grade. Three booster catches in four attempts, one booster reflight, zero ship catches attempted, one orbital flight: those are the totals Friday’s plan is written against.

Flights 1 to 4 built the profile the rest of the program stands on. Flight 1, on April 20, 2023, lost several engines and its thrust vector control and was destroyed by its flight termination system about four minutes in, having torn up a launch mount that had no water deluge system; the regulatory process that followed imposed 63 corrective actions. Flight 2, on November 18, 2023, fired all 33 engines for the full first-stage duration, performed the first hot-staging separation, and lost both stages—the booster in its boostback flip, the ship to a fire and automated destruction after it had passed the Kármán line as the heaviest object ever to reach space. Flight 3, on March 14, 2024, reached its target trajectory, ran an internal propellant-transfer demonstration and a dispenser-door test, and lost both stages on return. Flight 4, on June 6, 2024, was the first fully successful profile of the early campaign: the booster splashed down under control in the Gulf on a “virtual tower,” rehearsing the catch, and the ship survived reentry with severe forward-flap burn-through and splashed down in the Indian Ocean within its target region.

Flight 5 made the catch, and Flights 6 to 8 completed the booster half of the recovery record that has stood, untested, for twenty months. On October 13, 2024, Booster 12 flew back to Pad 1 and was caught in midair by the tower’s chopstick arms on SpaceX’s first attempt, while its ship made a controlled reentry and a precise Indian Ocean splashdown. Flight 6, on November 19, 2024, aborted its catch on automated health checks of tower hardware damaged at liftoff—the precedent for every gate in Friday’s plan—diverted to a soft Gulf splashdown, and flew the program’s first in-space Raptor relight. Flight 7, on January 16, 2025, caught Booster 14 cleanly and lost Ship 33 to a propellant leak, its debris raining over the Caribbean. Flight 8, on March 6, 2025, caught Booster 15—the third and, as of this briefing, most recent catch, achieved despite two boostback relight failures—and lost Ship 34 to engine shutdowns on ascent. No catch of any kind has been attempted in the twenty months since Flight 8.

Flights 9 to 11 are the program’s failure year and its recovery of form, in that order. Flight 9, on May 27, 2025, flew Booster 14 a second time—the first reflight of a Super Heavy, with 29 previously flown engines—and lost it at the start of its landing burn; the ship’s payload door failed, a fuel leak cost it attitude control, and it broke up over the Indian Ocean, the Block 2 ship’s third consecutive failure, after which the program paused for three months. Flight 10, on August 26, 2025, deployed eight Starlink simulators, relit a Raptor in space, and splashed the ship down within three meters of its Indian Ocean target despite substantial engine-section damage, while its booster splashed in the Gulf after simulating an engine-out landing. Flight 11, on October 13, 2025, the last Block 2 flight and the last from Pad 1 before its retrofit, brought both stages to controlled splashdowns and its ship through reentry largely undamaged despite tiles deliberately removed—Block 2 ending with its objectives substantially recovered.

The three Block 3 flights of 2026 state Friday’s problem and Friday’s promise in the same hand. Flight 12, on May 22, 2026, the Block 3 debut and Pad 2’s first launch, lost Booster 19 when only 20 of its 33 engines relit for boostback and only one relit in the landing sequence; its ship lost a vacuum Raptor, reached cutoff on engine-out capability, deployed 20 simulators and two modified functional Starlink V3 satellites, and completed a nominal reentry and landing flip. Flight 13, on July 24, 2026, watched Booster 20’s landing burn light 10 of 13 engines and lose five more before a hard Gulf impact, while Ship 40 made the first deployment of production Starlink V3 satellites—20, on a suborbital arc, burned up as planned after testing—then flew a banking maneuver mimicking a Starbase return, splashed down, tipped over, stayed afloat, and was returned to Starbase by boat in October, the first ship to come back to its launch site, albeit by barge. Flight 14, on September 28, 2026, was the orbital debut: Booster 21 to a controlled Gulf splashdown, Ship 41 to orbit at about 275 kilometers on two of its three vacuum engines, all 26 Starlink V3s of Group 31-1 deployed—about 44 tons, the first operational payload Starship has delivered to orbit—and then the coast cut short on a conservative health call and a Pacific splashdown about three hours after launch. The pattern the record shows is printed in the File in Brief and repeated here because Friday turns on it: the ships have completed their side of the profile three flights running, the boosters’ engine relights have failed in sequence, and the company’s demonstrated instinct, once in orbit, is to shorten the mission and protect the vehicle. 3. Friday’s Plan: A Ladder of Revocable Commitments

The mission, as SpaceX published it on November 7, flies Booster 22 and Ship 42, the fourth Block 3 pair, under the designation Starlink Group 31-2. The booster’s serial is carried at the grade of reporting—SpaceX’s page names Ship 42, announced October 28, and does not name the booster—while the ship’s is documented. Booster 22 static-fired at Pad 2 on October 25; Ship 42 static-fired at the Massey’s test site on October 26 to 27; Ship 42 had already been through catch rehearsals on September 11 to 12, when Pad 2’s arms lifted and held it in a simulated post-catch position and mated it against the ship quick-disconnect arm while suspended. As of Tuesday the booster stood rolled to Pad 2 and seated on the launch mount, with the ship still to be stacked, flight termination charges to be installed, and the satellites to be loaded. The flight director’s poll for propellant load comes at T−50 minutes on Friday; the webcast begins about 30 minutes before liftoff. SpaceX’s Flight 15 mission page, the source of the timeline that follows, is clipped below.

The ascent and the first catch occupy the flight’s first nine minutes. Liftoff at the window’s opening or after; maximum dynamic pressure at T+58 seconds; booster main engine cutoff at T+2 minutes 19 seconds; hot staging at T+2:21; the boostback burn from T+2:26 to T+3:02; the booster’s landing burn from T+6:10; and the tower catch at T+6:35, the landing burn’s shutdown and the arms’ closure in the same second on the published timeline. The ship’s engines cut off at T+8:09, at which point the ship is on its passively safe suborbital trajectory toward the Indian Ocean and the mission’s first real decision arrives: the orbital insertion burn, T+26:10 to T+26:30, flown only if the ship is healthy. A ship that fails that judgment is not lost; it is simply on the trajectory it was launched on, and it comes down in cleared water as ships have done eight times.

The long middle of the mission is a working coast, not a wait. Between T+35 minutes 1 second and T+1 hour 17 minutes 40 seconds, the ship deploys its 26 Starlink V3 satellites—three of them carrying the camera suites that will image its heat shield—and then settles into the roughly 21-hour coast at about 275 kilometers that SpaceX’s plan describes as roughly 14 orbits. The coast is the program’s first long-duration operating period: propellant boil-off and tank pressure management, battery and thermal cycling through about 14 day-night passes, avionics endurance, and the state of a vehicle that must, at the end of it, light a single engine precisely. Every prior Starship orbital endurance is measured against Flight 14’s three hours; the data the coast produces on boil-off is identified in the technical reporting as a primary objective in itself, because the propellant-transfer architecture of section 7 needs ships to loiter for days to weeks and no one has the numbers yet.

The return is gated three times before it is committed, and the gates are timed to geography. At T+1 hour 27 minutes 46 seconds, an early-return check over a disposal option near Hawaii; at T+7 hours 15 minutes 35 seconds, a mid-mission check over an option near Chile; at T+20 hours 41 minutes 55 seconds, a final check near Hawaii again. At any of them, a ship whose health, heat-shield imagery, propellant state, or destination weather argues against the catch can be deorbited into remote ocean instead. Past the final check, the catch decision itself is taken under SpaceX’s published conditions, quoted in the File in Brief: return proceeds only if the vehicle’s status meets the company’s internally developed, FAA-approved conditions, with controllers confirming that the systems critical for reentry maneuvers, the final landing burn, and the tower approach are healthy. The deorbit burn—a single Raptor, on SpaceX’s November 7 update—runs ten seconds, T+21:35:57 to T+21:36:07, and after it the ship is committed to the corridor.

The corridor is the first of its kind in the program: a reentry over land, ending at a tower the ship left the previous evening. Entry interface at T+22:10:46, over the Pacific; then a gliding descent across northern Mexico and southern Texas, steering around populated areas, passing north of South Padre Island, crossing the Gulf coast, and turning south to approach Pad 2 from over the water, with sonic booms expected to be audible across Cameron County. Transonic at T+22:29:34. The landing burn begins at T+22:32:16 with the flip to vertical on three engines, steps down to two at T+22:32:26 and to one at T+22:32:33, and ends at T+22:32:34 with the catch—an eighteen-second terminal maneuver, which for a window-open liftoff falls at about 3:17 p.m. Central time on Saturday, November 14. Even inside the corridor the profile retains exits: controllers can end the entry early, including tumbling the ship to ensure it burns up over the ocean, and a contingency water landing in the Gulf stands behind a late no-go on the tower.

Success on Friday comes in grades, and the briefing prints them in descending order because the program’s own history says the lower grades are the likelier news. Full success is both catches with the satellites deployed and the stack recovered intact at one pad. Below it: either catch achieved without the other—a first ship catch with the booster diverted or lost, or the first Block 3 booster catch with the ship disposed of at sea on a health call. Below that, conservative success: satellites deployed, both stages disposed of at sea on health calls, on a 22-hour profile that would still multiply the program’s longest orbital endurance by more than seven and return the boil-off record the transfer program needs. Failure is loss of the vehicle on ascent—the base rate is real, five of fourteen on the record’s accounting—or loss during entry over the corridor, the outcome the entire gate structure exists to keep from becoming a public-safety event. If the plan flies as published, it stacks five firsts in one mission: the first ship catch attempt, the first booster catch attempt since March 2025 and the first of a Block 3 booster, the first catch of any kind at Pad 2, the first attempt to recover both stages of an orbital system on one mission, and the longest Starship mission by a factor of about seven.

4. The Catch: Why the Tower, and Why There Are No Legs

Each Starbase pad pairs a launch mount with an integration tower carrying two large articulated arms—the chopsticks, on a tower the program calls Mechazilla. The arms ride a carriage up and down the tower, translate and rotate, and do three jobs with the same hardware: they lift and stack the ship onto the booster, stabilize the stack, and catch returning stages. Pad 2’s tower is a newer design than Pad 1’s, with shorter arms—two truss sections removed—electromechanical actuators in place of hydraulics, and no ship lifting pins. The booster catch, demonstrated three times, runs as follows: after hot staging the booster flips, fires a subset of its engines to aim itself back at the pad, coasts, steers by grid fin through the upper atmosphere, and lights its center engines for a landing burn that brings it to a near-hover beside the tower at essentially zero relative velocity—telemetry from the first catch showed under half a meter per second at capture—whereupon the arms close on the vehicle’s catch fittings and it ends hanging from the tower it launched from minutes earlier, ready to be lowered straight back onto the launch mount.

The ship catch runs the same play with a vehicle that arrives horizontally, belly-first, out of orbital reentry, and every element of it is unproven in flight. The ship descends in its skydiver attitude, steered by its four flaps; at the last moment it lights three center Raptors, flips to vertical, and flies itself into the space between the open arms, stepping its burn down from three engines to two to one as it settles across eighteen seconds. The arms close around two catch fittings positioned just below the ship’s forward flaps, and the ship ends suspended by points that have just survived reentry plasma. No ship has been caught; none has flown a terminal approach to a tower; the catch fittings have never carried a vehicle after a reentry. The ground rehearsal was real—Pad 2’s arms held Ship 42 itself in the simulated post-catch position in September—but a vehicle hanging at rest in its own arms in September is a rehearsal of the servicing, not of the arrival.

The absence of landing legs, which the commission asked after, is the design’s center and not an omission, on SpaceX’s stated reasoning in three parts. Mass: legs, hinges, actuators, locks, feet, and reinforced load paths are dead dry mass that must be accelerated on every flight and, on the ship, dragged through reentry behind or through a heat shield whose windward side is a continuous tile field and whose base is crowded with engines. Turnaround: a caught vehicle is already at the launch mount—lowered onto a booster, inspected in place, refueled through the tower’s quick disconnects, flown again without a sea voyage, a crane lift, or a transporter ride; the tower is at once the integration crane, the recovery system, and the launch mount, which is the operational substance of the airline analogy the company draws. Scaling: a tower can be built as strong as the economics require once, while legs must be built into every vehicle forever, in a system whose entire cost case rests on cadence. The reasoning is printed as the company’s rationale; the airline-style turnaround it promises remains undemonstrated—the fastest booster reflight to date took 101 days, Booster 14 between January 16 and May 27, 2025—and ships bound for the Moon or Mars will carry legs or an equivalent in any case, since no tower waits at the far end. The tower catch is the Earth-return solution, not the universal one.

The honest counterweight is also in the record: the catch concentrates risk in the most valuable structure on the site. A booster or ship that arrives sick does not crash on an empty pad or an ocean drone ship; it arrives at the tower. Flight 6’s catch was aborted precisely because liftoff had damaged tower hardware. A failed ship catch on Saturday could damage Pad 2’s arms or tower, and the consequences run past the weekend: Pad 2 is the only pad currently flying Block 3, the Florida activation is months behind it, and the cadence on which the economics of section 6 and the Artemis manifest of section 7 both depend runs through that tower. SpaceX accepts the trade in the open, and the gates of section 3 are the instrument by which it manages the acceptance.

5. The Payload: 26 Starlink V3 Satellites, and What They Add Up To

The Starlink V3 is SpaceX’s third-generation broadband satellite, designed from the start to fly only on Starship, and Friday’s load is its second orbital batch. Its design figures, from SpaceX’s May 2026 securities filing and technical disclosures: one terabit per second of downlink capacity per satellite, roughly ten times a V2 Mini’s 80 to 100 gigabits; 160 gigabits of uplink, roughly 22 to 24 times the earlier generation; beamforming arrays and six optical inter-satellite laser terminals reported in the technical summaries. Each satellite runs about 7 meters long folded, with solar arrays spanning about 60 meters tip to tip once deployed—roughly a Boeing 787’s wingspan—on argon Hall-effect thrusters, and is aimed at very low shells of about 330 to 370 kilometers, trading altitude for latency under 20 milliseconds and for natural orbital decay. Its mass SpaceX has not published: press estimates cluster at 1,900 to 2,000 kilograms, while Flight 14’s manifested total of about 44 tons across 26 satellites implies closer to 1,700 kilograms each before dispenser hardware, and this briefing prints the range, 1,700 to 2,000, rather than either end of it. At roughly three times a V2 Mini’s mass and in its folded dimensions, the satellite cannot be carried in useful numbers by Falcon 9; SpaceX’s stated full load is up to 60 per Starship flight.

Friday’s 26, designated Group 31-2, represent about 26 terabits per second of added network capacity at the design figure—which SpaceX equates to roughly ten Falcon 9 launches of V2 Mini satellites, the arithmetic being that a Falcon 9 Starlink mission adds about 2.3 terabits. Their deployed mass matches Flight 14’s about 44 tons, the largest payload mass Starship has orbited. Flight 13 flew the first 20 production V3s on a suborbital test arc in July; Flight 14 orbited the first 26; Friday’s would bring the orbiting total to 52, the beginning of a constellation for which SpaceX has asked the Federal Communications Commission to authorize up to 100,000 satellites, in an application filed July 6, 2026. Two qualifications belong to the product, and the briefing files them with the payload: V3 broadband service requires the commission’s authorization for the new constellation, which remains in process, and gigabit-class service requires upgraded user terminals—the satellites going up Friday are network capacity in orbit before they are a product a customer can buy. Inserted at about 275 kilometers, they will raise their own orbits over the following weeks.

Three of the 26 are instruments of the mission itself. They carry camera suites to image Ship 42’s heat shield in orbit—the state of about 18,000 tiles after ascent, and before the plasma of return—and the imagery they relay feeds the catch decision described in section 3. The practice descends directly from Flight 12, whose two modified functional V3 satellites photographed their ship and inspected its heat shield; whether Friday’s three are operational satellites fitted with cameras or additional modified units of the Flight 12 kind, the coverage reads consistently on the first and the dossier flags the ambiguity rather than resolving it. The production line behind the manifest deserves its line in the record: SpaceX’s filing states that its Redmond, Washington factory produced about 70 satellites a week from December 2025 to April 2026, about 3,640 a year at that rate—which is to say the factory builds a Starship load of V3s faster than Starship can currently fly them. The satellite line is waiting on the rocket, and specifically on the rocket’s cadence, which is waiting on recovery.

The dependency runs both ways, and it is the commercial shape of the entire program. Starlink V3 needs Starship, because nothing else can lift it economically; Starship’s business case needs Starlink V3, because it is the anchor payload that fills 100-ton-class flights while the external market develops—84 of SpaceX’s 113 launches in the first nine months of 2026 carried its own satellites, on the company’s third-quarter disclosures as compiled. The constellation and the rocket are one bet, not two, and Friday’s manifest—the owner’s own cargo, on the owner’s own rocket, photographed by the cargo on the way up—is that bet in its purest published form.

6. The Economics: The Ladder, Labeled by Its Claimants

SpaceX publishes no Starship price list and no rate card, and every number in this section is somebody’s target, model, or estimate, printed with its claimant attached. The physics underneath the argument is not in dispute. An expendable Starship throws away 39 Raptor engines, two stainless airframes, and a heat shield on every flight; a recovered one spends propellant—a few hundred thousand to under two million dollars’ worth of methane and oxygen at industrial prices, on analyst estimates—plus inspection and refurbishment. The entire argument is about how fast recovery becomes routine, and Friday’s ship catch is the first test of recovering the stage where the recurring manufacturing cost concentrates: the booster alone, on the Falcon 9 model that Starship’s Flights 5 to 9 extended, saves the cheaper, hardier stage and throws away the ship, its six engines, its heat shield, and its payload bay on every flight.

The bottom rung is the present: roughly $100 million per flight, on outside reporting’s estimate for individual test flights in the development program. At Flight 14’s 44 tons delivered and nothing recovered, that is over $2,000 per kilogram—worse on paper than the Falcon 9 it is meant to succeed, because a test flight is what it is. The next rung is modeled, not stated: analyst models of early operations, assuming booster reuse working and ship recovery validated, produce $15 to $30 million per flight, and at 100 to 150 tons per flight roughly $100 to $200 per kilogram. A skeptical school stands apart from the models at $500 to $1,000 per kilogram through mid-decade, arguing that refurbishment, heat-shield labor, and realistic cadence keep true costs well above the modeled figures. SpaceX’s own stated long-term design target, restated by Musk in May 2026 after Flight 12 and tied to a 100-plus-ton payload with full reusability, is $100 to $200 per kilogram—a company target, unachieved. Above it, the founder’s floated aspirations, about $10 million per flight and in the limit about $10 per kilogram, are filed as aspirations; his 2019 figure of about $2 million per flight is a dated projection that operations have not borne out, and no published engineering roadmap substantiates the $10-per-kilogram limit. The Boeing 747 framing now circulating with the flight—that expendable spaceflight throws away a new airliner on every trip—is SpaceX’s own analogy: directionally what the physics allows, and planned, not documented, at every step past the booster.

The comparison set keeps the ladder honest. Falcon 9 lists at about $67 to $74 million, delivering 17.4 tons in reusable mode, a nominal $2,700 to $3,800 per kilogram at full manifest—but Payload’s research found dedicated commercial Falcon 9 missions averaging only about 3,370 kilograms of actual customer payload, which makes the effective price for a typical customer closer to $20,000 per kilogram. That gap between nominal and effective price is the strongest structural argument for a very large vehicle: most customers cannot fill a medium rocket, and a Starship-class vehicle monetizes aggregation. Behind both stands the cautionary benchmark of the partly reusable era, the Space Shuttle at roughly $54,500 per kilogram in historical accounting, and the expendable era’s standard range of roughly $5,000 to $15,000.

Cadence is the multiplier under every figure, because reuse pays only at flight rate and amortization divides by flights flown. The indicators, as they stand on Tuesday: 14 Starship test flights in 43 months; an FAA authorization, since May 2025, for up to 25 Starship launches a year from Starbase; a pending federal environmental review, opened October 14, 2026, of a SpaceX proposal for up to 100 launches a year in a first phase and 456 a year in a second—the company’s ask, printed as an ask, the review being the opening of a multi-year process and an approval of nothing. Production is being built for that world: Booster 24 fully stacked, Booster 25 in stacking since about November 2, Ships 44 and 45 in work, a second pad flying at Starbase, Kennedy’s LC-39A in activation. Whether demand, licenses, and reliability converge on anything like those rates is the open question on which every number in this section hangs, and Friday is its next datum.

7. The Moon, Mars, and the Refueling Problem

The program Friday’s rocket serves is not the program of 2024, and the restructure is the context the flight lands in. Artemis II flew in April 2026: four astronauts around the Moon, farther from Earth than any humans before them, home safely off San Diego. On February 27, 2026, Administrator Jared Isaacman reformulated what follows: Artemis III, once the first crewed landing, became an Earth-orbit test mission, and the first landing moved to Artemis IV. Artemis III, targeted for mid-2027, is a roughly 14-day low-orbit mission in which Orion rendezvous and docks with test articles of both commercial landers—SpaceX’s Starship HLS test article and Blue Origin’s Blue Moon Mark 2—with Isaacman telling the workforce in September that the program stood “about 90 days broken” against that schedule. Artemis IV, in 2028, is the first crewed lunar landing since Apollo 17 in 1972; NASA has not pre-assigned which lander flies it, readiness is to decide, and both providers must first fly uncrewed demonstrations of their crewed landers—SpaceX targeting the end of 2027, Blue Origin early 2028. NASA’s Artemis pages are clipped below.

Starship’s part of that program is a $2.89 billion fixed-price contract, awarded in April 2021, and an architecture whose central maneuver has never been performed by anyone. A propellant-depot Starship waits in low orbit; a series of tanker Starships fills it; the lander refuels from the depot and then, under the 2026 revision—in which the lander climbs to meet Orion in a higher Earth orbit and pushes the joined stack toward the Moon, reducing the propellant the lander must aggregate—docks with Orion, flies it to low lunar orbit, and lands the crew. The tanker count per landing is the softest number in the architecture: published estimates run from about 6 to about 22, NASA-adjacent descriptions say “more than 10,” at least one estimate for later missions reaches into the high tens, and no official fixed number exists; this briefing prints the range and treats any single figure, wherever it appears, as an error. The pacing demonstration stands in front of all of it: ship-to-ship cryogenic propellant transfer in orbit, never done by any program, which NASA’s milestone chart places in the first quarter of 2027. Friday’s flight reportedly begins testing elements of it—docking and hatch mechanism aspects, on a report derived from the company’s earnings-call transcript, filed at that grade—and its 22-hour coast is the first long data set on the boil-off the transfer architecture must budget for.

Mars depends on the same two capabilities and is honest about its distance in a single condition. Everything in the Mars architecture waits on routine full recovery, which funds the production line the Mars ships would be built on, and on orbital refueling, without which no Starship reaches Mars with useful payload. On the record stands a plan for an uncrewed demonstration of about five Starship upper stages in a transfer window, with the Italian Space Agency contracted in 2025 to fly experiments on the first Mars flight, and Musk’s May 2025 statement that SpaceX was still targeting a 2026 Mars mission; whether that window remains a live target could not be verified in this research, and the mission stands as to-be-announced in current launch tables, with the transfer demonstration now charted for early 2027 bounding what a 2026 departure could carry. And the crewed era’s distance from Friday is stated by the Polaris program’s own published expectation: Polaris III, the first crewed Starship flight, is not expected until Starship has flown at least 100 successful cargo flights. Friday is flight 15.

8. The Field: Everyone Else, November 2026

SpaceX’s baseline, first, because the field is measured against it: 121 of the world’s 248 orbital launch attempts in 2026, about 49 percent, were SpaceX’s, on Flight Atlas figures compiled in late October. Falcon 9 flew its hundredth launch of 2026 on August 25, with about 140 to 145 projected for the year and one booster, B1067, flown 37 times; Falcon Heavy flew its first 2026 mission on October 1; and 84 of the company’s 113 launches in the year’s first nine months carried its own Starlink satellites. Against that baseline the closest rival is Blue Origin, and its year is the field’s story in miniature: New Glenn reached orbit on debut in January 2025 and lost its booster; landed that booster on the ship Jacklyn in November 2025, the second organization ever to land an orbital-class booster; flew the same booster again and landed it again in April 2026, the first reflight of a landed booster by anyone but SpaceX—and then, on May 28, 2026, a New Glenn booster exploded in a static fire at Cape Canaveral’s LC-36, in reporting attributed to a BE-4 oxygen valve, and the company has not flown since, aiming to return by the end of 2026 against a reported pad rebuild of more than a year, the two printed together as the record leaves them. New Glenn is a 45-ton-to-orbit vehicle recovering its first stage only; its larger announced variant has no firm service date; and the company, which holds the second Artemis lander contract, chose in October 2026 to defer orbital refueling on its accelerated path in favor of three expendable transfer stages.

The rest of the Western field is grounded, slipped, or expendable. ULA’s Vulcan has been grounded since a second solid-booster nozzle failure on February 12, 2026, and the Space Force has moved GPS III and Space Development Agency launches to SpaceX; the practical consequence, printed plainly, is that in late 2026 Western heavy lift is effectively one company. Rocket Lab’s Neutron—13 tons, a reusable first stage, a target price of about $50 million—was expected in late 2026 and current tracking puts its first launch in 2027, the date not settled. Europe’s Ariane 6 is flying, targeting six to eight flights in 2026, expendable, with reusable demonstrators still in test, and is not a near-term factor in the recovery race.

China is closing the recovery gap on its own track, and its entries belong on the same scoreboard. The Long March 10B flew for the first time on July 10, 2026, from the Hainan commercial site, and its first stage was caught by a net array on the recovery ship Linghangzhe—China’s first orbital booster recovery—with a reflight of the recovered stage planned by the end of 2026. LandSpace’s Zhuque-3 has landed an orbital booster on legs at a desert site, on thinner Western documentation. China planned about 140 national launches for 2026, up 52 percent on its record, is developing heavy reusable vehicles aimed at Starship’s class, and targets a crewed lunar landing before 2030 against Artemis IV’s 2028. The complete scoreboard, as of Tuesday, is short enough to print whole: the organizations that have recovered an orbital-class booster are SpaceX, Blue Origin, CASC, and LandSpace; those that have reflown one are SpaceX and Blue Origin; those that have caught any stage from the air with a tower number one; and those that have recovered an orbital upper stage number zero. Friday attempts to change the last line and extend the third. Recovery has spread; reflight has barely spread; full-system recovery has not begun anywhere, and that sentence is the competitive meaning of Flight 15 if it works, and the measure of the opening that remains for everyone else if it does not.

9. The License: The Paper Gate in Front of the Flight

Starship flies from Starbase under FAA vehicle operator license VOL 23-129, and the program’s pattern to date has been flight-specific. The license order for Flight 14 covered that flight alone and was issued on September 26 for the September 28 flight. Flight 15’s modification must do something no previous order has done: authorize returning the ship to the launch site for a tower catch, on a profile whose reentry crosses northern Mexico and southern Texas and overflies populated areas—which is why the license, rather than the hardware, is the pacing item in the technical reporting. SpaceX’s mission page said “pending regulatory approval” when published on November 7; reporting from November 5, when the FAA’s Starship page did not yet list a Flight 15 license, through November 10 records no issuance; and the desk’s own check of the FAA’s Starship page, clipped below, found the modification still outstanding at collection time. Gwynne Shotwell, on the company’s October 20 earnings call, stated the expectation in her own words: “We have had significant constructive engagement with the FAA, and we expect the license for our Flight 15 to include return to launch site and tower catch for both stages.” The statement is documented; the license, as of Tuesday, is not. A distinction SpaceX itself draws, and this briefing preserves, separates the two facts a quick account is likeliest to merge: the conditions governing the ship’s return and catch are already FAA-approved as a safety framework, in the company’s November 7 safety update—and an approved condition set is not a launch license. Both are true at once.

The environmental record underneath the license has been built in dated layers, and its largest numbers describe an application, not an approval. The original Starbase programmatic environmental assessment of 2022 authorized a low annual cadence; a May 2025 authorization raised Starbase to up to 25 Starship launches a year. In July 2026 the FAA issued a draft tiered environmental assessment for the modifications now in process, noting that completing environmental review does not itself approve a modification, which must also satisfy the safety, risk, and financial-responsibility rules. On September 2, 2026, the agency signed a final tiered assessment and a finding of no significant impact covering Pacific reentry contingencies and a trajectory back to Boca Chica, with a September 4 Federal Register notice covering the associated contingency landing areas and airspace closures—the environmental chassis under Friday’s return corridor. On October 14, 2026, the FAA published its notice of intent to prepare a programmatic environmental impact statement for Starbase, reviewing SpaceX’s proposal for up to 100 launches a year in a first phase and up to 456 in a second, with scoping comments due November 30, 2026—the next regulatory event after the flight itself, and a multi-year process that has approved nothing.

The airspace mechanics record the date’s own history of movement, which is the final evidence for the word “target.” The CADENA coordination network’s planning slides carried the mission through the autumn at November 8, then November 12, then November 13; SpaceX’s launch vice president first gave the 13th on a conference stage on November 5. Airspace notices cover launch backups November 14 through 19, and an FAA notice addresses the contingency reentry and splashdown of the upper stage in the Indian Ocean across November 13 to 20—its primary timing windows falling on the night of the 13th, corresponding to the ship’s passively safe trajectory, the footprint it would follow if it never inserted into orbit. The Starbase return, a day later, is covered by the license modification and the reentry closures of the September record. What to watch between now and Friday is therefore short and specific: the modification itself, and whether its text covers the ship’s return explicitly; any further slip in the coordination channels; and weather at Starbase twice—Friday evening for the launch and Saturday afternoon for the return, a full day apart—plus conditions along a corridor spanning two oceans’ worth of longitude. Florida stands behind all of it: the FAA is expected to add Kennedy’s LC-39A to the license, and Flight 16—Booster 23 and Ship 43—is listed for no earlier than December 10, 2026 as the first Florida Starship launch, though the technical reporting judges early 2027 more likely on the hardware’s schedule; the disagreement is printed in the ledger below.

10. The Risks: Ranked by the Record, Not by Drama

First, the paper risk: the license does not arrive, or arrives without the ship’s return. It is the most probable way Friday disappoints, and the cheapest. Flight 14’s license came two days before flight; Flight 15’s modification is more novel; a slip into the November 14 to 19 backup window costs the program little, and date slippage is this program’s base rate—the coordination record has already moved the date twice this autumn. A modification covering the launch and the booster catch but not the ship’s return would fly a lesser mission inside the same window, and nothing in Tuesday’s record excludes it.

Second, the booster’s engine relights, the live technical risk with the freshest evidence against it. Flight 12 relit 20 of 33 for boostback and one in the landing sequence; Flight 13’s landing burn lit 10 of 13 and lost five more; Flight 14’s booster suffered several descent shutdowns though its splashdown was controlled. No Block 3 booster has yet completed a landing burn sequence cleanly. A booster that cannot hold its relights either diverts to the Gulf—the outcome the gates are built to choose—or arrives at Pad 2 in poor shape, which is the case the catch-gate automation exists to refuse. Third, ship engine-out on ascent: Flights 12 and 14 each lost a vacuum Raptor early and reached their targets anyway on engine-out capability; a second loss, or a sea-level loss at the wrong moment, ends the orbital attempt and returns the mission to its suborbital Indian Ocean profile—a failure of ambition rather than of safety, by design.

Fourth, the coast itself: twenty-two hours of operating time no Starship has ever accumulated. The program’s longest orbital endurance is about three hours. Friday multiplies it by about seven, across propellant boil-off and tank-pressure management, battery and thermal cycling through roughly 14 day-night passes, avionics and flap actuation after a day in vacuum, and a deorbit burn on a single Raptor at the end of it. Every hour of the coast is uncharted operating time for this vehicle—which is, of course, the point of flying it, and the reason the boil-off record is a primary objective rather than a by-product. Fifth, reentry over land: ships have completed full reentries into controlled water landings eight times, always with an ocean beneath the entire descent. Friday’s ship must survive the same heating—peak shield temperatures around 1,400 degrees across 18,000 tiles—and then fly a precision glide across northern Mexico and southern Texas, shedding energy, steering around communities, and arriving at one specific tower with its catch fittings structurally sound after plasma flight. Tile loss that was data on Flight 10 is a divert-or-destroy decision on Flight 15, and the three camera satellites exist because the program wants eyes on exactly that variable before it commits.

Sixth, the terminal maneuver, and seventh, what stands behind it. The catch itself is eighteen seconds: three engines stepping down to one, a 52-meter vehicle translating sideways into a pair of arms while falling. The booster version has worked three times and been refused once; the ship version has been rehearsed only with the vehicle hanging from the arms at rest, and its commander prices it at 50 to 60 percent—his figure, printed as his. If a catch goes wrong at the arms, the loss is not only the vehicle: Pad 2 is one of two Starbase pads and the only one flying Block 3, the Florida activation runs months behind it, and the program’s cadence runs through that tower—the risk with the longest shadow in the file, and the reason the abort-to-Gulf and burn-up options are built into the profile at every gate. Eighth and last, weather, twice: Friday evening at Starbase for the launch and Saturday afternoon at Starbase for the catch, plus the corridor between. A mission can launch inside its window and still lose its ending to weather that was no factor when it lifted off.

12. The Closing Analysis: Commitment, Rationed by Instruments

The desk’s analysis, on the whole record, is that Flight 15’s significance is structural before it is spectacular. A surfer deciding whether to paddle for a wave reads the water, commits while commitment is still possible, and accepts that past a certain point the decision makes itself. Friday’s mission is that decision written as engineering: a vehicle that leaves the pad uncommitted, on a trajectory that ends safely in the ocean; that is offered its commitment points one at a time, each priced in data—an engine burn at 26 minutes, three ocean exits across a day, a final ten-second burn taken only when the instruments, including cameras flown up for the purpose, say the water is right; and that, past the last gate, has no decision left to make. The industry stands in the same posture at larger scale. Booster recovery has been demonstrated on three continents; the fully reusable heavy-lift system—the wave that reprices launch costs, constellation economics, the Artemis manifest, and the Mars architecture at once—has been demonstrated nowhere, and the shore-side evidence says the principal believes the set is coming: boosters stacked two deep, ships in build beyond them, a second pad flying, a third site activating, a satellite factory outproducing its rocket, and a regulatory filing asking to study 456 launches a year. Companies do not build that much board for water they think is flat.

What the record adds, against any romance of commitment, is the counter-discipline in the same company’s own hand. SpaceX cut Flight 14’s six-orbit mission to two on a conservative health call and brought its ship home early; the gates, the exits, and the Hawaii and Chile options are how this program declines waves. Paddling is not catching—the commander prices the catch at 50 to 60 percent, both Block 3 boosters that attempted landing burns this year were lost, and the license was unsigned three days out—and a declined wave costs this program a window, not a season, because the ocean stays. Friday is not the day Starship becomes a spaceline. It is the day the program finds out whether the ship half of the machine can come home at all, with the Moon manifest, the Mars architecture, a 100,000-satellite filing, and the cost curve of access to orbit all standing on the answer—and if the answer is yes, the remarkable event stops being the catch and becomes, whenever it happens, the second flight of Ship 42: the first time an orbital spacecraft has ever flown again.
