Physical AI, Filed From the Record

Objective

The House Took Fifty Years to Grow Hands: X10 and the Relay Era, the Sensor Stack, the Luxury Tier, and the Humanoid Machines of 2026

Home automation began in 1975 as a signal riding the power line to a relay: the house could be switched and could report nothing back. Fifty years of development then gave the house senses—microphones, cameras, a laser that maps the floor—and a nervous system of mesh radios and voice assistants, without giving it any way to act on what it sensed. The luxury tier wired whole estates at installed costs running to $300,000, and every published scope in that tier is environmental: light, shade, climate, sound, gates. No platform at any price lists the ability to pick an object up. Physical AI is the name for the correction now arriving: generalist machines with arms and hands, priced from $4,900 to unpublished, trained in simulation and on human video. The 2026 ledger of that arrival is narrower than its announcements. The one consumer humanoid with an order page, 1X’s NEO, has a factory in full production and, as of November 13, 2026, zero verified deliveries to a paying customer. The strongest commercial record in the field belongs to a warehouse machine moving totes under a multi-year service contract. And across every serious estimate, the actuators account for half to three-fifths of what a humanoid costs to build. The record, era by era and machine by machine, follows.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 2315 ET, Friday, November 20, 2026.

The Record in Brief

The arc divides into three eras. In the first, X10, developed by Pico Electronics in 1975 and sold from 1978, sent commands over the house wiring as bursts at the zero crossings of the alternating current—one-way, unacknowledged, unauthenticated. A controller could order a lamp on; nothing in the protocol could confirm that the lamp was on, and a neighbor on the same distribution transformer could receive the signal. In the second era, the house acquired senses in sequence: the learning thermostat in 2011, the voice assistant in 2014, the doorbell camera, the LiDAR vacuum that mapped the home in 2016, and the Matter standard in 2022 to bind them. Through 2020 the flagship stack was eyes, ears, and a floor-bound puck—perception complete, manipulation absent. The era’s architecture risk has a date: April 15, 2022, when Insteon’s servers shut down without notice and hub-dependent homes went dark. The third era is the present one, and its machines are countable. 1X’s NEO, at $20,000 or $499 a month, is the only humanoid a consumer can order; its Hayward factory entered full-scale production on April 30, 2026, and the verified count of units delivered to a paying customer as of November 13, 2026 is zero, with early output routed to internal testing and a company statement pointing to first shipments in December. Tesla’s Optimus is in early production at Fremont, is not for sale at any price, and is targeted at outside deliveries around the end of 2027. Figure’s Figure 03 works in BMW plants; Boston Dynamics’ entire 2026 Atlas production is committed to Hyundai and Google DeepMind; Unitree’s machines are orderable today, the R1 from $4,900. Underneath the machines, two ledgers govern. The hardware ledger: actuators and drive components run at roughly 50 to 60 percent of a humanoid’s bill of materials on every sourced estimate—Schaeffler at about 50 percent, Morgan Stanley at 56, Hyundai Mobis at 60. The software ledger: the shipping reasoning stack is NVIDIA’s Isaac GR00T line, now at N1.7, trained on 20,854 hours of human egocentric video and in simulation, where the verified compressions are specific—42 years of a single robot’s experience in about 32 hours of compute in 2021, about 10 years in two hours in 2025, and 140 simulated years of self-play accumulated by Skild AI’s soccer policy in September 2026. There is no product named GR00T 2. The record follows.

1975: A Signal Riding the Power Line

X10 was developed in 1975 by Pico Electronics of Glenrothes, Scotland, and named for a plain reason: it was Pico’s tenth project. Products reached the market in 1978 through a partnership with BSR, opening with a 16-channel command console, a lamp module, and an appliance module. The signaling is documented to the half-cycle. The carrier is 120 kHz, sent as bursts at the zero crossings of the 50 or 60 Hz mains waveform; a 1 ms burst at a zero crossing is a logic 1, its absence a logic 0, and each bit is followed by its complement in the next half-cycle. Addressing is a 4-bit house code, letters A through P, plus a unit code from 1 to 16—256 possible addresses—and a frame carries a start code, the house code, and a key code holding either a unit number or a function: On, Off, Dim, Bright. A complete frame, transmitted twice with gaps, takes roughly a second.

The protocol’s blindness is structural, not incidental. Base X10 is one-way and fire-and-forget: no acknowledgment that a command arrived, no confirmation that a load switched. Two-way modules able to answer a Status Request were a later, minority addition. The failure modes are equally specific: powerline noise from motors, televisions, and switching power supplies swamps the 120 kHz signal; some devices act as signal suckers and absorb it; split-phase wiring strands a signal on one leg unless a phase coupler is fitted at the panel; and signals travel to a neighbor sharing the same distribution transformer, because the protocol carries no authentication and no encryption. The system reached the personal computer early—the CP-290 interface dates to the 1980s—and the late-1990s tinkering culture anchored on the FireCracker CM17A, a transmit-only serial-port radio dongle relayed onto the powerline through a transceiver, documented in hobbyist hands on machines as small as a 6502. The mainstream face of the era was not X10 at all: it was the Clapper, Joseph Enterprises’ sound-activated switch, famous through television advertising from the mid-to-late 1980s, while X10 remained a hobbyist system. The first era’s whole capability, stated plainly: a house that could be ordered, in the dark, and could not answer.

The Sensor Era: Eyes, Ears, and a Floor-Bound Puck

The second era added senses one at a time, and the dates form a single sequence. TiVo shipped its first DVR on March 31, 1999, and defined the connected living room of the 2000s; it discontinued its DVR hardware in October 2025. Sonos, founded in August 2002, introduced its wireless multiroom ZonePlayer line in January 2005, the template for app-era home audio—still without any manipulation capability. The mesh-radio layer arrived in the same decade: the Zigbee Alliance in 2002, the Z-Wave Alliance in 2005, and Philips Hue made Zigbee consumer-visible in 2012. The Nest Learning Thermostat was released on October 25, 2011, and reset consumer expectations for design and learning behavior; Google acquired Nest Labs in January 2014 for $3.2 billion. SmartThings launched on Kickstarter in 2012 and was acquired by Samsung in 2014. Home Assistant was started by Paulus Schoutsen on September 17, 2013, as a personal project to interface Philips Hue lights, published on GitHub that November under Apache 2.0, with local control and privacy as founding principles.

Amazon Echo was first released on November 6, 2014, to Prime members, with general release in June 2015; the first Google Home speaker followed on November 4, 2016. Ring’s original Video Doorbell dates to December 2012, and Amazon acquired Ring in 2018. LiDAR home mapping arrived with the Xiaomi Mi Robot Vacuum, built by Roborock and announced in August 2016, its laser distance sensor mapping the home as it cleaned; Roborock’s own-brand S5 followed in 2018. Matter, announced as Project Connected Home over IP in December 2019 by Amazon, Apple, Google, and the Zigbee Alliance, reached version 1.0 on October 4, 2022; Matter 1.2, in October 2023, added major appliances and robot vacuums. By 2020 the flagship consumer stack was complete and its limit was exact: Echo and Google Home for ears, Ring for eyes, a LiDAR puck for the floor—and nothing in the stack could move an object from one place to another. The era’s warning case is dated April 15, 2022, when Insteon’s servers shut down abruptly and hub-dependent homes were bricked without notice: the ambient house, rented from a cloud, could be repossessed by a server shutdown. The second era’s capability, stated as plainly as the first: a house that could see, hear, and report—and could not act.

The Luxury Tier: A Quarter of a Million Dollars, No Hands

Above the consumer tier, the same fifty years produced a dealer-locked luxury market. Crestron was founded by engineer George Feldstein in Rockleigh, New Jersey—the obituary record has him leaving his job and starting the business in 1969, while the company’s reference entries give a 1972 founding date; both dates stand in the record, attributed. By 1990 Crestron had over 100 employees and roughly $5 million in sales, serving luxury homes, boardrooms, and institutions. Its control-system rival AMX was founded in 1982 in Richardson, Texas. The 1990 state of the art divided exactly as the market still divides: a consumer tier of X10 Powerhouse modules, timers, and the CP-290, and a luxury tier of hardwired Crestron and early Lutron HomeWorks-class systems—wall keypads, dedicated touch panels, programmed per house.

The present tier’s terms are published by its own installers. All four flagship platforms—Crestron, Control4, Savant, and Lutron HomeWorks—require certified-dealer programming; homeowners cannot reprogram their own systems, and installers state this plainly. Installed costs, from integrators’ published ranges: a Hudson Valley integrator puts average projects at $30,000 to $200,000 and large estates at $50,000 to $250,000; an Atlanta integrator’s 2026 table gives Control4 at $10,000 to $50,000 and above, Savant at $15,000 to $75,000 and above, and Crestron Home at $40,000 to $150,000 and above; a Maryland integrator prices estate-scale work at $75,000 to $250,000 and above; a New York Lutron installer puts HomeWorks projects from around $50,000, commonly between $80,000 and $300,000. And the scopes are uniform. Every one of these installers’ published scopes is environmental and electrical—lighting and shades, climate, distributed audio and video, security and cameras, gates, pool and spa. No luxury platform lists any physical-manipulation capability. A $250,000 Crestron house can dim to 2700K and lower its shades on a geofence, and it cannot pick a wet towel off the floor. The last inch of the automated house—the distance between sensing a thing and moving it—is the distance the third era exists to close.

The Consumer Machine: NEO, Its Order Page, and Its Empty Delivery Ledger

1X’s NEO is the one humanoid a consumer can order, and its specification, per 1X’s own order page, is complete: 5 feet 6 inches tall, 66 pounds, lifts 154 pounds, carries 55, arm payload 18; 75 degrees of freedom in total, the hands at 22 each on the order page—a figure 1X’s July 9, 2026 announcement of a new hand supersedes at 25 per hand, 22 in fingers and palm plus 3 at the wrist, force-controlled with tactile and shear sensing; the order page appears stale, and both figures stand dated. The body is a soft shell of custom 3D lattice polymer with no pinch points, low-inertia tendon drives rated 95 percent backdrivable, hands rated IP68 and body IP44, four beamforming microphones, dual 8.85-megapixel 90 Hz stereo fisheye cameras, and compute on NVIDIA Jetson Thor at up to 2,070 FP4 TFLOPS. The order page disagrees with itself on the battery—842 watt-hours in the specification table, 0.75 kilowatt-hours in the same page’s FAQ—with runtime given as about four hours either way. NEO runs Redwood, 1X’s vision-language-action model, introduced June 10, 2025 and trained on teleoperated and autonomous episodes from 1X’s EVE and NEO robots; the company also lists a 1X World Model dated January 12, 2026.

The commercial record divides into production and delivery, and the two do not meet. Preorders opened October 28, 2025, at $20,000 outright or $499 a month, against a $200 deposit; the order banner calls the deposit fully refundable, and the pre-order terms limit a full refund to 14 days after the Order Invitation email—the terms, not the banner, are the record. 1X took more than 10,000 orders in the first days, by the company’s account. The Hayward factory began full-scale production on April 30, 2026, at a stated capacity of 10,000 robots a year, with early output prioritized to internal testing. As of November 13, 2026, the count of NEO units verifiably delivered to a paying customer is zero: The Information reported the absence of deliveries on August 27, 2026, Reuters carried it the same day, and 1X has issued no delay announcement and no first-delivery post. A company briefing carried by RobotToday has Børnich pointing to shipments beginning in December 2026; that line stands as announced, not as delivered. Production is not delivery, and the delivery ledger is the one that is empty.

What a delivered NEO would do in 2026 is also on the record, and it includes a person. Expert Mode, 1X’s teleoperation bridge, works as follows per the company’s launch briefing and FAQ: the owner books a session in the app and must approve it; a United States-based 1X employee pilots the robot wearing a VR headset; people in the home can be blurred in the operator’s feed; owners can set no-go zones the robot respects even under teleoperation; the ear-ring light shows when a session is active; and each session doubles as training data. In July 2026 reporting, the Wall Street Journal’s Joanna Stern found every household task in her session driven by a teleoperator, and 1X is explicit that teleoperation is part of the product at launch. None of the safeguards has been independently audited, for the documented reason: the robots are not yet in customers’ homes.

The Industrial Machines: BMW, Fremont, Hyundai, and the Tote Line

Figure’s record is the fullest deployment file in the field. Figure 02, the predecessor, completed an 11-month deployment at BMW’s Plant Spartanburg loading sheet metal: 10-hour shifts, more than 90,000 parts loaded, over 1,250 runtime hours, contributing to more than 30,000 BMW X3 vehicles, with over 99 percent successful placement per shift; the fleet was retired in November 2025. Figure 03, unveiled October 9, 2025—173 centimeters, about 61 kilograms, a 2.3 kilowatt-hour battery, palm cameras, fingertip sensors detecting 3 grams, wireless charging—is built at Figure’s BotQ plant at a stated 12,000 units a year of capacity; over 350 units had been delivered by April 2026, and since June 30, 2026 one has run parts sequencing at Spartanburg under Helix 02. A second agreement, with Catalyst Brands on May 26, 2026, covers distribution centers. Figure publishes no price, and consumer deliveries are zero. Helix 02, released in January 2026, is a single vision-language-action network controlling the whole body from pixels: its System 0 layer of 10 million parameters, trained on over 1,000 hours of human motion data and running at 1 kHz, replaced 109,504 lines of hand-written C++, under a System 1 visuomotor policy and a System 2 semantic layer; its showpiece was a four-minute autonomous dishwasher load and unload.

Tesla’s Optimus stands at the opposite entry from NEO: production announced, sale absent. On its third-quarter earnings call, October 21, 2026, Tesla said early production has begun on a high-volume line at Fremont, in the space where Model S and X were built until May 2026—some reporting dates the line’s start to late August 2026; the October call is the announcement of record. The line is designed for about a million robots a year; reaching 20,000 a week will take at least a year on a slow, bumpy ramp. The first units go to Tesla’s internal Optimus Academy; outside deliveries are expected around the end of 2027, industrial customers first. The $20,000 to $30,000 figure attached to Optimus is a long-term cost target, not a price: Tesla publishes no price and takes no reservations, and Musk denied a December 2026 reservation opening on October 30, 2026. The third-generation robot has never had a formal public unveil.

Boston Dynamics’ Atlas, in its product version, was unveiled at CES on January 5, 2026, and is in production at the company’s Boston headquarters: 1.9 meters, 90 kilograms, 56 degrees of freedom, 50 kilograms of instant lift and 30 sustained, a 2.3-meter reach, IP67 sealing, about four hours per battery with autonomous swap in about three minutes. The entire 2026 production run is committed to two customers—Hyundai’s Robotics Metaplant Application Center in Georgia, opened September 21, 2026, and Google DeepMind. The first customer outside the Hyundai group is planned for 2027, and Hyundai’s production-line deployment from 2028. Boston Dynamics publishes no price; a figure of about $130,000 to $140,000 was relayed to analysts at CES and reported through a Samsung Securities analyst in the Boston Globe, and it stands in the record as single-relay sourcing, printed with that caution attached. Apptronik’s Apollo has run pilots at Mercedes-Benz’s Digital Factory Campus in Berlin and lab evaluation with GXO; Apollo 2 was unveiled June 30, 2026, for task-data collection fleets, and the company puts production versions in 2027 and beyond, on $935 million raised at about a $5 billion valuation.

The strongest commercial record in the category belongs to Agility Robotics’ Digit, and it is a service record, not a sales record: a multi-year robots-as-a-service contract at a GXO-operated Spanx facility in Georgia—the first formal commercial humanoid deployment—moving totes from mobile robots to conveyors, with more than 100,000 totes moved and a claimed 65,000-plus fleet operational hours across nine customer sites, among them Amazon, Schaeffler, Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada, and Mercado Libre; one count puts about 250 commercial Digit units in service. Unitree holds the field’s price floor. The G1, launched in May 2024 at $16,000 maker-direct, held that price for roughly 17 months before stepping down once, to $13,500 in a store capture dated December 24, 2025; as of August 2026 it is orderable but backordered, with United States dealer prices around $17,990. The R1, introduced July 25, 2025 from $5,900, listed from $4,900 maker-direct by September 2026 and actually ships—the first walking humanoid under $5,000 at a real checkout. Clone Robotics’ Protoclone is the field’s outlier, and its honest record is narrower than its pitch: a musculoskeletal android of 206-bone polymer skeleton and more than 1,000 Myofiber artificial muscles, shown in 2025 videos actuating while suspended from a frame; the current prototype runs on compressed air, water hydraulics is the production plan, and no footage shows it bearing its own weight, standing, or walking.

Hands, and the Actuator Ledger

The hand is where the category’s claims meet a specification sheet. LinkerBot’s Linker Hand O30 is a five-fingered direct-drive dexterous hand with 20 fully active degrees of freedom—every joint independently motor-driven, no tendons or linkages—at 730 grams, a 30-kilogram rated payload, an integrated 6-by-11 tactile array across the fingertips, CAN-FD control at 500 Hz, and plus-or-minus 0.2 millimeter repeatability, per the manufacturer’s manual V1.0 of July 14, 2026. The maker’s further claim, that the O30 is the only full direct-drive dexterous hand in mass production, stands as the maker’s claim, not as an established fact; the specifications are the verified part. The supply chain behind the joints is documented at the name level. Harmonic Drive Systems of Japan is the incumbent strain-wave gear maker whose name became the generic term; China’s Leaderdrive sold 425,158 harmonic reducers in 2025, up 72 percent year on year, supplying AgiBot and UBTech—the desk prints the unit sales and drops the market-share percentages, because the sourced share figures conflict beyond reconciliation. Green Harmonic is the other Chinese reducer name, reported as a second source for Optimus. Schaeffler sells integrated rotary actuators, planetary roller screws, and bearings into humanoid programs; Hyundai Mobis supplies Atlas’s actuators. An Optimus-class machine is reported to carry about 14 to 16 planetary roller screws, about 14 harmonic reducers, roughly 20 coreless motors per hand, and over 40 servo motors.

The cost consequence is the actuator ledger, and every sourced estimate puts the same weight in the same place. Schaeffler puts actuators at about 50 percent of a typical humanoid’s bill of materials. Morgan Stanley’s teardown estimate for an Optimus-class machine is about 56 percent of a roughly $55,000 bill of materials. Hyundai Mobis puts the figure at about 60 percent of material cost. Bank of America’s projected 2030 split—linear 27 percent, rotary 24—sums to 51 percent. The range across the record is therefore 50 to 60 percent: the majority of what a humanoid costs to build is the machinery of moving. The electronics industry priced its entry against that ledger on March 16, 2026, when Infineon, NXP, and STMicroelectronics all announced partnerships with NVIDIA to sell humanoid hardware around the Jetson Thor platform, with Infineon anticipating about $500 of parts content per robot. Analyst shipment totals carry the same instruction to print the firm with the figure: Goldman Sachs estimated about 75,000 humanoids shipped worldwide in 2026; Omdia counted 13,318 shipped in 2025 and ranked AgiBot first at 5,168 units, a figure Unitree disputes with its own count of more than 5,500; a May 2026 industry memo put only about 7,000 to 8,000 humanoids as operational worldwide. The spread is the record.

The Software Stack: World Models, Vision-Language-Action Models, and Simulated Years

The stack beneath the machines consolidated in public on a single day. At CES on January 5, 2026, NVIDIA announced Cosmos Transfer 2.5 and Cosmos Predict 2.5, open world models for synthetic data generation and policy evaluation in simulation; Cosmos Reason 2, an open reasoning vision-language model; Isaac GR00T N1.6; Isaac Lab-Arena, an open-source evaluation framework; and the OSMO edge-to-cloud workflow framework. The current GR00T is Isaac GR00T N1.7: early access April 17, 2026, general availability by November 2026—a 3-billion-parameter open reasoning VLA, commercially licensed, whose System 2 backbone is Cosmos-Reason2-2B and whose pretraining, called EgoScale, used 20,854 hours of human egocentric video across more than 20 task categories. NVIDIA claims the first dexterity scaling law for the field: going from 1,000 to 20,000 hours of human video more than doubles average task completion. No product named GR00T 2 exists in NVIDIA’s newsroom, model cards, or repositories; the line runs N1, N1.6, N1.7. The System 1 and System 2 split is concrete in the shipping stacks, not diagrammatic: GR00T N1.7 pairs its reasoning backbone with a diffusion-transformer action head, and Figure’s Helix 02 pairs a semantic layer with the 1 kHz learned whole-body layer that replaced hand-written control code. OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra, released September 3, 2026, is a general frontier language model; no NVIDIA, Figure, or 1X source places it inside a robot control stack, and the reasoning backbones actually shipping in robots are the two named above.

Simulation is where the training years come from, and the compressions are documented at specific scales with dates. Newton, an open-source GPU-accelerated physics engine co-developed by NVIDIA, Google DeepMind, and Disney Research and managed by the Linux Foundation, went beta September 29, 2025 and reached 1.0 in March 2026, with native integration in Isaac Lab 3.0—itself in general availability on October 20, 2026. NVIDIA’s GTC 2026 figures claim 252-times acceleration for humanoid locomotion and 475-times for in-hand manipulation against MJX baselines; those are vendor numbers and are printed as such. The experience compressions: NVIDIA’s Isaac Gym work trained a dexterous hand policy in about 32 hours of compute equivalent to 42 years of a single robot’s experience, in 2021; NVIDIA’s 2025 locomotion demonstrations compressed about 10 years into two hours across thousands of parallel simulated robots; and Skild AI’s soccer policy accumulated 140 simulated years of self-play in Isaac Sim before transfer to a physical robot, in September 2026. The mechanism is uniform—thousands of parallel instances on one GPU—and the 140-year figure belongs to the Skild case alone. Egocentric human video is the second training source, now at production scale: GR00T N1.7’s 20,854 hours is the headline instance, and Helix 02’s System 0 was trained on over 1,000 hours of human motion data. The academic edge of the same method is HumanX, from HKUST and Shanghai AI Laboratory, which transferred 10 skills across five domains, basketball to reactive fighting, zero-shot to a physical Unitree G1. Two measures of NVIDIA’s position circulate with different denominators, both printed labeled: ABI Research, carried by Reuters on March 16, 2026, estimated NVIDIA’s platform is used in more than 80 percent of humanoid robots—a platform-presence estimate—while a single thinner report puts Jetson’s share of edge-AI-chip revenue at about 39 percent, flagged as directional by its carrier.

The Open Tabletop: What a Hobbyist Needs in November 2026

The bottom of the stack is open, cheap, and documented. The tabletop standard is the SO-101, a six-degree-of-freedom open-source arm from The Robot Studio and Hugging Face’s LeRobot line—the SO-100 of October 2024, the SO-101 of April 2025, Apache 2.0—built from six Feetech STS3215 servos. Parts run about $122 for one follower arm and about $230 for a leader-follower pair before printing the frame; assembled kits sell for roughly $249 to $316. GR00T N1.7’s documented hardware floors set the compute requirement: inference needs one GPU with 16 GB or more of memory—the README names the RTX 4090, L40, H100, Jetson AGX Thor, Jetson AGX Orin, and DGX Spark—and fine-tuning needs 40 GB or more, with H100 or L40 nodes recommended. A 16 GB consumer card therefore runs a trained policy locally, and a Jetson developer kit runs it at the edge on hardware like the SO-101. Jetson Thor has been generally available since August 2025: Blackwell-based, up to 2,070 FP4 TFLOPS in a 40-to-130-watt envelope, 128 GB of memory on the module, a $3,499 developer kit, and the T5000 production module reported at $2,999 in volume. The hobbyist’s arm now runs the same model family as the factory humanoid, on a card that fits in a desktop.

What the Record Shows

The fifty-year record closes on four instruments. A protocol: X10, which could command and could not confirm, and whose blindness was never repaired, only succeeded. A scope list: the luxury tier’s, environmental from top to bottom at prices to $300,000, the documentary proof that the automated house’s missing faculty was never sensing or switching but handling. An order page: exactly one for a consumer humanoid, behind which stands a factory in full production and a delivery ledger that reads zero—production and delivery are separate facts, and in November 2026 they have not yet met. And a bill of materials: half to three-fifths actuators on every sourced estimate, which is why the field’s center of gravity sits in gear factories in Japan and China as much as in model laboratories. The machines that work today work in factories and warehouses, under service contracts, at unpublished prices. The machine that can be ordered for a home arrives, when it arrives, with a person at the controls for the tasks its software has not mastered. The record shows the hands built, priced, and in production—and the delivery column still empty.
