The Huang Reinstatement, Filed From the Record

Objective

Jensen Huang Reinstated: The Unanimous Vote, the Government’s Input, and the Investigation That Continues

Nvidia’s board voted unanimously on Monday, November 23, 2026, to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, chief executive, and director, effective immediately—eleven days after it accepted his resignation and six days after its chairman told investors the board did not intend to bring him back. The board said it was satisfied with Mr. Huang’s candor and ability to lead, and that input from senior levels of the United States government left it confident his return poses no national security risk. The stock rose 31 percent on the day. The board’s own investigation did not end: the company states it will proceed to conclusion. The desk prints the announcement, the reported effort behind it, and the inquiries that remain open. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0900 ET, Tuesday, November 24, 2026.

THE ANNOUNCEMENT

Nvidia announced before the market opened on Monday, November 23, that Jensen Huang had been reinstated as president and chief executive officer and as a member of the board, effective immediately. The board’s vote was unanimous.

The company’s statement gives the grounds in its own words. The vote followed “a series of discussions and meetings with Mr. Huang, after which the Board was satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang’s candor and ability to lead NVIDIA.” It continues: “In the process of reaching its decision, the Board received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang’s reinstatement poses no national security risk.”

Harvey C. Jones, the chairman, said: “The Board believes Jensen is the right person to lead NVIDIA, and that his reinstatement is in the best interests of our stockholders, employees, partners and customers.” The statement thanked Colette Kress “for her outstanding service as interim CEO, in addition to her continuing responsibilities as NVIDIA’s Chief Financial Officer.” Ms. Kress, chief financial officer since 2013, had held the chief executive’s office for the eleven days. Mr. Huang, in the same statement, thanked the chairman, Ms. Kress, and the executive staff for leading the company during the period.

ELEVEN DAYS, THREE POSITIONS

The board’s positions, in order. On Thursday, November 12, the company announced that Mr. Huang had resigned as president, chief executive, and director, effective immediately. The statement gave no reason. Ms. Kress became interim chief executive, Mr. Jones became the first chairman in the company’s history, and the board opened a search for a successor inside and outside the company.

On Tuesday, November 17, on the company’s earnings call, Mr. Jones told investors the board had “unanimously accepted” the resignation and “does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO.”

On Monday, November 23, the board reinstated him unanimously. The successor search the board had announced eleven days earlier closed without a successor. No account names a candidate the search produced.

THE GOVERNMENT’S PART, AS REPORTED

The company’s statement says the board “received input from senior levels of the United States government.” It does not say from whom, or in what form.

The public advocacy is on the record. President Trump had said the week before that he wanted Mr. Huang back. On Monday he posted that “the NVIDIA Board of Directors has made the correct and proper decision to return Jensen Huang to the position of CEO,” and told CNBC the same morning that he had spoken to the board “directly and indirectly” and had convinced them. Reuters reported the reversal came under pressure from the President and also from employees, shareholders, and business partners. The Wall Street Journal’s account, printed below in the clippings, names the mechanism: the Attorney General’s Saturday briefing to the President and the Vice President, and a weekend of administration calls to directors. That account rests on officials and people familiar with the matter; the board’s own statement confirms only that input was received.

THE MARKET’S ANSWER

Nvidia’s shares closed Monday at $165.05, up 31.48 percent on the day—the stock’s best one-day percentage gain since 2003—and the company regained more than $900 billion of market value in the session, the largest one-day addition on record. The gain followed a multi-week decline that had run from the resignation through the week the board said there would be no return.

On Tuesday in Asia, the markets that supply and surround Nvidia’s production rose with it: Seoul’s Kospi index closed up 7.5 percent, Taipei’s market up 9.1 percent with Taiwan Semiconductor up 7.8 percent, and Tokyo’s Nikkei average up 4.5 percent.

WHAT DID NOT END ON MONDAY

The reinstatement did not close the matter that produced the resignation. The company’s statement reiterates that the committee of independent directors formed to investigate the reporting that Mr. Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort “will proceed to conclusion,” with independent counsel, and that Mr. Huang supports the investigation and will cooperate with it. The statement also records a second body, a National Security and Export Control Committee of independent directors, formed to oversee trade compliance and national security risks.

Beyond the board’s own inquiries, the record carries standing federal and state probes, inventoried by Reuters in the clippings below: an FBI and intelligence review of Mr. Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang, a Singapore-based businesswoman whom United States intelligence agencies examined for ties to China’s Ministry of State Security—China has denied the espionage allegations—a Justice Department antitrust review of Nvidia, and a Securities and Exchange Commission probe of the company’s artificial-intelligence investments and data-center guarantees. Nvidia has declined to comment on the probes. On Monday, hours after the reinstatement, the Commerce Department blacklisted Megaspeed, the Singapore firm Ms. Huang heads, which is suspected of smuggling Nvidia chips into China.

Mr. Huang is reported to be scheduled to testify before Congress in early December on the espionage question. Congressional committees are expected to question him in the coming days and weeks.

WHAT THE RECORD DOES NOT SHOW

The record does not show what the government told the board beyond the statement’s one sentence, or whether that input was written or oral. It does not show whether the independent committee’s findings will be published when its investigation concludes. It does not show what the board weighed on Sunday beyond the accounts printed below, which rest on people familiar with the session and have not been confirmed by the company. And it does not show what the December hearings will ask, or what Mr. Huang will answer. The announcement closed the vacancy. The questions the vacancy raised remain open, and the desk prints them as open.
