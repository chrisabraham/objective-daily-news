COP31, Filed From the Record

Objective

COP31: The Split Presidency, the Missing Words, and the Pact That Failed

The thirty-first climate conference met at Antalya under a presidency split between Türkiye and Australia, ran more than fifty hours past its scheduled close, and ended on Sunday, November 22, 2026, without the overarching agreement it had convened to produce—the first such failure since Copenhagen in 2009. This report sets out the record in the order it stands: how the conference came to have two heads, what its central text said when it was first drafted and what it no longer said when it was finally tabled, who rejected it in the closing plenary and who would have carried it, what the conference adopted anyway in its machinery, and what now travels to Bonn and to Addis Ababa. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0845 ET, Tuesday, November 24, 2026.

The Conference as Convened

The thirty-first Conference of the Parties to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change met at the Antalya Expo Center in Antalya, Türkiye, from Monday, November 9, to Friday, November 20, 2026, as scheduled. More than 190 countries sent delegations; the French paper Le Monde counted 194. A world leaders’ summit sat inside the first week, on November 11 and 12. The conference did not close on its scheduled Friday. It closed on Sunday, November 22, at 1933 local time, more than fifty hours into overtime—the longest conference of the parties on record—without adopting the overarching agreement, called a cover decision, that is the customary product of these meetings. It was the first such failure since the fifteenth conference, at Copenhagen in 2009.

The conference was run by two countries. At the thirtieth conference, at Belém in Brazil in 2025, Türkiye and Australia had both sought the presidency and neither would withdraw; the two belong to the same regional group from which the presidency rotated that year, and the treaty carries no mechanism for deciding between two claimants and no provision for a majority vote. The compromise, recorded in a modalities agreement dated November 21, 2025, split the office: Türkiye holds the presidency, with its environment minister, Murat Kurum, as president of the conference, and Australia leads the negotiations, with its climate change and energy minister, Chris Bowen, holding what the modalities call exclusive authority over them. Australia had campaigned for the conference for three years and, by the reporting from Belém, held the support of twenty-three of the twenty-eight nations in the regional group; it conceded the hosting and took the negotiating chair. The pre-conference meetings were held in October in Fiji and Tuvalu. Türkiye ran the action agenda, the program of voluntary commitments that runs beside the formal talks.

The brief for Antalya was implementation. The Belém conference had closed with a pledge to triple adaptation finance, a just-transition mechanism, and two road maps promised by the Brazilian presidency—one on moving away from fossil fuels, one on halting and reversing deforestation—which Brazil brought forward at Antalya. The presidency framed the meeting as the place where dialogue becomes global action. The agenda carried 165 negotiated items. Loss and damage—the fund for harms already suffered—stood in renewed focus after a catastrophic glacier collapse in Nepal. The United States did not take part as a party: under President Donald Trump it has withdrawn from international climate action, a fact recorded by the wire and wire-service accounts of the closing. Nineteen Democratic members of the House of Representatives attended in the second week as a delegation of the Sustainable Energy and Environment Coalition.

The Text That Shrank

The cover decision—the single text that summarizes what a conference decided—was titled the Antalya Pact. Its first draft was published on Monday, November 16, at eighteen pages and 144 paragraphs. Paragraph 65 of that draft called for a faster transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems and for the phase-out of inefficient fossil-fuel subsidies. The draft put a figure on the Belém pledge to triple adaptation finance: at least 120 billion dollars a year by 2035. A draft dated November 19 entertained, in brackets and therefore unsettled, a collective carbon-capture figure as high as 2.5 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide a year by 2035.

The fossil-fuel language did not survive. Every subsequent draft removed all mention of fossil fuels, after objections from Saudi Arabia and other oil- and gas-producing nations; the final text reached the subject only by recalling, by reference, the consensus reached at Dubai in 2023, where the parties had agreed for the first time to transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems. The condensed final package was released at about 0200 local time on Sunday, November 22, cut to ten pages. It contained no mention of fossil fuels, of oil, of natural gas, or of coal. It retained the presidency’s electrification ambition—to raise electricity’s share of global final energy consumption to 35 percent by 2035, from about 23 percent—without mandating that the new electricity be renewable and, in the final version, without its earlier explicit link to the standing goals of tripling renewable capacity by 2030 and doubling the rate of energy-efficiency improvement. It set a collective goal of deploying carbon capture, utilization and storage at gigatonne scale by 2035. On adaptation finance it called for scaled-up resources and easier access to grants and concessional finance, with no dollar figure and no baseline year; the 120-billion-dollar figure of the first draft was gone. On loss and damage it urged stronger coordination and predictable funding; the specific funding-gap numbers carried in the November 19 draft were gone. It launched an Antalya-to-Addis Ababa road map on adaptation and resilience finance, to be carried by the Turkish presidency and by Ethiopia, host of the next conference, with conclusions due there. Delegations were given until 0300 Greenwich Mean Time to lodge objections, ahead of a closing plenary set for 0600.

Three deadlocks ran under the drafting, and the Atlantic Council’s experts named them in the same order the record gives them: whether the final document would mention fossil fuels; how much developed countries would contribute to adaptation finance; and whether the electrification focus was, as its critics charged, an implicit greenwashing of continued fossil-fuel production, since the target counts electricity from any source. Behind the first stood the arithmetic of the process: a cover decision requires consensus, and the petrostates—Saudi Arabia and Russia named in the reporting among them—have long opposed any reference to fossil fuels in these texts.

The Sunday Plenary

At the closing plenary on Sunday, the text was rejected. The European Union, the United Kingdom, Panama, Switzerland and Norway rejected it, and the Marshall Islands, Belize and Vanuatu, and the group of least developed countries with them. Ireland’s climate minister, Darragh O’Brien, spoke for the European Union. The rejection, as the French paper Le Monde carried it, read: “We will not endorse a decision that does not respond to the science, that disregards the implementation gap, and that actually risks deepening the chasm between global commitments and the action required to correct the overshoot and bring 1.5 degrees back to within reach.” A second statement in the same account read: “This text falls short of what international law requires, what science demands, and what our vulnerable communities urgently need.” The objectors’ listed grounds included the omission itself: “The major cause of the climate crisis—coal, oil and gas—fails to rate a mention.” The text had its supporters in the hall: Russia, China and the Arab countries, by Le Monde‘s account.

The presidency acknowledged the count in the room. Kurum said: “We have done our utmost,” and “it is clear that there are parties in this hall who oppose this text. We recognize that there is no consensus on this document.” The Associated Press reported that Kurum was absent from the final meeting called to approve the pact, and that the announcement of the failure fell to Elchin Allahverdiyev of Azerbaijan. Bowen, who had chaired the negotiations, said: “Unfortunately, we were unable to reach agreement on a cover decision. And that’s disappointing, very disappointing.” The secretary-general of the United Nations, António Guterres, said: “I am deeply disappointed that COP31 has ended without consensus on an overarching outcome,” and, in the statement carried by the Inter Press Service: “This setback does not change the facts. There may be no consensus in the negotiating room. There is no ambiguity in the science.” Mohamed Adow, director of the energy think tank Power Shift Africa, called it “the worst ever COP” and said “its collapse needs to be a wake up call for the world”; he told Reuters he believed the conference “was hijacked by some oil states.” Because consensus was not reached, the draft Antalya Pact—with its electrification target, its carbon-capture goal, its road map and the rest—has no formal standing. The desk records the fact without a verdict on it: the text was not adopted.

What Was Adopted Anyway

The cover decision was the conference’s headline product, and it failed. The conference’s machinery, item by item, produced a record of its own, and the two records stand separately.

On the evening of Friday, November 20—before the cover decision collapsed—the parties adopted the Article 6 package: the guidance decisions on Articles 6.2, 6.4 and 6.8 of the Paris Agreement, which govern how countries trade emissions reductions and credit them, and the decision on funding for Article 6.2 infrastructure. The adoption passed with no comment from any party. The rulebook settled at Baku in 2024 was left intact rather than reopened; the formal review of the Article 6.2 guidance remains scheduled for 2028; an informal dialogue on the technical expert reviews was requested for the Bonn sessions in June 2027; and the funding question for Article 6.2 infrastructure goes to Bonn as well, toward a draft decision at the next conference. The supervisory body of the Article 6.4 mechanism was directed to keep its standards, methodologies and tools anchored in environmental integrity and the best available science, and to be more transparent about how it orders its work. The Adaptation Fund completed its transition and will receive a share of proceeds from the Article 6.4 mechanism.

The just-transition decision extended the Just Transition Work Programme by three years and operationalized, through a phased approach, the mechanism agreed at Belém the year before. Bowen had promised the summit would give birth to that mechanism; the pushback against building a new body came mainly from European countries, and the mechanism stands operationalized in name, without a settled institutional structure, mandate or resources of its own. The adopted text carries the first mention of critical minerals in an agreed conference decision, recognizing equitable value chains and local value addition for developing countries. A decision on the Global Goal on Adaptation was adopted, carrying forward the work of measuring progress, including indicators such as the coverage of flood early-warning systems. A decision on the Climate Finance Work Programme—created at Belém and added as a standing item at Antalya—appoints two co-chairs, one from a developed and one from a developing country, to organize its activities in 2027 and 2028, among them workshops and a ministerial roundtable at the next conference on implementing the new collective quantified goal on climate finance, under which developed countries are to mobilize 300 billion dollars a year by 2035. The decision does not take up the question the developing countries brought to Antalya: a definition, with a baseline, of the Belém pledge to triple adaptation finance.

Outside the negotiated texts, the presidency’s electrification ambition gathered signatures without gathering consensus: more than seventy countries backed the 35-by-35 target by the World Resources Institute’s count, about eighty by the Atlantic Council’s, and a closing report from the venue put the number near ninety. Canada announced 116 million Canadian dollars in international climate projects, including up to 10 million for early-warning systems. The United Kingdom put 400 million pounds into a new fund for clean power, grids and storage in developing economies, a fund intended to mobilize five billion. On November 12, fourteen countries issued an Antalya Statement and a policy playbook for growing carbon markets; both are initiatives of a coalition of governments, not texts negotiated at the conference, and neither depended on the closing.

What Stands, and What Waits

A failed cover decision does not repeal anything. The Paris Agreement stands, and the decisions taken under it stand, including the Dubai consensus of 2023, whose sentence on transitioning away from fossil fuels the Antalya presidency’s final draft recalled by reference and could not carry in its own words. The obligations the parties already hold—including the finance goal of 300 billion dollars a year by 2035—are unchanged by the absence of a new text. What Antalya was asked to add, it did not add: no restated sentence on fossil fuels, no quantified adaptation-finance target with a baseline against which it could be measured, no figures for the loss-and-damage funding gap, and no decision on the infrastructure funding for the carbon-market machinery it had just re-adopted.

Those questions now travel. The next formal sessions are the intersessional meetings at Bonn in June 2027, where the Article 6.2 dialogue and the infrastructure-funding work are already on the table. The thirty-second conference meets at Addis Ababa, in Ethiopia, in November 2027. The Antalya-to-Addis Ababa road map—the one product of the failed pact that was designed to outlive it, since it was launched rather than adopted—asks the two presidencies to map predictable sources of finance for adaptation and loss and damage, with its conclusions due for consideration at Addis Ababa.

What the collapse means is itself in dispute, and the dispute is part of the record. The World Resources Institute’s president, Ani Dasgupta, called the outcome “a failure of political leadership, not a failure of the Paris Agreement.” Adow’s verdict, that the process was captured by the states it most needed to move, stands beside it. The Atlantic Council’s experts, several of them present in Antalya, recorded a third position: that the failure is a lost opportunity and a political miscalculation by a two-headed presidency, and that the commitments made in Paris and Dubai bind regardless—that consensus, on this showing, is not everything in climate policy. A former Canadian environment minister, writing the day the conference closed, drew the conclusion furthest from the institution: that it is time to boycott the conferences altogether. The desk prints the four positions in one paragraph and joins none of them. The record is printed whole. The desk draws no conclusion from it.
