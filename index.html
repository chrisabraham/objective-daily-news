The House Took Fifty Years to Grow Hands: X10 and the Relay Era, the Sensor Stack, the Luxury Tier, and the Humanoid Machines of 2026

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 2315 ET, Friday, November 20, 2026

The arc divides into three eras. In the first, X10, developed by Pico Electronics in 1975 and sold from 1978, sent commands over the house wiring as bursts at the zero crossings of the alternating current—one-way, unacknowledged, unauthenticated. A controller could order a lamp on; nothing in the protocol could confirm that the lamp was on, and a neighbor on the same distribution transformer could receive the signal. In the second era, the house acquired senses in sequence: the learning thermostat in 2011, the voice assistant in 2014, the doorbell camera, the LiDAR vacuum that mapped the home in 2016, and the Matter standard in 2022 to bind them. Through 2020 the flagship stack was eyes, ears, and a floor-bound puck—perception complete, manipulation absent. The era’s architecture risk has a date: April 15, 2022, when Insteon’s servers shut down without notice and hub-dependent homes went dark. The third era is the present one, and its machines are countable. 1X’s NEO, at $20,000 or $499 a month, is the only humanoid a consumer can order; its Hayward factory entered full-scale production on April 30, 2026, and the verified count of units delivered to a paying customer as of November 13, 2026 is zero, with early output routed to internal testing and a company statement pointing to first shipments in December. Tesla’s Optimus is in early production at Fremont, is not for sale at any price, and is targeted at outside deliveries around the end of 2027. Figure’s Figure 03 works in BMW plants; Boston Dynamics’ entire 2026 Atlas production is committed to Hyundai and Google DeepMind; Unitree’s machines are orderable today, the R1 from $4,900. Underneath the machines, two ledgers govern. The hardware ledger: actuators and drive components run at roughly 50 to 60 percent of a humanoid’s bill of materials on every sourced estimate—Schaeffler at about 50 percent, Morgan Stanley at 56, Hyundai Mobis at 60. The software ledger: the shipping reasoning stack is NVIDIA’s Isaac GR00T line, now at N1.7, trained on 20,854 hours of human egocentric video and in simulation, where the verified compressions are specific—42 years of a single robot’s experience in about 32 hours of compute in 2021, about 10 years in two hours in 2025, and 140 simulated years of self-play accumulated by Skild AI’s soccer policy in September 2026. Bartleby the Scrivener: Could It Happen in New York Today?

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1330 ET, Friday, November 20, 2026

The story’s beats divide into three classes under the modern record. Two beats are now impossible as written: no American jurisdiction may arrest a man for vagrancy as a condition of being—the Supreme Court voided the classic vagrancy ordinances in 1972—and no pretrial detainee in New York may be warehoused unexamined while he starves, because the charge the story’s conduct now maps to carries mandatory release, and a refusal to eat in custody triggers medical monitoring and, at the point of danger, a court proceeding on feeding that New York’s highest court resolved in the state’s favor in 2013. Three beats are rerouted: the dismissal, the removal from the office, and the arrest each still happen, but each now generates process—a personally communicated order, a desk appearance ticket or an arraignment inside about a day, a competency examination if the defendant cannot or will not participate. One beat remains possible, and it is the story’s first: the refusal itself. New York City holds a legal right to shelter unique in the United States, and in the fiscal year ending June 30, 2025, it recorded 634 deaths of people experiencing homelessness, 329 of them people classified as non-sheltered. Fifteen people died in the city’s jail custody in 2025. The nets exist, are named in law, and are documented in operation.
