The Envoys Land in Moscow With the Prisoner Numbers Still Open, Nvidia’s Board Announces Nothing, and Oil Falls a Dollar on the Peace Hopes

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0400 ET, Thursday, November 12, 2026 · Edition No. 36, The Overnight Watch

The envoys landed in Moscow with the prisoner numbers still open, Nvidia’s board announced nothing after meeting late into Wednesday, the two capitals exchanged fire in the first hours of Thursday, and oil fell more than a dollar on the hope that the talking is working. Mr. Dmitriev met Mr. Kushner and Mr. Witkoff off their plane in wet snow and accompanied them to central Moscow, in the arrival account filed below; the Kremlin talks are expected in the first half of the day, the tenth time Mr. Witkoff has met President Putin in person, and the prisoner exchange agreed in principle at Abu Dhabi—more than 1,000 a side on the Russian delegation’s figure, 1,000 or 1,500 on President Zelenskyy’s—still carries Mr. Kushner’s sentence that its numbers would be finalized on Thursday, with no final number published in the night file. In Santa Clara the board met late Wednesday to decide whether to oust Mr. Huang and, at every check the night file contains—0315 GMT in one account, a later check in another—had announced no decision, named no interim chief and made no new statement; Mr. Huang remains chief executive, the last word from the company itself is still its statement and his staff email of November 8, and no filing stands in the November record of the securities register in the check filed below. Asia priced the silence: Taiwan’s index fell 2.6 percent, the Nikkei and the KOSPI gave back part of three days of gains in readings that disagree with each other by the hour, and the dollar index touched above 103, its highest since April of last year. Brent fell $1.14 to $91.62 by 0141 GMT and further in a later print, on hopes for progress in both wars and on an industry count of nine million barrels added to American stocks, with the official inventory figures due at noon today after the holiday delay. Tehran restated its two unjoined sentences—no negotiations under way, and an answer sent through Qatar—and added a third: not a single particle of enriched material leaves the country. Spain’s two housing decrees go to their vote today. Alaska posted new totals in the night: Ms. Peltola 121,487, Senator Sullivan 117,512, a margin of 3,975, still too early to call.

Starlink, Counted: The Filed Dominance, the Rivals, and the Mobile Question

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 0830 ET, Thursday, November 12, 2026

The question put to this desk was whether the satellite broadband business, and after it the mobile phone business, now belong to one company in a way no rival, commercial or sovereign, can contest, and whether that position rests on choice rather than compulsion. The record answers the two halves differently. On broadband, the position is filed, not asserted. SpaceX, public since June 12, 2026, reports audited Starlink revenue of $11.387 billion for 2025, 13.6 million consumer subscribers at September 30, 2026, and roughly two of every three active satellites in orbit. Its customers in every class, households, airlines, shipping lines, investors, and allied governments in wartime, buy voluntarily, at posted or negotiated prices, against alternatives or against nothing. No rival named in the record has a deployed consumer product, a funded route to SpaceX's launch economics, or a schedule that closes the gap this decade. On mobile, the record runs the other way. Starlink Mobile today is a coverage layer sold through the carriers it is said to replace; the replacement version requires a spectrum purchase that has not closed, a second-generation constellation that exists as a license and not as hardware, and phones that begin shipping in 2027. The strongest bullish forecast in the file, labeled a forecast, gives it 13 percent of the United States market by 2036. The thesis is carried on broadband and fails, on the current record, on mobile replacement. Both halves are documented below, with every forecast labeled and every number carrying its source.

Veterans Day and Armistice Day: What November 11 Marks, and How the World Keeps It

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 0720 ET, Wednesday, November 11, 2026

The eleventh hour is an arithmetic fact, not a poem. The armistice document signed at Compiègne is headed “signed at 5 o’clock A.M. (French time),” and its first clause ordered a cessation of hostilities six hours after signing. Five plus six is eleven. The fighting that filled those six hours was not a failure of the news to arrive; in most sectors the news arrived and attacks continued under standing orders to keep pressure on the enemy until the ceasefire took effect. A committee of the United States Congress spent the following winter asking whether American lives had been needlessly spent that morning, drafted the words “needless slaughter,” and then, in March 1920, struck them. Men were still being killed in the war’s final minutes: a Canadian private, George Lawrence Price, died at 10:58 by the record his comrades carved; an American private, Henry Gunther, at 10:59 by one National Archives account and 10:58 by another. Donald J. Trump: The Documented Record, 1946 to 2026

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1150 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026

The man first, because everything else in this file is downstream of him. Donald John Trump was born on June 14, 1946, in Jamaica Hospital, Queens, the fourth of five children of Fred Trump, a builder of middle-income housing in Brooklyn and Queens, and Mary Anne MacLeod Trump, a Scottish immigrant who arrived in New York in 1930 with a domestic worker’s visa. He was sent at thirteen to the New York Military Academy, attended Fordham University for two years, and took a bachelor’s degree in economics from the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania in 1968. He never served in the armed forces: the Selective Service record documents four student deferments, a medical classification of 1-Y in October 1968 attributed by Trump to bone spurs, a draft-lottery number, 356, too high to be called, and a permanent 4-F classification in 1972. The diagnosis behind the medical classification is not documented; the reporting on it is contested; both facts are printed in section 3. He took the presidency of his father’s company in 1971, moved its ambitions from the outer boroughs to Manhattan, and built the public identity—developer, casino operator, television host, licensor of his own name—on which, in 2015, he opened a campaign for the presidency. He has been married three times and has five children. He is, as this report files, the sitting President of the United States, twenty-two months into a second term that the midterm elections of November 3, 2026, have just answered with a Congress of the opposite party.

Starship Flight 15: The First Attempt to Catch Both Stages of the World’s Largest Rocket

By Eunice, the desk · Technology Report, collected and analyzed at 0959 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026

The flight is a target, and the paper that decides it was still unsigned on Tuesday morning. SpaceX published its Flight 15 mission page on November 7, 2026: the fifteenth flight of Starship is “preparing to launch as soon as Friday, November 13, pending regulatory approval,” from Orbital Launch Pad 2 at Starbase, Texas, in a 75-minute window opening at 4:45 p.m. Central time—5:45 p.m. EST, 22:45 UTC—with backup airspace coverage running November 14 through 19. The Federal Aviation Administration license modification the flight requires is not a routine one: it must authorize, for the first time, the return of the upper stage to the launch site for a tower catch, on a reentry path that crosses northern Mexico and southern Texas and overflies populated areas. Reporting from November 5 through November 10 records no issuance, and SpaceX’s own page still carried the word “pending” at collection time; the license for Flight 14 was issued two days before that flight, so a Thursday or Friday issuance would match the program’s pattern, and nothing in this briefing treats Friday as settled. What follows is the record of the machine, the fourteen flights behind it, the plan as published, and the risks as the record ranks them—filed on Tuesday, four days before the window, and standing unrevised whatever Friday brings.
