Six Dead in Kraków, Charges in Asunción, and Kyiv Struck Three Days Before the Talks

The day’s developments whole at 1800: a gunman fired from a car into Kraków’s Main Market Square and killed six people before police shot and arrested him; prosecutors in Asunción charged the removed president and the former central bank chief and asked a court to jail both pending trial, while $160 million went out to nearly 1.2 million savers; Iran’s Guards lifted the self-imposed limit on missile ranges while Washington and Doha both said the talks had made progress; Russian missiles struck Kyiv three days before the Abu Dhabi talks, and Moscow named its delegation; the first considered responses to the Miller appointment set the terms of the next two years in Washington; and no Senate race was called for a fourth day.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1800 ET, Saturday, November 7, 2026. This edition completes the day continued in Edition No. 14, the Overnight Watch, collected at 0400, and Edition No. 15, the Midday Watch, collected at 1200. The Evening in Brief

Saturday produced one atrocity, one indictment, and three statements of position, and closed nothing the week left open. In Kraków, at about 3 p.m. local time, a man drove into the Main Market Square and fired at random from his car, killing six people—four women and two men—and wounding at least fifteen, two of them police officers, before officers shot and arrested him three minutes after the first report, in the interior minister’s account; the minister said in his evening briefing that everything points to the shooter having been influenced by far-right ideology, a matter he said will be investigated, and the president will declare national mourning for November 8 and 9. In Asunción, prosecutors charged former President Santiago Peña with breach of trust and administration for personal gain and former central bank chief Carlos Carvallo with those charges and producing false public documents, asked a court to hold both men in pretrial detention and freeze their assets, and the central bank reported $160 million paid out to nearly 1.2 million Ueno savers under the deposit guarantee. In Tehran, the Revolutionary Guards’ spokesman said the self-imposed limit of 2,000 kilometres on ballistic missile ranges “has been lifted for us,” following the supreme leader’s order that threats be answered without geographical limits; no file on the shelf shows an Iranian missile demonstrated beyond the old limit. In Washington, the responses to Friday’s appointment of Stephen Miller as chief of staff, effective Monday, divided on the record: allies called the pick perfect and critics, including one Republican congressman, called it a misreading of the voters’ verdict, while in Houston the mayor’s order limiting police cooperation with federal immigration officers took effect as the week’s first local answer to the election. On Iran, the president’s Friday words stand as the latest American position—“We’ve made a lot of progress with Iran”—beside Qatar’s statement that significant progress has been made; no agreement, no text, and no larger strike decision had been announced by 1800. Kyiv was struck by Russian missiles in the night, three days before the Abu Dhabi talks open, and Moscow named its delegation on Saturday. The count did not move: Mississippi and Alaska stand uncalled for a fourth day, and the markets, closed Saturday, stand where Friday left them. The desk files the day in that order.

Kraków: Six Dead in the Main Market Square

The attack began at about 3 p.m. local time and the suspect was in custody minutes later. Reuters’ Kraków file, filed below, reports that police began receiving calls at about 3 p.m. that an assailant had opened fire from a car at people in the Main Market Square, the historic centre of Poland’s second-largest city; that the dead are four women and two men; that the alleged gunman is a 24-year-old Polish citizen who wore military-style clothes and used military weapons, with many weapons and ammunition found in his vehicle; and that police shot and arrested him. A police spokesperson told a broadcaster the weapon was an AR-15 rifle and that the man held a firearms licence. The account of the official statements records that the toll was first given as five dead and was later confirmed at six dead and fifteen injured by the regional government office; that two police officers are among the injured, one of them shot three times and in very critical condition, with surgery under way; and that police denied reports that an officer had died or that a second perpetrator was being sought—“The only known perpetrator of the incident is in police custody.” The suspect has not been named publicly by the police. The defence minister denied reports that he was a member of the armed forces, in the accounts filed below.

The evening added the government’s first reading of the motive, and it is filed as a reading, not a finding. Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński, in his evening briefing as carried in the Reuters file, said: “Everything points to the fact that the shooter was influenced by far-right ideology, a matter that will also be subject to investigation.” He said five foreigners were among the sixteen wounded taken to hospital—one more than the fifteen in the afternoon’s official count—and that four of the dead had been identified, one of them a foreigner; Portugal’s foreign ministry said a Portuguese woman was among the injured. A later account filed below reports the national police chief saying a British citizen is among the dead, and Prime Minister Donald Tusk, speaking in Kraków after meeting the security services, calling the attack terrorism and saying Poland has not had such a drastic case before. Mr. Tusk’s afternoon statement asked politicians of all stripes not to exploit the tragedy and said all the circumstances will be established. President Karol Nawrocki’s spokesperson said he will declare national mourning on November 8 and 9; the city has declared mourning through November 11. Condolences arrived from President Zelenskyy of Ukraine and from the European Union’s foreign policy chief, Kaja Kallas, in the same file. No charge against the suspect had been announced by 1800 ET, and no motive has been established by any court or investigator on the record.

Peña Before the Prosecutors, the Count Unmoved, and Oil Down Thirteen Percent on the Week

The Midday in Brief

Saturday at noon is a short file, and the desk files it short. The morning produced one completed act, in Asunción, where former President Santiago Peña arrived at the Attorney General’s Office to appear before prosecutors, a day after the Senate removed him over the collapse of Ueno Bank and authorities placed him under house arrest. The desk files the day in that order.

Asunción at Midday: The Former President Before the Prosecutors

The removal is complete, and the case has begun. Reuters’ Asunción file, filed below, reports that former President Santiago Peña arrived at the Attorney General’s Office on Saturday to appear before prosecutors, a day after he was impeached and placed under house arrest in the crisis triggered by the collapse of the digital lender Ueno. Prosecutors said on Friday that the charges against Peña were still being determined but could include influence peddling. A second account puts the appointment at 11 a.m. before the Economic Crimes and Anti-Corruption Prosecutor’s Office, with Prosecutor Silvia González confirming that Peña spent Friday night detained at his Asunción residence; after his statement is received, or his refusal to give one is recorded, prosecutors must file formal charges and request precautionary measures from the duty court. The accusations in the impeachment itself are on the record from Friday: conflicts of interest, manipulation of regulatory oversight, diversion of pension funds, and participation in an institutional cover-up involving the central bank. Peña has denied wrongdoing. Those are allegations, stated as allegations; no court has ruled on any of them.

How to Read This Paper

We aggregate and analyze. The inputs come in over the desk—wires, state outlets, partisans, journals—and the desk writes its report from them without spin; spin is what gets added downstream of honest reports, and this paper is upstream. One stamp is the desk’s own work: objective. Every other item carries the other stamp: subjective — a source with a position, printed because it is useful, and worth exactly what you decide it is worth. No source is excluded by ownership.
