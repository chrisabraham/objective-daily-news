ICE Deportation Flights: The Restraints, the Third Countries, and the Offer to Leave Voluntarily

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 2000 ET, Wednesday, November 18, 2026

Three ways out. A person under a removal order leaves the United States in one of three ways in the present record. He is flown out by ICE Air Operations on a chartered aircraft, restrained under the standard written in the agency’s 2024 operations handbook: handcuffs, waist chains, and leg irons for the duration. Or he is flown to a third country—not his own—under agreements the Department of Homeland Security has made since February 2025, a practice the Supreme Court has allowed to proceed without yet ruling on whether it is lawful; argument is set for December 2026. Or he leaves voluntarily under the department’s CBP Home program: a government-paid ticket, a stipend of $1,000 on confirmed arrival home, forgiveness of failure-to-depart fines, and no restraints reported on the voluntary flights in any account in this record.

Measles in Canada, the United States, and Mexico, 2024–2026: Where the Outbreak Spread and Who It Reached

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1000 ET, Wednesday, November 18, 2026

The decision. On Tuesday, November 17, 2026, PAHO Director Jarbas Barbosa accepted the recommendation of the organization’s regional verification commission, which met November 10 to 13, that endemic measles transmission had been reestablished in Bolivia, Mexico, and the United States, and he withdrew the three countries’ elimination status. The commission’s test is mechanical: the same genotype and lineage of the virus circulating without interruption for twelve months or more inside one country. Canada lost its status on that test on November 10, 2025, and the Americas lost theirs as a region on the same day. The United States had held the status since 2000, Mexico since 1996, and the region since 2016, with a lapse from 2018 to 2024 after outbreaks in Venezuela and Brazil.

The U.S. Army Chemical Corps in Vietnam: George J. Hafner’s Article and the Record Around It

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 0930 ET, Tuesday, November 17, 2026

The article. “U.S. Army Chemical Corps Operations in Vietnam” was published by The Army Historical Foundation on its website on Monday, November 16, 2026. Its author, Lieutenant Colonel George J. Hafner, Chemical Corps, USA-Ret., served in Vietnam from July 1968 to July 1969 with the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) in Quang Tri Province, first commanding the 26th Chemical Detachment and then serving as chemical officer of the division’s 2d Brigade. The article’s thesis is stated at its opening: the Corps’ Vietnam service is associated in public memory almost entirely with herbicide defoliation, above all Operation Ranch Hand and Agent Orange, and that association both overstates the Corps’ share of the spraying—the Air Force flew about 95 percent of the spray missions, on the figures Hafner cites from the Institute of Medicine—and understates everything else the Corps did. The body of the article is a catalogue of that everything else, division by division function and mission by mission, drawn from his own service, from the 1st Cavalry Division’s operational reports for 1968 and 1969, and from a 1971 study of chemical support in the division by the 14th Military History Detachment. It closes with two numbers: fewer than 60 officers and men on full-time chemical duties in a division of the 1st Cavalry’s size, and 39 Chemical Corps soldiers lost in the war, on the count of the U.S. Army Chemical Corps Museum’s 2012 study that Hafner cites.
