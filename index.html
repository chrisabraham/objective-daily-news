Race and Ethnicity: What the Record Shows About the Government's Classification of Americans

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1000 ET, Saturday, November 14, 2026

The United States government sorts its residents into racial and ethnic categories of its own design, and it revises the design on its own schedule. Under the federal standard issued in 1977 and reaffirmed in 1997, a person having origins in the Middle East or North Africa is White, and has been White in federal statistics for nearly fifty years; more than 3.5 million people reported Middle Eastern or North African descent inside the White category in the 2020 census. A revision of the standard, published in the Federal Register on March 28, 2024, redraws the map: seven minimum categories where the old standard kept five races and a separate ethnicity, one combined question where the old standard asked two, Middle Eastern or North African standing as a category of its own, detailed origins collected by default, full compliance due by 2029, and the 2030 census as the first count under the new design. Implementation has since slipped twice, the Census Bureau has withdrawn its plan to introduce the new questions on its largest survey in 2027, and a rule proposed on September 10, 2026 would bar race and ethnicity questions from the decennial census altogether. The people being classified account for none of these changes. The record behind the current filings runs through two centuries of the same operation: a naturalization statute that admitted “free white persons” and left the courts to decide who they were, applicant by applicant; a federal dictionary of 1911 that counted South Italians as a race apart from North Italians; a Virginia registrar who reclassified his state's Indian families as colored by circular and surname list; a one-drop rule written into a state statute and into census instructions; and, abroad, a South African classification office that had reclassified 18,469 people by 1956 and a French republic whose law forbids the questions entirely. Every version of the system arrived carrying a justification in its era's moral vocabulary. The justifications are part of the record, and this report prints them.

NIMBYism: What the Record Shows About the Veto Over Housing, Energy, and Data Centers

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1945 ET, Friday, November 13, 2026

The opposition has a consistent structure across fifty years of American land-use fights, and the research literature now describes it with unusual precision. Residents support housing, clean energy, data centers, and homeless services in the abstract at high rates. They oppose the same facilities at specific addresses near their homes at high rates. The gap between the two positions is not explained by ideology: renters in expensive cities register it at nearly the same level as homeowners, and liberal homeowners register it alongside conservatives. Three mechanisms account for most of the measured effect. Losses register roughly twice as heavily as equivalent gains. A home is most owners' largest asset and cannot be diversified, so its protection dominates local voting. And opposition rises measurably when a developer's profit is made salient, by twenty percentage points in the best-known experiment, an effect its authors attribute to resentment of the developer's gain rather than fear of the resident's loss. The venues that decide these questions hear almost exclusively from opponents: in the largest study of hearing participation, about two-thirds of commenters opposed the housing before them and about fifteen percent supported it. The outcomes are legible in the record: a 495-home project on a San Francisco parking lot reversed on environmental grounds in 2021 and still unbuilt in 2026; a 25,000-job Amazon campus withdrawn from Queens in 2019 while polls showed most New Yorkers approved of it; data-center projects worth $130 billion blocked or delayed in the first quarter of 2026 alone.

Concrete: What the Record Shows About the Most Consumed Material on Earth

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1800 ET, Friday, November 13, 2026

Concrete is a manufactured rock with a twenty-century documentary record, and the record supports a short set of load-bearing facts. The material is cement paste (Portland cement and water) binding sand and stone; it sets by hydration, a chemical reaction that proceeds underwater and continues for years, reaching about 90 percent of its strength in 28 days; its tensile strength runs a tenth to a seventh of its compressive strength, so modern concrete is a composite with steel, the steel carrying tension and the rock carrying compression. The Romans built with a volcanic-ash version, opus caementicium, whose durability was a mystery for two millennia; the mechanism, hot mixing with quicklime, leaving reactive lime clasts that dissolve into cracks and recrystallize as stone, was demonstrated by MIT researchers in Science Advances in January 2023 and confirmed archaeologically on December 9, 2025, when a Nature Communications paper reported an unfinished Pompeii construction site holding dry piles of quicklime premixed with pozzolan, exactly as the hypothesis required. The Pantheon, completed about 126 AD, remains the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. The modern material dates to Joseph Aspdin's 1824 patent for Portland cement and to the reinforced patents of the later nineteenth century, Monier's pivotal 1867 filing among them, and its monument is the Hoover Dam: 3.25 million cubic yards in the dam itself, by the Bureau of Reclamation's own figures, cooled through 582 miles of embedded pipe because a single monolithic pour would have taken an estimated 125 years to cool. The scale today is about 4 billion tonnes of cement a year, a figure now falling from its 2023 peak of 4.1 billion as China's building boom ends, and China poured more cement in 2011 to 2013, about 6.5 billion tonnes, than the United States poured in the entire twentieth century, about 4.5 billion. The costs are two. Cement accounts for 7 to 8 percent of global carbon dioxide emissions, and roughly 60 percent of that is process emissions from calcining limestone, chemistry rather than fuel, which is why the working fixes are clinker substitution, commercial since 2025 in the form of LC3, and carbon capture, operating since June 2025 at Heidelberg's Brevik plant in Norway; the third announced fix, electrochemical cement, has a standards designation and two technology-company offtake deals and no plant, its federal grant cancelled in May 2025 and its first works unbuilt toward 2028. And the material's American folklore is adjudicated fact: in 1980s New York, a club of six concrete contractors, coordinated by four crime families, controlled Manhattan contracts above $2 million and paid a 2 percent cut to Ralph Scopo, the concrete workers' union president who received 100 years in the 1986 Mafia Commission Trial. The culture keeps returning to the material, most recently John Wilson's 2026 feature documentary, The History of Concrete, and the desk's standard for the subject remains the one set by David Owen's 2003 New Yorker piece, whose famous figures this report prints as Owen's, where no primary source carries them.

Podcasting: What the Record Shows About Who Invented It, Who Named It, and Who Made It Famous

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1700 ET, Friday, November 13, 2026

Podcasting's mechanism was working before it had a name, its name existed before most of its famous hosts had recorded anything, and its mass market opened on June 28, 2005, when Apple put more than 3,000 shows into iTunes and took a million subscriptions in two days, four years before Adam Carolla, Marc Maron, and Joe Rogan launched the shows the popular story starts with. The mechanism is one element in a syndication format: the RSS enclosure, proposed by Tristan Louis in October 2000, implemented by Dave Winer in the RSS 0.92 specification of December 25, 2000, and demonstrated by Winer on January 11, 2001, in an essay, “Payloads for RSS,” that credits Adam Curry's persistence as what got it built. The first sustained original content arrived in September 2003, Christopher Lydon's interview feed, built by Winer; Kevin Marks demonstrated delivery from feed to iPod in October 2003; Ben Hammersley put the word podcasting in print in February 2004 and Dannie Gregoire gave the practice its working name that September; Curry's Daily Source Code, from August 13, 2004, was the developer community's test feed and the medium's first celebrity-fronted show. Curry himself was not the mascot of the story but one of its principals: an MTV host who registered mtv.com in 1993 and fought his network over it in federal court, then co-founded a web company absorbed into a public company in a 1999 merger the SEC records at a reported $230 million valuation, though what he personally took from it is documented nowhere, and his own later figure for it is his own. The comedians' contribution is real and different in kind: Carolla proved a fired radio host could rebuild an audience independently, Maron built the confessional interview into the medium's prestige form, Rogan built its largest audience and its licensing price, and none of the three invented, or claimed to invent, the machinery. The record also corrects the record-keepers: the first download record belongs to Ricky Gervais's newspaper show in 2006, Carolla's 2011 Guinness title is a certification his own certified total contradicts against Gervais's published numbers, and the Library of Congress introduces Curry as the Podfather in a sentence that misdates his show by a year. The argument the inventors started, over whether the medium stays open, is still running: it is called Podcasting 2.0, and its founding show, Curry and John C. Dvorak's No Agenda, ended on October 29, 2026, three months after Dvorak's death, after nineteen years.
