The Evening File — The Desk’s Report

Objective

The Chips Bounce Back, the Count Does Not Move, and No Decision Comes

The day’s developments whole at 1800: the morning’s payrolls beat stands as the day’s largest settled fact, New York closes mixed with technology up and the Dow down, the semiconductor index recovers about a third of Thursday’s fall, a Federal Reserve president says more tightening is likely, Europe closes lower across the board, no race is called for a third day, and the two decisions the week is waiting on—Iran in Washington, the count in Mississippi and Alaska—are both still waiting at evening.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1800 ET, Friday, November 6, 2026. This edition completes the day continued in Edition No. 10, the Overnight Watch, collected at 0400, and Edition No. 11, the Midday Watch, collected at 1200. Those editions stand unrevised, as a log stands. Where the evening figures differ from the noon figures, the difference is stated here, in the open.

The Evening in Brief

Friday corrected Thursday in the labor report and did not correct it in the count. The October payrolls figure printed at midday stands at the close: 165,000 jobs added, against a consensus near 60,000, unemployment at 4.1 percent, and the two prior months revised up by a combined 84,000. Around that fact the afternoon produced its considered responses. The chip shares that fell twelve percent on Thursday recovered about four percent. The Dow closed lower for the day and the Nasdaq higher, a split that held from the opening bell to the close. San Francisco’s Federal Reserve president, speaking after the payrolls, said the central bank will probably have to tighten further. Europe, which had no payrolls report of its own, closed down about one percent. In the count, Mississippi’s Democratic lead stands near 2,000 votes in the afternoon tallies, Alaska’s stands unchanged at 637 with its next update set for Tuesday, and Colorado’s 4th District stands wider, at 3,278 votes in the state’s afternoon figures. No race was called. On Iran, no decision was announced. On Ukraine, the Kremlin’s answer stands at no details, and Kyiv’s president asked for guarantees clear on paper. The desk files those in that order.

The Markets at the Close: A Bounce, Not a Recovery

New York closed in the split it opened in. The week’s closing account, in the market recap of November 2 to 6 filed below, puts Friday’s closes at 7,376.86 for the S&P 500, up 0.3 percent; 24,579.42 for the Nasdaq Composite, up 1.1 percent; and 51,285.73 for the Dow Jones Industrial Average, down 0.5 percent. The post-market scorecard carries the same Dow close, 51,285.73, down 0.46 percent, with the Nasdaq 100 up 1.36 percent and the volatility index down 9 percent to 25.64. A second closing account, carrying Investing.com’s figures, runs the day a shade stronger—the Dow down 0.26 percent, the S&P 500 up 0.65 percent, the Nasdaq up 1.46 percent, and the volatility index down 14.25 percent to 24.14. The two accounts differ in the last digits and agree on the shape: technology and the broad index up, the Dow down, fear down from Thursday’s close of 28.09 and still above every level of the week before the election.

The chips recovered part of Thursday, and the part is stated plainly. The semiconductor index advanced 4.2 percent on Friday after falling more than 12 percent on Thursday, per the rebound account filed below, which puts the Nasdaq Composite up 1.71 percent at its hour. Advanced Micro Devices led the recovery: up 11.0 percent at the close in the market data filed below, after a fall of 17.3 percent on Thursday, and up about 8 percent in the morning account in the chip file, which has Intel up 5 percent and Micron up 8 percent at its hour. Nvidia closed up 2.8 percent in the same market data, after rising about 6 percent in early trading and falling 9.3 percent on Thursday. No account on the shelf describes Friday as a recovery of the week. The week’s totals in the recap are the standing measure: the S&P 500 down 2.7 percent, the Nasdaq Composite down 3.4 percent, and the Dow down 0.9 percent—stocks’ worst week since June, in that account’s words, made of a Wednesday rally, a Thursday rout that erased it, and a Friday bounce that recovered a fraction of the rout.

Europe closed lower, and its close is the day’s plainest contrast with New York. The pan-European STOXX 600 closed down 1 percent at 648.33, with insurance the leading faller, down 2.6 percent, and travel the only rising sector, up 0.9 percent, per Reuters’ closing file. The closing wrap puts the national closes in a row: Germany’s DAX down 1.92 percent at 25,820.37, France’s CAC 40 down 0.81 percent at 8,139.01, Britain’s FTSE 100 down 1.01 percent at 10,673.93, Spain’s IBEX down 0.60 percent, and Italy’s FTSE MIB down 0.90 percent. Teleperformance closed down 15.3 percent in the Reuters file, after cutting its revenue outlook—the same name that led the midday fall at 18 percent. Europe fell on the day the American technology shares rose. The midday edition printed the shelter argument, that Europe’s smaller share of the AI trade cushions it; at the close the cushion held in degree, as it held at noon, and the direction was down in every market on the continent’s list.

The Rate File at Evening: More Tightening Is Likely, in One President’s Expectation

The afternoon’s new voice on rates is San Francisco’s, and she spoke after the payrolls. Mary Daly, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, said on Friday that the central bank will probably have to tighten policy further. “My expectation is we’re going to have to tighten policy further,” she said in a television interview carried in the account of her remarks filed below, adding that exactly when and how much is still to be decided. She called the payrolls report good news for American workers, said she favors a gradual approach, and said she is prepared to act if the economy calls for it. Reuters’ account of the same interview adds her description of the week’s technology repricing as painful but orderly. Ms. Daly does not vote on the rate-setting committee this year; she votes next year. Her sentence is filed as her expectation, stated on the day the labor report beat every forecast on the shelf.

The Midday File — The Desk’s Report

Objective

Payrolls Triple the Forecast, the Chips Bounce, and Mississippi Widens

At noon the morning has corrected the week in three places. The October payrolls report, the test the Overnight Watch left at 0830, came in at 165,000 jobs against a consensus of 59,000, with unemployment down to 4.1 percent and the two prior months revised up by 84,000. New York opened split and an hour in the chips were bouncing and the Dow was not. And the count corrected itself in the open: Mississippi’s margin is wider in the newest tally, Colorado’s 4th stands at 3,069 in the wire’s latest totals, and no race was called in the morning.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1200 ET, Friday, November 6, 2026. This edition continues Edition No. 10, the Overnight Watch, collected at 0400. Where the noon figures differ from the overnight figures, the difference is stated here, in the open; the overnight edition stands unrevised, as a log stands.

The Noon in Brief

The morning’s largest settled fact is a labor report, not a market move. Employers added 165,000 jobs in October, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported at 0830, against forecasts of 59,000 in the Dow Jones consensus and 65,000 in the Reuters poll. The unemployment rate fell to 4.1 percent from 4.2. September’s gain, first printed at 29,000 in every edition of this paper since it was released, is revised to 60,000, and August’s to 186,000. The correction is filed where corrections belong, at the top of the next edition. Around that fact the morning arranged itself: the Nasdaq bounced on chip buying and the Dow did not follow; Europe fell at its midday by more than the overnight futures had promised; Brent seesawed across $100 and back under it; gold fell for a second session; the odds of a December rate increase rose from about two in three to better than three in four; and in Washington and Moscow the official statements of the morning were, in order, a deadline reached in the House Republican leadership race and a spokesman saying there are no details.

The Payrolls: 165,000, and the Record Corrected Upward

The report beat the highest forecast on the shelf, not only the consensus. CNN’s account of the release and USA Today’s account carry the same headline figures: 165,000 jobs added in October, unemployment at 4.1 percent, and combined upward revisions of 84,000 to August and September—August up 53,000 to 186,000, September up 31,000 to 60,000. The release file adds the detail under the headline: private payrolls up 159,000 against 64,000 expected, and the unrounded unemployment rate at 4.141 percent against 4.1753 percent the month before. The three-month average gain now stands at 137,000. The September figure this paper carried at 29,000 for five weeks was the bureau’s first estimate; the bureau’s second estimate is 60,000, and the log records the change rather than quietly replacing it.

The hiring was led by the services that employ the most people. Professional and business services added 70,000 jobs, against a prior-year average of 15,000 a month in the accounts; health care added 42,000, transportation and warehousing 25,000, and manufacturing 19,000, per the market account carrying the report’s breakdown. Average hourly earnings rose 14 cents, or 0.4 percent, to $38.00, and 3.1 percent over the year—the strongest monthly wage gain since January in one account, and above the 0.3 percent and 2.9 percent the forecasts had carried. The labor force grew by 421,000 and participation rose to 61.9 percent from 61.8. Two figures in the same report run the other way and are printed with the rest: the number working part time for economic reasons rose by 339,000 to 4.8 million, and long-term unemployment stands at 2.0 million, 27.6 percent of all unemployed, up from about 1.1 million in 2023. The private ADP report, already in at 86,000, which the overnight edition printed as the morning’s advance bearing, pointed the right way and understated the official figure by half.

The consumers in the same morning’s second report are less cheerful than the payrolls, and the survey predates the election. The University of Michigan’s preliminary November sentiment index rose to 51.0 from October’s final 48.0, against expectations of 47.5 to 48, per Dow Jones Newswires’ account: current conditions at 51.0, up from 46.7, expectations at 50.9, up from 48.8, and one-year inflation expectations down to 4.3 percent from 4.7. The interviews ran from October 20 to November 2 and closed before Tuesday’s results were known; the final November reading, with post-election interviews in it, comes later in the month. The index stands near its record low of 44.8, set in May. The two reports together are the morning’s economic whole: the hiring is stronger than anyone forecast, and the households being hired remain, by their own account, gloomy.

How to Read This Paper

We aggregate and analyze. The inputs come in over the desk—wires, state outlets, partisans, journals—and the desk writes its report from them without spin; spin is what gets added downstream of honest reports, and this paper is upstream. One stamp is the desk’s own work: objective. Every other item carries the other stamp: subjective — a source with a position, printed because it is useful, and worth exactly what you decide it is worth. No source is excluded by ownership.
