The Talks Open in Abu Dhabi, Asia Extends the Rally, and China Passes a Trillion

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0400 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026 · Edition No. 28, The Overnight Watch

The night opened one table, extended one rally, and produced one very large number, and it settled no war, no coalition and no count. In Abu Dhabi, negotiators from Russia, Ukraine and the United States sat down Tuesday for their first three-way meeting since February, two days on security guarantees, in the Associated Press account filed below; European officials are in the city for separate meetings and not at the table, and in Paris the G7 ministers spent Monday telling Secretary Rubio that Europe belongs at it. Tuesday holds the rest of the week’s appointments: Secretary Hegseth at the Pentagon at 0800, the inflation report at 0830, Alaska’s next count, Mississippi’s deadline, and the second day in both Paris and Abu Dhabi.

Nuclear Power in 2026: The Operating Record, the Cost of Building New, the Small Reactors, and the Data Centers

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 0815 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026

The machine first, because everything else in this file is downstream of it. A nuclear reactor is basically a steam engine—with atoms instead of coal. Fission is only a heat source: it boils water, the steam turns a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator, in the same Rankine cycle a coal plant runs. Because the water in a light-water reactor must stay liquid under pressure, its steam is cooler than a fossil plant’s, and the fleet converts about a third of its heat to electricity—32.7 percent on the Energy Information Administration’s 2024 heat-rate table, against about 44 percent for the gas fleet and about 32 percent for coal on the same instrument. The governing hazard is not the chain reaction, which stops when control rods go in; it is decay heat, the roughly 6 to 7 percent of full power that fission products keep generating after shutdown, still about 1 percent hours later. Every serious accident in the record is, at its physical core, a failure to remove that residual heat. The rest of this report—the fleet, the costs, the small reactors, the waste—is the record of what that machine has done in seventy years of commercial operation.

The Nuclear Taboo: Eighty-One Years of Non-Use, the Threats and Tests of 2025–2026, and the Treaties That No Longer Bind

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 0742 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026

The behavior is not in dispute; everything else about the taboo is. No state has used a nuclear weapon in war since August 9, 1945, and that record includes nuclear states fighting costly and losing wars against non-nuclear adversaries—the United States in Vietnam, the Soviet Union in Afghanistan. What holds the record in place is the contested part. Nina Tannenwald, whose 1999 article and 2007 book gave the prohibition its scholarly name, argues a normative taboo: a de facto prohibition against the first use of nuclear weapons, built by stigma. Her critics answer with weaker instruments—a tradition of non-use that rests on effects and reputation and can be altered, in T. V. Paul’s formulation, if material and political circumstances compel; a public aversion that survey experiments find to be weak and conditional; a norm its own defenders describe as under pressure. No researcher the desk found argues, on measurement, that the taboo is strengthening. The strongest claims for its health are behavioral: the weapons remain unused, including through the two most dangerous years of the record since 1962, and the cause of that non-use is unattributed.

Pete Hegseth Impeachment: Thomas Massie Files Eight Articles and Forces a House Vote

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1549 ET, Monday, November 9, 2026

Representative Thomas Massie of Kentucky, a Republican, stood on the House floor on Monday afternoon, November 9, 2026, read a 34-page resolution, and refiled eight articles of impeachment against Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth as a privileged resolution, and that single filing sets the week: House leaders must act within two legislative days, a vote on the articles themselves, on a motion to table them, or on a motion to refer them to committee is expected by Wednesday, and a simple majority of the House would impeach the secretary. The articles charge high crimes and misdemeanors. Three of them allege violations of the War Powers Resolution of 1973 in the war with Iran: waging the war with no declaration of war, no statutory authorization, no attack on the United States, and no imminent threat; continuing it after both houses of Congress directed the removal of United States forces in House Concurrent Resolution 86; and continuing hostilities past the resolution’s 60-day termination period and its further 30-day withdrawal period, a span Massie’s office states at more than 90 days. The remaining five allege ignoring the laws that minimize civilian casualties, extrajudicial killings in the lethal strikes on suspected drug-trafficking boats, retaliation against Senator Mark Kelly of Arizona for protected speech, the seizure of Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores in Operation ABSOLUTE RESOLVE, and an unlawful war in Yemen in Operation ROUGH RIDER. The answers stand on the record beside the charges. Hegseth said on Monday, “We’re not worried about that,” and announced a Pentagon press conference for Tuesday, his first there since May. President Donald Trump has backed his secretary in public, in an Oval Office remark and in a “60 Minutes” interview broadcast on Sunday. Speaker Mike Johnson opposes impeachment while hostilities continue. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said on Sunday that he is “a hard yes.” Massie first filed the same eight articles on September 15; the House left for its campaign recess the next day, the notice expired, and Monday’s filing restarts the clock in a lame-duck House that Republicans still control until January, one week after midterm elections in which they lost both chambers. If the House impeaches Hegseth, he becomes the third Cabinet secretary impeached in the history of the republic, after William Belknap in 1876 and Alejandro Mayorkas in 2024, and conviction and removal would then require two-thirds of the Senate, where Republicans hold 53 of 100 seats. The articles are allegations in a charging document filed by one member of the House. They are findings by no court, no tribunal, and no inspector general, and the desk files them at that grade, with every answer the record holds.
