Wall Street Buys the Morning, the Autopsies Begin in Kraków, and the Mediator Goes In at Noon

The news at midday: the morning’s file corrected itself in three places before noon, and the corrections are the lead. Kraków’s wounded now stand in a governor’s hospital count, not in a weekend tally. Alaska’s next count stands on Tuesday, not Monday. Asia’s rebound closed smaller than its morning print, and Seoul closed down. Against the corrections, the day’s two scheduled acts ran on time: Mr. Miller took the chief of staff’s office in Washington, and the G7 ministers sat in Paris. At noon exactly, the mediator went into the Oval Office. What he carries out is not yet in any file. Wall Street, handed the same record, bought it: the Dow up 540 points at the open and oil down five percent.

In Kraków, Governor Krzysztof Klęczar told reporters that fifteen people injured in the attack remain in hospitals, fourteen of them in stable condition and one, a police officer who took part in the arrest, in serious condition, in Reuters’ Monday file filed below; the overnight edition’s figure was seventeen injured in the prosecutors’ morning count, and both are printed in the Cocked Hat. The Corrections at Noon: Three Morning Figures, Restated

The first correction is a hospital count. The weekend’s Kraków wounded ran fifteen, sixteen and seventeen in successive files, the seventeen a prosecutors’ count carried in Monday’s morning accounts. The midday figure is of a different kind: fifteen people remain in hospitals, the governor of the province said at a Monday press conference, fourteen stable and one officer serious. A count of the injured and a count of those still in hospital are not the same instrument, and the desk prints the governor’s figure as the midday state of the hospitals without striking the earlier tallies, which counted the wounded at other hours. On the two officers the accounts still divide: one in a coma in one Monday account, one in serious condition in the governor’s words, the second officer’s state given as regained consciousness in Sunday’s file and not restated at midday.

The second correction is a date. The overnight edition carried Alaska’s next count as due Monday or Tuesday. The count guide checked Monday morning against the Division of Elections puts the next scheduled count on Tuesday, November 10, carrying early votes cast October 30 to November 2 and questioned ballots, in the count guide filed below. Nothing moves in Alaska today. The ranked-choice tabulation stands on November 18.

The third correction is a close. Tokyo’s morning print, up 3.44 percent at 59,411 in the overnight file, closed at 58,775.50, up 2.33 percent, in the Asia close account filed below. Seoul’s morning gain, up between 1.4 and 1.8 percent in the overnight accounts, closed down 0.81 percent at 5,018.85. The morning’s rebound was real at the morning hour and smaller at the close; both prints stand, each under its hour.

Kraków at 0400: The Suspect Is Dead, and the Questions Stay With the Living

Poland’s second day of national mourning began with an announcement from the police who shot the man. “We would like to inform you that the perpetrator of the tragic events died in hospital early this morning,” Małopolska police wrote on X, in the accounts filed below. The man, 24, named in Polish media as Michał O., had been in critical condition since Saturday, when officers shot him within minutes of the first shots in the Main Market Square. He had not been formally arrested and had not been questioned; prosecutors had said both waited on his medical condition. That waiting is now permanent. The investigation the national prosecutor’s office opened as a terrorist offence covers two acts, the killing of six people in the square with the attempted killing of many others including police officers, and the killing of the three at the home; with the suspect dead, its work is the work the overnight accounts list: the full sequence, the source of the weapons, the meaning of the emblems, and whether anyone else took part in planning.

The morning’s new evidence is clothing and guns, and both arrive attributed and unfinished. Interior ministry spokeswoman Karolina Galecka told TOK FM radio on Monday that the man’s clothes carried many different emblems, partly examined and interpreted, the patches associated with various motifs that specialists are still analysing; she would not say more while the investigation runs. The minister’s Saturday reading stands where it stood: fascination with far-right ideology and very radical, ultra-nationalistic views, in the interior minister’s words, and a reading is what it remains. On the weapons, one Monday account counts 11 firearms recovered in all, four from the man or his car in Kraków and seven from the family home, in the victims and firearms account filed below; Polish media report he held a firearms permit and may have obtained guns from the home, and police have not confirmed that account. The wounded stand at 17 injured in the prosecutors’ count carried in the morning accounts, against 15 and 16 in the weekend’s files, with the two officers’ conditions divided in the accounts between regained consciousness in the evening file and one officer in a coma in the Monday account; both are printed in the Cocked Hat. The six of the square stand as the midnight edition named them by age and nationality, and the three at home stand at father 53, mother 52 and sister 21 in the Sunday confirmation the morning accounts repeat. Kraków’s own mourning runs through November 11; the national mourning ends Monday night.

Organ Donation in America: Who Gets the Organ, the Law, the Black Market, and What Changed

On Sunday night a network drama put a liver, a computer, and a rich man in one operating room, and on Monday morning the country is asking how an organ is actually assigned. The record answers plainly: one national list, an order set by published medical formulas, no hospital algorithm of its own, and no judge who has ever ordered an organ to a named patient. What moves a person up the list is documented and narrower—an insurance clearance before the list, a center’s quiet power to refuse an organ in a patient’s name, exception points from review boards, and, for those who can pay for duplicate evaluations and a fast flight, a second and third listing. Around that machinery stand the law that makes selling an organ a federal crime, a waiting list of 109,817 people, a black market abroad sized once, in 2007, and never again, and a quarter-century of invention that has changed what an organ is. The desk files the whole record, attributed and graded.

Genocide and Conquest: The Definition, the Intent Test, and Where the Courts Drew the Line

“Genocide” is a word with a birth certificate: coined in 1944, defined by treaty in 1948 as five acts committed with intent to destroy a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part, as such, and applied by courts only where that intent is proven. “Conquest” is the older word: it names the transfer of peoples and territory by force, and it carries no legal test at all. The desk files the coinage, the Convention, the older record the word was carried back to, and the judgments.

Deportation in America: Visa Overstays, Removal Orders, and How the Law Actually Works

Deportation in the United States is an administrative process, not a criminal sentence: being present without status is a civil violation, while entering improperly and reentering after removal are federal crimes, and the system runs on that seam. Its caseload is majority non-criminal in the recent record—two-thirds of fiscal 2024 removals involved no criminal conviction or pending charge—its court backlog stands above three million cases, more than 1.4 million people carry final removal orders unenforced, and the statute that governs it asks nothing about whether the person removed is good. The desk files the law, the ladder, the counts, and the exposure, in that order.

The 24-year-old Polish national died at about 4:30 a.m. on November 9, in critical condition since officers shot him, having undergone at least three operations, regional governor Krzysztof Jan Klęczar said. Identified in Polish media only as Michał O., the suspect was a member of a sport shooting club. President Karol Nawrocki declared two days of national mourning for November 8 and 9, and Kraków’s mayor extended the city’s own mourning until November 11.

The Daily Lens — the Kraków shooting suspect dies as the terror inquiry continues

The man suspected of shooting dead six people in central Kraków on Saturday has died in hospital, Polish police said on Monday, bringing the death toll from the attack and its aftermath to ten. With the suspect dead, the criminal case against him cannot proceed to trial, although the inquiry continues. The square reopened to the public within 24 hours, with bullet marks still visible on shop and cafe windows, and national mourning covered Sunday and Monday.

Zark News — the Kraków gunman dies in hospital as the terror probe widens to the family killings

The gunman who killed six people in Kraków’s Main Market Square on Saturday has died in hospital, Polish police said early on Monday, as prosecutors widened their investigation to the killing of three members of his own family. Justice Minister Waldemar Żurek said the gunman acted with the aim of seriously intimidating a large number of people, making this a terrorist offence, in the minister’s words. Polish media have named him as Michał Oksiński; police have not officially released his name.
