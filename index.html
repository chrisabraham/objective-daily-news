The Intermediaries: The Paid Economy Between Americans and Their Benefits

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1500 ET, Friday, November 27, 2026

Between an American and a federal benefit there usually stands a paid intermediary. The disability claimant hires a representative whose fee the Social Security Administration itself withholds from back pay and mails to the representative. The health insurance shopper is enrolled, in most cases, by a broker whose commission the insurer pays. The homeless man who walks into a church kitchen for breakfast meets a case manager whose salary a federal grant pays, whose work is to connect him to the benefits. Its prices are set in statutes and regulations, its largest payment rail is operated by the government itself, and its scale is printed in the government’s own reports: in the five calendar years 2018 through 2022, the Social Security Administration paid claimant representatives about $6.5 billion out of past-due benefits. This report files the record of the layer in its parts: the fee, the firms, the market in claimants, the fraud case, the marketplace’s two doors, the telephone program, and the kitchen. The desk draws no conclusion from it.

SNAP Twenty-Seven Days After November 1: The Beverage Restrictions, the Expanded Work Requirements, and the Proposed End of Broad-Based Categorical Eligibility

On November 1, 2026, four more states—Montana, North Dakota, South Carolina, and Virginia—began refusing SNAP benefits for soda and other sweetened drinks at the register, bringing to twelve the number of states where the restrictions are in force. The refusals are the visible edge of a larger contraction. Behind them stand the expanded work requirements of the reconciliation law signed on July 4, 2025, which states have been applying at recertification through this year, and a proposed rule published on November 10, 2026, under which the Department of Agriculture’s own analysis projects that 6.3 million people would lose benefits entirely. The Emptying of the Asylums and the Two Memories of Ronald Reagan

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 0900 ET, Friday, November 27, 2026

The emptying of the American state psychiatric hospitals is filed in the public record under two verdicts, and the verdicts do not share a page. In one filing, the releases were a liberation: the restoration of legal personhood to people held indefinitely without crime, charge, or sentence. In the other, the releases were an abandonment: the discharge of people with severe mental illness into communities whose promised services were never built, from which a share of them reached the shelters, the streets, and the jails. Both filings cite the same statutes, the same court decisions, and the same census. This report files the chronology the two verdicts share—the laws, the cases, the money, and the counts—in the order it ran. A companion report filed today, The Bed Census, prints the bed and prison counts in full; this report is confined to the releases and to what the record says followed them.
