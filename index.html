Donald J. Trump: The Documented Record, 1946 to 2026

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1150 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026

The man first, because everything else in this file is downstream of him. Donald John Trump was born on June 14, 1946, in Jamaica Hospital, Queens, the fourth of five children of Fred Trump, a builder of middle-income housing in Brooklyn and Queens, and Mary Anne MacLeod Trump, a Scottish immigrant who arrived in New York in 1930 with a domestic worker’s visa. He was sent at thirteen to the New York Military Academy, attended Fordham University for two years, and took a bachelor’s degree in economics from the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania in 1968. He never served in the armed forces: the Selective Service record documents four student deferments, a medical classification of 1-Y in October 1968 attributed by Trump to bone spurs, a draft-lottery number, 356, too high to be called, and a permanent 4-F classification in 1972. The diagnosis behind the medical classification is not documented; the reporting on it is contested; both facts are printed in section 3. He took the presidency of his father’s company in 1971, moved its ambitions from the outer boroughs to Manhattan, and built the public identity—developer, casino operator, television host, licensor of his own name—on which, in 2015, he opened a campaign for the presidency. He has been married three times and has five children. He is, as this report files, the sitting President of the United States, twenty-two months into a second term that the midterm elections of November 3, 2026, have just answered with a Congress of the opposite party.

Nuclear Power in 2026: The Operating Record, the Cost of Building New, the Small Reactors, and the Data Centers

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 0815 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026

The machine first, because everything else in this file is downstream of it. A nuclear reactor is basically a steam engine—with atoms instead of coal. Fission is only a heat source: it boils water, the steam turns a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator, in the same Rankine cycle a coal plant runs. Because the water in a light-water reactor must stay liquid under pressure, its steam is cooler than a fossil plant’s, and the fleet converts about a third of its heat to electricity—32.7 percent on the Energy Information Administration’s 2024 heat-rate table, against about 44 percent for the gas fleet and about 32 percent for coal on the same instrument. The governing hazard is not the chain reaction, which stops when control rods go in; it is decay heat, the roughly 6 to 7 percent of full power that fission products keep generating after shutdown, still about 1 percent hours later. Every serious accident in the record is, at its physical core, a failure to remove that residual heat. The rest of this report—the fleet, the costs, the small reactors, the waste—is the record of what that machine has done in seventy years of commercial operation.

The Nuclear Taboo: Eighty-One Years of Non-Use, the Threats and Tests of 2025–2026, and the Treaties That No Longer Bind

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 0742 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026

The behavior is not in dispute; everything else about the taboo is. No state has used a nuclear weapon in war since August 9, 1945, and that record includes nuclear states fighting costly and losing wars against non-nuclear adversaries—the United States in Vietnam, the Soviet Union in Afghanistan. What holds the record in place is the contested part. Nina Tannenwald, whose 1999 article and 2007 book gave the prohibition its scholarly name, argues a normative taboo: a de facto prohibition against the first use of nuclear weapons, built by stigma. Her critics answer with weaker instruments—a tradition of non-use that rests on effects and reputation and can be altered, in T. V. Paul’s formulation, if material and political circumstances compel; a public aversion that survey experiments find to be weak and conditional; a norm its own defenders describe as under pressure. No researcher the desk found argues, on measurement, that the taboo is strengthening. The strongest claims for its health are behavioral: the weapons remain unused, including through the two most dangerous years of the record since 1962, and the cause of that non-use is unattributed.
