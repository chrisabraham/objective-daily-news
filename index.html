The Evening in Brief

Monday ended with its meetings held, its markets closed, its one new legal sentence imposed in London, its one new parliamentary clock started in Washington, and none of its open questions answered. In Washington, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, Qatar’s prime minister and foreign minister, met President Trump in the Oval Office at noon and Vice President Vance separately, and by 1800 no readout had been issued and no agreement announced, in the afternoon account and the evening account filed below. Tehran spoke for itself the same day: President Masoud Pezeshkian said Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei had instructed officials to end the state of “neither war nor peace,” that Iran could negotiate with dignity, and that it would not surrender to force, in the Tehran account filed below, and Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei defended Tehran’s two-day proposal as the most reasonable offer on the table. In the House, Representative Thomas Massie refiled eight articles of impeachment against Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth as a privileged resolution; House leaders have two legislative days to act. In New York the Dow closed up more than eleven hundred points on the Associated Press print and the S&P 500 had its best day since April, as Brent crude fell 5.8 percent to $92.57. In Kraków the National Prosecutor’s Office spokesman, Przemysław Nowak, said investigators could not yet link the dead gunman responsibly to any ideology, in the evening brief filed below. In Jerusalem Mr. Eisenkot and Mr. Liberman agreed the next speaker of the Knesset will be a member of Yashar, in the speaker account filed below. In London Mr. Justice Bennathan sentenced Russell Brand to ten years and six months in prison. The desk files the day in that order.

Washington at Evening: The Mediator Leaves No Word, and the House Gets a Clock

The noon meeting produced, by evening, a fact of attendance and nothing else. The Qatari prime minister met the president behind closed doors and the vice president separately, having visited Tehran last week; the White House released no readout, and Fox News reported it had sought comment and received no response before publication. The terms on the table stand where the midday edition printed them: an end to the American blockade of Iran’s ports, the lifting of some sanctions and a halt to bombing, in return for Tehran stopping its own closure of the Strait of Hormuz, with nuclear talks to follow—a plan Washington has repeatedly rejected, and which Secretary Rubio, in Paris for the G7, again said was not near a breakthrough, its demands in part unacceptable, outrageous and not realistic. Tehran’s considered response arrived Monday in two voices. Mr. Baghaei defended the plan. President Pezeshkian, at a ceremony in Tehran, gave the day its fullest Iranian sentence: the Leader had ordered an end to the neither-war-nor-peace state; “we can negotiate with dignity and resolve the issue with dignity”; and Iran would not surrender in the face of force, intimidation or bullying. The president’s own Sunday sentence stands unwithdrawn beside them: fresh attacks are highly unlikely if talks produce a strong agreement. No side moved a term on Monday. The mediation moved a man between capitals, and the record of the day is the movement and the statements, not a settlement.

Genocide and Conquest: The Definition, the Intent Test, and Where the Courts Drew the Line

“Genocide” is a word with a birth certificate: coined in 1944, defined by treaty in 1948 as five acts committed with intent to destroy a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part, as such, and applied by courts only where that intent is proven. “Conquest” is the older word: it names the transfer of peoples and territory by force, and it carries no legal test at all. The desk files the coinage, the Convention, the older record the word was carried back to, and the judgments.

Deportation in America: Visa Overstays, Removal Orders, and How the Law Actually Works

Deportation in the United States is an administrative process, not a criminal sentence: being present without status is a civil violation, while entering improperly and reentering after removal are federal crimes, and the system runs on that seam. Its caseload is majority non-criminal in the recent record—two-thirds of fiscal 2024 removals involved no criminal conviction or pending charge—its court backlog stands above three million cases, more than 1.4 million people carry final removal orders unenforced, and the statute that governs it asks nothing about whether the person removed is good.
