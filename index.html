Presidential Reversal: What the Record Shows About Each Administration Undoing the Last

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1200 ET, Saturday, November 14, 2026

Reversal is the standing first act of American government, and it is conducted in paperwork. Since 1981, every president who has taken office from the opposing party has begun his administration by undoing pieces of his predecessor's work, and the undoing runs through four instruments whose texts survive: the executive order that revokes earlier executive orders by number; the regulatory freeze memorandum that stops the pipeline of unfinished rules on the first day; the Congressional Review Act resolution by which Congress and the new president together void a finished rule and bar its return in substantially the same form; and the withdrawal or postponement of rules still pending when the government changes hands. The American Presidency Project's count of the first hundred days shows the volume rising across the modern transitions: five prior executive orders revoked in 2001, nine in 2009, thirteen in 2017, and sixty-seven in 2021, when half of the new president's executive orders carried a revocation inside them. In January 2025 the bulk rescission became a document of its own: Executive Order 14148, signed on the first day, revoked seventy-eight orders and memoranda of the previous administration in a single list, and a second order eight weeks later revoked eighteen more. Congress's instrument has followed the same curve: the Congressional Research Service counts twenty successful disapprovals in the Act's first twenty-eight years—one in 2001, sixteen in 2017–2018, three in 2021—and the trackers of the present Congress count twenty-three more since January 2025. Each era's documents state the era's reasons in the era's vocabulary, and this report prints them from the documents. The elections held in the years of high inflation stand in the same file, and the desk prints those numbers beside the reversal counts, drawing no line between them. The record closes with the forward state of the instruments: reversal is now announced before inaugurations, the current administration continued rescinding through the autumn of 2025, and the statute's own carryover provision has already loaded the next Congress's window with the present administration's late rules.

Free Speech: What the Record Shows About What the First Amendment Protects, and What Other Democracies Do Instead

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1055 ET, Saturday, November 14, 2026

Two documents describe the American settlement on speech, and both are short. The first is the First Amendment, ratified December 15, 1791: “Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press.” The second is the sentence with which Justice Alito closed the Supreme Court's unanimous judgment in Matal v. Tam in 2017: “Speech that demeans on the basis of race, ethnicity, gender, religion, age, disability, or any other similar ground is hateful; but the proudest boast of our free speech jurisprudence is that we protect the freedom to express ‘the thought that we hate.’” Between the text and that sentence stands the whole of the record this report prints: what the amendment forbids, what it permits, the narrow categories it leaves outside, the line it draws at the government's own hand—including the hand applied indirectly, through threats, through pressure on private platforms, and through money—and the contrasting settlements of the other democracies, in which expression is a qualified right, balanced in the text itself against dignity, reputation, and public order, and enforced against posts, memes, and T-shirts by arrest and imprisonment. The comparison is between two legal architectures: one in which the limit sits outside the right, in a short list of harms the courts police case by case, and one in which the limit is written into the right's own text, where legislatures and police apply it in advance.

Race and Ethnicity: What the Record Shows About the Government's Classification of Americans

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1000 ET, Saturday, November 14, 2026

The United States government sorts its residents into racial and ethnic categories of its own design, and it revises the design on its own schedule. Under the federal standard issued in 1977 and reaffirmed in 1997, a person having origins in the Middle East or North Africa is White, and has been White in federal statistics for nearly fifty years; more than 3.5 million people reported Middle Eastern or North African descent inside the White category in the 2020 census. A revision of the standard, published in the Federal Register on March 28, 2024, redraws the map: seven minimum categories where the old standard kept five races and a separate ethnicity, one combined question where the old standard asked two, Middle Eastern or North African standing as a category of its own, detailed origins collected by default, full compliance due by 2029, and the 2030 census as the first count under the new design. Implementation has since slipped twice, the Census Bureau has withdrawn its plan to introduce the new questions on its largest survey in 2027, and a rule proposed on September 10, 2026 would bar race and ethnicity questions from the decennial census altogether. The people being classified account for none of these changes. The record behind the current filings runs through two centuries of the same operation: a naturalization statute that admitted “free white persons” and left the courts to decide who they were, applicant by applicant; a federal dictionary of 1911 that counted South Italians as a race apart from North Italians; a Virginia registrar who reclassified his state's Indian families as colored by circular and surname list; a one-drop rule written into a state statute and into census instructions; and, abroad, a South African classification office that had reclassified 18,469 people by 1956 and a French republic whose law forbids the questions entirely. Every version of the system arrived carrying a justification in its era's moral vocabulary. The justifications are part of the record, and this report prints them.
