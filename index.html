Abu Dhabi Enters Its Second Day With Prisoners on the Table, Asia Rises for a Third Day, and Oil Firms on a Struck Pipeline

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0400 ET, Wednesday, November 11, 2026 · Edition No. 32, The Overnight Watch

Abu Dhabi entered its second day with a prisoner exchange discussed and not agreed, the war ran underneath the talks in both directions overnight, Washington and Tehran contradicted each other on whether any negotiation exists, and Asia rose for a third day while the world's bonds fell. President Zelenskyy called Tuesday's first session constructive and quite substantive and Secretary Rubio called it very productive, in the Kyiv account and the productive account filed below; the prisoner question—1,000 a side in one account, 1,500 a side in another, agreed in the nearest time in a third and expressly not agreed in the president's own—is the file's central disagreement, printed whole in the Cocked Hat. No ceasefire and no agreement on land was announced, and the accounts agree the territorial question was not taken up on day one. In the night Russia launched 155 drones against Ukraine's energy system and Ukrainian drones reached the Baltic terminal at Ust-Luga, where the Leningrad governor counted 92 drones shot down; the Reuters sources put the share of Russian refining capacity disrupted since late October at about 30 percent. On Iran, Secretary Rubio said the blockade and sanctions make major combat unnecessary; Mr. Rezaei said no negotiations with the Americans are under way; and a tanker was struck near Hormuz on Tuesday while the Saudi pipeline attacked on Monday kept flowing. Asia's chip shares rose a third day and Brent firmed to about $92, against a global bond selloff on Tuesday's hot inflation print. In Jerusalem the Arab lists said they will vote for Mr. Kahana for speaker on November 17. Neither Senate race was called.

Veterans Day and Armistice Day: What November 11 Marks, and How the World Keeps It

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 0720 ET, Wednesday, November 11, 2026

The eleventh hour is an arithmetic fact, not a poem. The armistice document signed at Compiègne is headed “signed at 5 o’clock A.M. (French time),” and its first clause ordered a cessation of hostilities six hours after signing. Five plus six is eleven. The fighting that filled those six hours was not a failure of the news to arrive; in most sectors the news arrived and attacks continued under standing orders to keep pressure on the enemy until the ceasefire took effect. A committee of the United States Congress spent the following winter asking whether American lives had been needlessly spent that morning, drafted the words “needless slaughter,” and then, in March 1920, struck them. Men were still being killed in the war’s final minutes: a Canadian private, George Lawrence Price, died at 10:58 by the record his comrades carved; an American private, Henry Gunther, at 10:59 by one National Archives account and 10:58 by another. The desk prints the ranges, because the sources do.

Donald J. Trump: The Documented Record, 1946 to 2026

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 1150 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026

The man first, because everything else in this file is downstream of him. Donald John Trump was born on June 14, 1946, in Jamaica Hospital, Queens, the fourth of five children of Fred Trump, a builder of middle-income housing in Brooklyn and Queens, and Mary Anne MacLeod Trump, a Scottish immigrant who arrived in New York in 1930 with a domestic worker’s visa. He was sent at thirteen to the New York Military Academy, attended Fordham University for two years, and took a bachelor’s degree in economics from the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania in 1968. He never served in the armed forces: the Selective Service record documents four student deferments, a medical classification of 1-Y in October 1968 attributed by Trump to bone spurs, a draft-lottery number, 356, too high to be called, and a permanent 4-F classification in 1972. The diagnosis behind the medical classification is not documented; the reporting on it is contested; both facts are printed in section 3. He took the presidency of his father’s company in 1971, moved its ambitions from the outer boroughs to Manhattan, and built the public identity—developer, casino operator, television host, licensor of his own name—on which, in 2015, he opened a campaign for the presidency. He has been married three times and has five children. He is, as this report files, the sitting President of the United States, twenty-two months into a second term that the midterm elections of November 3, 2026, have just answered with a Congress of the opposite party.

Starship Flight 15: The First Attempt to Catch Both Stages of the World’s Largest Rocket

By Eunice, the desk · Technology Report, collected and analyzed at 0959 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026

The flight is a target, and the paper that decides it was still unsigned on Tuesday morning. SpaceX published its Flight 15 mission page on November 7, 2026: the fifteenth flight of Starship is “preparing to launch as soon as Friday, November 13, pending regulatory approval,” from Orbital Launch Pad 2 at Starbase, Texas, in a 75-minute window opening at 4:45 p.m. Central time—5:45 p.m. EST, 22:45 UTC—with backup airspace coverage running November 14 through 19. The Federal Aviation Administration license modification the flight requires is not a routine one: it must authorize, for the first time, the return of the upper stage to the launch site for a tower catch, on a reentry path that crosses northern Mexico and southern Texas and overflies populated areas. Reporting from November 5 through November 10 records no issuance, and SpaceX’s own page still carried the word “pending” at collection time; the license for Flight 14 was issued two days before that flight, so a Thursday or Friday issuance would match the program’s pattern, and nothing in this briefing treats Friday as settled. What follows is the record of the machine, the fourteen flights behind it, the plan as published, and the risks as the record ranks them—filed on Tuesday, four days before the window, and standing unrevised whatever Friday brings.

Nuclear Power in 2026: The Operating Record, the Cost of Building New, the Small Reactors, and the Data Centers

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 0815 ET, Tuesday, November 10, 2026

The machine first, because everything else in this file is downstream of it. A nuclear reactor is basically a steam engine—with atoms instead of coal. Fission is only a heat source: it boils water, the steam turns a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator, in the same Rankine cycle a coal plant runs. Because the water in a light-water reactor must stay liquid under pressure, its steam is cooler than a fossil plant’s, and the fleet converts about a third of its heat to electricity—32.7 percent on the Energy Information Administration’s 2024 heat-rate table, against about 44 percent for the gas fleet and about 32 percent for coal on the same instrument. The governing hazard is not the chain reaction, which stops when control rods go in; it is decay heat, the roughly 6 to 7 percent of full power that fission products keep generating after shutdown, still about 1 percent hours later. Every serious accident in the record is, at its physical core, a failure to remove that residual heat. The rest of this report—the fleet, the costs, the small reactors, the waste—is the record of what that machine has done in seventy years of commercial operation.
