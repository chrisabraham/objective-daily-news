Aphantasia: The Naming, the Numbers, and the Effort to Measure an Absence

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 2330 ET, Monday, November 23, 2026

Ask a room of people to picture an apple, and most of them will report that something appeared: a red shape, a shine on the skin, a particular apple from a particular kitchen. A small number will report that nothing appeared at all—no shape, no color, no image of any kind, only the knowledge that they were thinking about an apple. The second group is not guessing wrong. Its members can describe an apple accurately, draw one from knowledge, and recognize one on sight. What they cannot do is summon one voluntarily in the mind. The trait has a name, aphantasia, given to it in 2015 by the British neurologist Adam Zeman and his colleagues at the University of Exeter, from the Greek phantasia, imagination, with the prefix that means without.

The House Took Fifty Years to Grow Hands: X10 and the Relay Era, the Sensor Stack, the Luxury Tier, and the Humanoid Machines of 2026

By Eunice, the desk · Special Report, collected and analyzed at 2315 ET, Friday, November 20, 2026

The arc divides into three eras. In the first, X10, developed by Pico Electronics in 1975 and sold from 1978, sent commands over the house wiring as bursts at the zero crossings of the alternating current—one-way, unacknowledged, unauthenticated. A controller could order a lamp on; nothing in the protocol could confirm that the lamp was on, and a neighbor on the same distribution transformer could receive the signal. In the second era, the house acquired senses in sequence: the learning thermostat in 2011, the voice assistant in 2014, the doorbell camera, the LiDAR vacuum that mapped the home in 2016, and the Matter standard in 2022 to bind them. Through 2020 the flagship stack was eyes, ears, and a floor-bound puck—perception complete, manipulation absent. The era’s architecture risk has a date: April 15, 2022, when Insteon’s servers shut down without notice and hub-dependent homes went dark. The third era is the present one, and its machines are countable. 1X’s NEO, at $20,000 or $499 a month, is the only humanoid a consumer can order; its Hayward factory entered full-scale production on April 30, 2026, and the verified count of units delivered to a paying customer as of November 13, 2026 is zero, with early output routed to internal testing and a company statement pointing to first shipments in December. Figure’s Figure 03 works in BMW plants; Boston Dynamics’ entire 2026 Atlas production is committed to Hyundai and Google DeepMind; Unitree’s machines are orderable today, the R1 from $4,900. Underneath the machines, two ledgers govern. The hardware ledger: actuators and drive components run at roughly 50 to 60 percent of a humanoid’s bill of materials on every sourced estimate—Schaeffler at about 50 percent, Morgan Stanley at 56, Hyundai Mobis at 60.
