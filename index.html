Deportation in America: Visa Overstays, Removal Orders, and How the Law Actually Works

Deportation in the United States is an administrative process, not a criminal sentence: being present without status is a civil violation, while entering improperly and reentering after removal are federal crimes, and the system runs on that seam. Its caseload is majority non-criminal in the recent record—two-thirds of fiscal 2024 removals involved no criminal conviction or pending charge—its court backlog stands above three million cases, more than 1.4 million people carry final removal orders unenforced, and the statute that governs it asks nothing about whether the person removed is good. The desk files the law, the ladder, the counts, and the exposure, in that order.

Election Integrity in America: Voting, Mail-In Ballots, and the Theft Claims of 2016, 2020, 2024, and 2026

American elections are run by thousands of local offices under fifty state systems, and the documented record of fraud in them is real, old, and small against the count: 1,619 proven cases in the most-cited database across forty-three years and roughly 2.26 billion federal ballots. Four consecutive national elections have now drawn theft claims, from four different directions, and none of the four has produced evidence that changed a certified result. The desk files the machinery, the history, the modern turn, and the four claims, each tested by the same instrument.

The Evening in Brief

In the Gulf, Iranian media reported projectile impacts on Qeshm Island late Sunday and Iranian state television reported three projectiles launched from Sirik toward vessels; in Iraqi Kurdistan a drone attack halted production at the Khor Mor gas field, in the live file filed below. Al-Ayyat at 1800: Seven Dead on the Railway

The day’s new event happened on the railway south of Cairo, and its numbers are a staircase. Train No. 134, running from Giza to Fayoum, struck the rear of Train No. 2010, running from Cairo to Aswan, between Kafr Ammar and Raqqa stations in the Al-Ayyat district of Giza province, the National Authority for Egyptian Railways said; several carriages left the track and some overturned. The chief prosecutor’s office said its initial inspection found the Giza–Fayoum train had hit the last carriage of the Aswan train, which was stationary on the same track, and that a train conductor was among the dead, in the collated account filed below. The Health Ministry’s first count, carried by the wires at midday, was four dead and 88 injured. By the evening the ministry’s toll stood at seven dead and 112 injured, reported by Sky News and Al Jazeera; a further account of the ministry’s figures carries 125 injured, 132 cases received by hospitals in all, 71 of the injured discharged and 43 still in hospital—23 at Al-Ayyat, 13 at Al-Wasta and 7 at Atfih. Seventy-two ambulances took part in the evacuation. The two injured counts, 112 and 125, are both in the record under the ministry’s name and the desk prints both; the dead, at seven, agree in every later account. President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi ordered an investigation and, in his words, that anyone found to have been negligent be held accountable; the prosecutor general sent a team to examine the site, assess the damage and fix the exact toll; railway crews and cranes were dispatched to clear the line. No cause has been stated by any authority. Al-Ayyat carries a record in this file: a fire on a holiday train there in 2002 killed more than 370 people, the worst disaster on Egypt’s railways. The dead of Sunday have not been named.

The Midday in Brief

Sunday at noon is a file of openings and standings, and the desk files it in that order. In Kraków, on the first day of Poland’s national mourning, prosecutors opened the formal investigation the overnight edition left unopened: the Saturday attack is classified as a terrorist offence, the investigation covers two acts—the murder of six people in the Main Market Square and the murder of three people at the suspect’s home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski—and prosecutors are preparing six murder charges that could rise to nine if the same hand is established for the three at home, in the prosecutors’ Sunday statement filed below. The suspect has not been formally arrested, because his condition in hospital does not allow it; he is under constant surveillance, and no motive has been established. In Jerusalem, the mandate the night announced was handed over: President Isaac Herzog gave Gadi Eisenkot the task of forming Israel’s next government at the ceremony set for 5 p.m. local time, 1000 ET, on the strength of 67 recommendations against 46 for Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu; Mr. Eisenkot has 28 days, and no coalition exists. In the Strait of Hormuz, Iran’s Revolutionary Guards raised their own tally in the morning from two tankers burning to three, still naming none of them, and their spokesman dismissed the American Task Force Iron Gate, announced Saturday, as wordplay. In Paris, Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said Europeans will be represented at Tuesday’s talks in Abu Dhabi, which had been presented until Sunday as a trilateral table. The Senate count did not move: Mississippi stands at 2,574 votes and Alaska at 637 in the Sunday-morning tallies, and both counts speak next on Tuesday. The markets are closed in the United States and Europe; the Sunday reading is Friday’s settlements—Brent at $98.32, West Texas Intermediate at $90.83—and India’s one-hour Diwali session, which closed up. That is the whole of the midday record: a case opened, a mandate handed over, a tally raised without names, a table enlarged, a count standing, and a week waiting on Tuesday.

Kraków at 1200: Prosecutors Open the Case

The morning’s completed act in Poland is a legal one. A prosecutor of the Małopolska Branch of the National Prosecutor’s Office for Organised Crime and Corruption has commenced an investigation into Saturday’s attack, classified under terrorist offences, spokesman Tomasz Waszczuk announced, in the statement carried by the Polish news agency and filed below. The investigation covers two distinct acts: the murder of six people with a firearm in the square, with the attempted murder of others including police officers on duty, and the murder of the three people in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski. Prosecutor General Waldemar Żurek noted that the division handles cases related to terrorist crimes and that both acts carry the potential penalty of life imprisonment. Mr. Waszczuk told ABC News that prosecutors are preparing six murder charges, potentially rising to nine if investigators establish that the gunman was also responsible for the deaths of his parents and sister, in the second account filed below. The three at home were found with bullet wounds, the police spokesman said, so it is likely they died of gunshot wounds; likely is the word in the record, and no finding has been made. The suspect himself has not yet been formally arrested, because his medical condition required hospital transport; he remains under constant surveillance and cannot yet be questioned, and prosecutors say that may not be possible for hours or days. One fact of care arrived with the legal file: both injured police officers have regained consciousness, the spokesman confirmed.

The corrections the midday watch exists to print are in this case, and the desk prints them as disagreements, not as choices. The wounded count does not agree across the Sunday files: the prosecutors’ statement carries 16 injured, including the two officers; the morning accounts from the square carry 15. The overnight edition carried both figures in its own files and printed the disagreement; at noon it stands unresolved. The suspect’s age does not agree either: the prosecutors and the police give 24, as this paper has filed him, while several Sunday accounts give 25, and one gives a birth year, 2002, that supports neither cleanly. The age of the sister found dead at home does not agree: the accounts the overnight edition relied on gave 21, following the police confirmation; the prosecutors’ account filed this morning gives 20, for a father of 53 and a mother of 52 on whom all accounts agree. The desk does not round a disputed fact into print; it publishes the triangle. What is settled at noon is the shape of the case: nine dead in one investigation, six in a public square and three in a private house, a terrorist-offence classification, an interior minister’s reading of radical, ultra-nationalist views that remains a reading, and a country in the first of two days of national mourning, with a Mass for the victims set for 4 p.m. local time at St. Mary’s Basilica and Kraków’s own mourning running through November 11. No motive has been established by any investigator, and no charge has yet been laid against a man who cannot be questioned.
