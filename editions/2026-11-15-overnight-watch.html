The Airlines at 0400: Saturday Closed Above 6,400 Cancelled, Sunday Begins at 875 Down, and the Promise Falls Due at the Close of Business

Saturday’s final counts closed the recovery day above every count the day itself had produced, and the desk prints the closing figures as the day’s record. Airlines had cancelled more than 6,400 flights in the United States and delayed more than 8,000 on Saturday as of 2135 Eastern, in the wire account at the top of this report, on the tracker’s data; nearly 6,500 flights were cancelled on the day at the 2200 count, in the midnight edition’s tracker account; about 5,600 had been cancelled by 1800, in the London account filed below; and more than 6,150 were cancelled and over 7,300 delayed in the second wire account filed below. Friday’s figures stand beneath them: about 9,200 cancelled and 7,500 delayed on the day of the stop. The administrator’s arithmetic of the stop itself entered the file on Saturday and stands in the London account: about 20,000 flights still had journeys to complete when the stop began, about 8,000 of them were able to continue afterwards, and approximately 12,000 could not recover—roughly 9,000 commercial flights and 3,000 private flights among them. No second stop was ordered on Saturday or overnight. Departures ran, aircraft and crews were repositioned through the night, and transatlantic services had returned to normal by Sunday morning with knock-on disruption continuing into Ireland and England, in the transatlantic account filed below.

What Sunday holds, at 0400, is a number and a promise, and they do not yet agree. The number is 875: flights scheduled for Sunday already cancelled by 2135 on Saturday night, in the wire account above, up from 160 at 1530 on Saturday in the same file’s earlier reading; the tracker’s midnight figure, nearly 900, stands beside it in the midnight edition. One carrier had cancelled roughly a quarter of its planned Sunday operation by late Saturday; the rates on the other carriers were trending far lower; and a major airline said late on Saturday that it had completed its planned cancellations for that night and Sunday, although individual flights could still be cancelled while it restored its operation, in the London account. The promise is the one the file has carried since Saturday: full schedule integrity by the close of business on Sunday, in the administrator’s words and the airlines’. The administrator’s own caution stands beside the promise in the second wire account: the worst is behind us, but a significant amount of recovery work remains. On cause, the night added no finding. The account stands where Saturday’s press conference left it: a contractor replacing copper wire with fiber at a remote location, a new router flooding the network with more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds in the Secretary’s figure, 17 of 22 en route centers affected and none dark, additional testing under way with the contractor, and the Secretary’s question—installer error, user error, or a faulty router—unanswered, all in the wire account of the briefing. Settled: the stop, its mechanism in outline, and Saturday’s cost. Open: whose error, if anyone’s, and whether Sunday closes whole, which today’s counts will answer.

The Gulf at 0400: Manama Issues an Alert, the Radar Claim and Denial Stand, and Three Tankers Are Claimed on Mines

Manama spoke overnight, and what it said was addressed to its own residents, not to either capital’s account of Friday. Bahrain’s Ministry of Interior issued an alert on November 15 urging citizens and residents to remain calm, proceed to the nearest safe location, and follow news through official channels for the protection of public safety, in the alert account at the top of this report, which carries the ministry’s message as posted. The alert is the first nationwide threat alert from Bahrain since September in the summary account filed below, and it arrived one day after the Guards’ claim against the radar. No official confirmation of the alert’s cause stands in the accounts; informal sources reported drones and missiles overflying the kingdom’s airspace, in the alert account, and the desk prints that as a report of a report, nothing more. Against the alert, the two standing accounts of Friday stand exactly where they stood: the Guards’ claim that its aerospace force attacked and destroyed the early-warning radar of the American forces in Bahrain, in retaliation for the strike on the tower at Sirik, carried in the claims account filed below, which adds the Guards’ figure of six drones against a radar at a helicopter base; and Central Command’s denial, restated in the denial account filed below: two one-way attack drones launched toward Bahrain, both shot down, no injuries, no damage. Neither side has produced evidence the other accepts. The alert does not settle the radar question; it settles only that Manama judged its residents should be told to take shelter, and that fact now stands in the file beside the claim and the denial.

The strait gained a claim overnight of the kind this file has learned to carry unverified. Iran’s Navy said three tankers caught fire on Saturday after colliding with naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz, in the claims account above, which records that the wire had not been able to verify the report; the summary account puts the figure at two to three oil and liquefied-gas tankers struck and on fire, against American statements that the main shipping lanes have been cleared of mines. The Guards said the strait remained closed, in the same account. No shipping authority’s confirmation of a tanker fire stands in any account the desk read by 0400, and the desk files the claim at that size. The wider Gulf file also carried, in the claims account, a Saudi report that its air defenses intercepted and destroyed two ballistic missiles launched toward Riyadh, attributed by its coalition spokesman to the Houthi militia; and the standing figures of the maritime file, in the same account: 25 seafarers dead and many injured or missing across some 134 incidents in the strait and the wider area as of November 11, in the International Maritime Organization’s count as reported. Tehran’s seven conditions stand unanswered, the mediators’ work on an interim agreement stands as the midnight edition filed it, and the blockade stands in force. What changed overnight is the file’s center of gravity: the next dated occasion in the Gulf is still Shenzhen on Thursday, where Washington says the President will raise the war in Iran, but Manama’s alert is now the file’s newest fact, and it is dated today.
