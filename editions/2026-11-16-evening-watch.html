The Commerce Rule: The Rental Route Closed, Five Countries Named, and Ninety Days to a Licence

The Administration’s first policy answer to the scandal arrived on Monday, and it is a licensing rule. The Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule so that the existing restrictions, in its words, “cannot be circumvented through remote computing arrangements involving data centers in third countries,” in the loophole account filed below. A licence is required for covered chips going to a data centre anywhere in the world that provides more than 12 million in aggregate total processing performance to a company headquartered in China, Macau, or another listed country, or whose ultimate parent is headquartered there, in the rule account filed below, which reckons the threshold at roughly 750 H100 processors or about 330 Blackwell B200s by the rule’s formula. Centres already selling that much computing power to such companies have 90 days from the rule’s taking effect to obtain a licence, and exporters will need a licence to send advanced chips and servers to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, which the rule names as carrying a higher-than-normal risk of diversion, in the same accounts; the one carve-out named is computing used only for commercial recommender systems, in the rule account. The rule formally rescinds the January 2025 AI Diffusion Rule, in both accounts, and it takes effect when it appears in the Federal Register, which had not happened in the accounts the desk read, with comments due 60 days after publication, in the rule account. The company at the centre of the file stands inside it: the Singapore company where the woman named in the allegations worked is suspected of selling remote access to American chips to Chinese firms, says it operates lawfully, and has opened an internal review, in the closing account. The President said the same day that selling other chips is “a good thing,” in the same account. No finding against any company named in this file stands in the evening file. Settled: the rule, its threshold, and its five countries. Open: its effective date, and what it does to Tuesday’s guidance.

Chairman Warsh at Noon: Three Scenarios for Super Intelligence, No Forecast, and No Rate Proposed

The chairman’s remarks, which began at the hour the midday edition was stamped, are the day’s set speech, and the desk prints their structure whole. Speaking in San Francisco at the opening of the Productivity and Jobs Task Force Conference, Chairman Warsh said the staff have begun modelling what the technology he calls super intelligence could do to the economy, and set out three scenarios, in the wire account above and in the scenarios account filed below. In the first, the technology raises productivity and the gains are shared broadly across labour and the owners of capital: “In that event, real GDP growth rises persistently above its long-run average, and the US stays close to full employment,” in the wire account. In the second, the gains arrive and the workers are left behind, with unemployment rising even as the economy grows, in the scenarios account. In the third, the heavy investment disappoints, equity valuations fall, and unemployment rises, in the same account. “These are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts,” the chairman said, in the same account; “Our job is to prepare for whatever comes next,” in the wire account. The technology could prove distinct from earlier technologies as a force to “supercharge idea generation,” he said, and machines that can amass or exceed human knowledge are still machines and do not take the place of human judgment, morality, or responsibility, in the growth account filed below. “Our role in all this is neither advocacy nor opposition,” he said, in the wire account. “Our responsibility is to recognize that the widespread adoption of this technology will have significant impact on the economy, with important implications for monetary policy down the road. The big questions are about magnitude, scope, and timing.” A second central bank voice entered the file the same day: Governor Barr warned that the construction boom is increasingly financed through arrangements whose potential maximum value the bank estimates at more than $3 trillion off balance sheet, in the scenarios account. The chairman proposed no change in interest rates, in every account; the markets’ pricing stands where the week left it, fully priced for a quarter-point rise at the December 8 and 9 meeting in the midday file, and at 95.7 percent for a quarter-point rise in December in the closing account’s reading of the futures.
