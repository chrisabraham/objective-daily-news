Six Dead in Kraków, Charges in Asunción, and Kyiv Struck Three Days Before the Talks

The day’s developments whole at 1800: a gunman fired from a car into Kraków’s Main Market Square and killed six people before police shot and arrested him; prosecutors in Asunción charged the removed president and the former central bank chief and asked a court to jail both pending trial, while $160 million went out to nearly 1.2 million savers; Iran’s Guards lifted the self-imposed limit on missile ranges while Washington and Doha both said the talks had made progress; Russian missiles struck Kyiv three days before the Abu Dhabi talks, and Moscow named its delegation; the first considered responses to the Miller appointment set the terms of the next two years in Washington; and no Senate race was called for a fourth day.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1800 ET, Saturday, November 7, 2026. This edition completes the day continued in Edition No. 14, the Overnight Watch, collected at 0400, and Edition No. 15, the Midday Watch, collected at 1200. Those editions stand unrevised, as a log stands. Where the evening figures differ from the midday figures, the difference is stated here, in the open.

The Evening in Brief

Saturday produced one atrocity, one indictment, and three statements of position, and closed nothing the week left open. In Kraków, at about 3 p.m. local time, a man drove into the Main Market Square and fired at random from his car, killing six people—four women and two men—and wounding at least fifteen, two of them police officers, before officers shot and arrested him three minutes after the first report, in the interior minister’s account; the minister said in his evening briefing that everything points to the shooter having been influenced by far-right ideology, a matter he said will be investigated, and the president will declare national mourning for November 8 and 9. In Asunción, prosecutors charged former President Santiago Peña with breach of trust and administration for personal gain and former central bank chief Carlos Carvallo with those charges and producing false public documents, asked a court to hold both men in pretrial detention and freeze their assets, and the central bank reported $160 million paid out to nearly 1.2 million Ueno savers under the deposit guarantee. In Tehran, the Revolutionary Guards’ spokesman said the self-imposed limit of 2,000 kilometres on ballistic missile ranges “has been lifted for us,” following the supreme leader’s order that threats be answered without geographical limits; no file on the shelf shows an Iranian missile demonstrated beyond the old limit. In Washington, the responses to Friday’s appointment of Stephen Miller as chief of staff, effective Monday, divided on the record: allies called the pick perfect and critics, including one Republican congressman, called it a misreading of the voters’ verdict, while in Houston the mayor’s order limiting police cooperation with federal immigration officers took effect as the week’s first local answer to the election. On Iran, the president’s Friday words stand as the latest American position—“We’ve made a lot of progress with Iran”—beside Qatar’s statement that significant progress has been made; no agreement, no text, and no larger strike decision had been announced by 1800. Kyiv was struck by Russian missiles in the night, three days before the Abu Dhabi talks open, and Moscow named its delegation on Saturday. The count did not move: Mississippi and Alaska stand uncalled for a fourth day, and the markets, closed Saturday, stand where Friday left them. The desk files the day in that order.

Kraków: Six Dead in the Main Market Square

The attack began at about 3 p.m. local time and the suspect was in custody minutes later. Reuters’ Kraków file, filed below, reports that police began receiving calls at about 3 p.m. that an assailant had opened fire from a car at people in the Main Market Square, the historic centre of Poland’s second-largest city; that the dead are four women and two men; that the alleged gunman is a 24-year-old Polish citizen who wore military-style clothes and used military weapons, with many weapons and ammunition found in his vehicle; and that police shot and arrested him. A police spokesperson told a broadcaster the weapon was an AR-15 rifle and that the man held a firearms licence. The account of the official statements records that the toll was first given as five dead and was later confirmed at six dead and fifteen injured by the regional government office; that two police officers are among the injured, one of them shot three times and in very critical condition, with surgery under way; and that police denied reports that an officer had died or that a second perpetrator was being sought—“The only known perpetrator of the incident is in police custody.” The suspect has not been named publicly by the police. The defence minister denied reports that he was a member of the armed forces, in the accounts filed below.

The evening added the government’s first reading of the motive, and it is filed as a reading, not a finding. Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński, in his evening briefing as carried in the Reuters file, said: “Everything points to the fact that the shooter was influenced by far-right ideology, a matter that will also be subject to investigation.” He said five foreigners were among the sixteen wounded taken to hospital—one more than the fifteen in the afternoon’s official count—and that four of the dead had been identified, one of them a foreigner; Portugal’s foreign ministry said a Portuguese woman was among the injured. A later account filed below reports the national police chief saying a British citizen is among the dead, and Prime Minister Donald Tusk, speaking in Kraków after meeting the security services, calling the attack terrorism and saying Poland has not had such a drastic case before. Mr. Tusk’s afternoon statement asked politicians of all stripes not to exploit the tragedy and said all the circumstances will be established. President Karol Nawrocki’s spokesperson said he will declare national mourning on November 8 and 9; the city has declared mourning through November 11. Condolences arrived from President Zelenskyy of Ukraine and from the European Union’s foreign policy chief, Kaja Kallas, in the same file. No charge against the suspect had been announced by 1800 ET, and no motive has been established by any court or investigator on the record.

Asunción at Evening: Charges Filed, Detention Asked, Savers Paid

The case the midday edition left at the prosecutor’s door was filed in the afternoon. Reuters’ Asunción file, updated after the appearance, reports that prosecutors on Saturday charged former President Santiago Peña with breach of trust and administration for personal gain and asked a court to jail him pending trial; that former central bank chief Carlos Carvallo was charged with breach of trust, producing false public documents, and administration for personal gain, with his pretrial detention also sought; that prosecutors allege Peña used his position to influence agencies and state entities to keep large amounts of public money deposited at Ueno despite his financial ties to the lender; and that the two charges against Peña could carry up to ten years in prison each, in the prosecutor’s statement. A second account adds that both men declined to say anything at the hearing, that prosecutors asked for both men’s assets to be frozen and for six months to formulate the final case, that twenty-seven people have been charged in total, and that a judge has ordered six former Ueno directors held in pretrial detention. Peña’s lawyer, Ricardo Preda, rejected pretrial detention before the charges were filed, saying nothing justified it. Peña has denied wrongdoing and said the bank’s problems were a regulatory matter. The charges are charges; the detention request is a request; a judge has not ruled on it, and no court has ruled on any allegation in the case.

The other half of the day in Asunción is money moving back to savers, and a new government forming under protest. The central bank said on Saturday that the equivalent of $160 million had been paid out to nearly 1.2 million savers under the deposit guarantee scheme, in the Reuters file—the first payment figure of the crisis, against a bank that served about three million customers and held as much as $850 million in public money. The fuller account of the week filed below records that President Pedro Alliana’s first decree appointed Ariel Martínez as minister of the interior, a post he held in 2018, with Juan Carlos Baruja arriving at Public Works; that the prosecutorial hypothesis examines deposits by public bodies and social security entities between 2023 and 2026 and allegedly irregular credits to companies linked to Grupo Vázquez; and that protests over the Ueno case continue, with protesters and opposition sectors questioning Alliana and some calling for early elections. The guarantee’s structure stands as the central bank published it: a first group paid in full up to ten million guaraníes, an advance of up to forty minimum wages, and a coverage limit of seventy-five minimum wages per depositor—three different figures, not three payments to be added. What Alliana promised in words, that those affected will recover their funds, and what the guarantee pays in stages, remain two different statements, and the desk keeps them separate.

Washington at Evening: The Miller Appointment Read From Every Side

Friday’s appointment produced its considered readings on Saturday, and they divide cleanly. The facts stand as filed Friday: Susie Wiles submitted her resignation on Thursday evening; the president announced Stephen Miller, his deputy chief of staff and the architect of the administration’s immigration enforcement programme, as her successor, effective Monday; Mrs. Wiles will move to the president’s political operation; and the president told CBS News he intends further changes, saying of his Cabinet, “I love my Cabinet. I think they’re good. But I’ll be doing maybe a few changes.” Saturday’s analysis, filed below, reads the appointment as the president doubling down after first suggesting openness to working with Democrats: allies who urged the change argued Mr. Miller had proven he could carry out the president’s programme without a learning period and could repair the party’s standing with its own movement; Steve Bannon called him “a perfect pick.” The other reading is on the same record: Representative Marlin Stutzman of Indiana, a Republican, said in a local radio appearance, “We lost Latinos because of the deportation look,” singling out Mr. Miller, and added that the party must wake up to being in the minority. Representative Robert Garcia, the incoming Oversight leadership’s ranking member, said, “Stephen Miller should lawyer up.” Representative Chellie Pingree of Maine called the choice a move “from very bad to worse than you could possibly imagine.” An immigration-reform advocate quoted in the analysis said calls to work with the president on reform are not realistic while Mr. Miller is in the White House. The president’s own words on the pick were: “Stephen’s been with me from the beginning. He gets it. Very good politically. And he’s a very good man.” Mr. Miller posted that serving as chief of staff is “the privilege of my lifetime” and that “the mission now is more clear than ever.” Both readings agree on one fact: the appointment takes effect Monday, and the two years of divided government begin with it.

The election’s first local consequence in Texas took effect the same weekend. Reuters’ Houston file reports that Mayor John Whitmire on Friday ordered the city’s police to curtail cooperation with federal immigration officers: officers on routine stops are barred from alerting Immigration and Customs Enforcement solely on the basis of a civil immigration warrant, and may notify the agency only when the person is also being arrested on a jailable offence. The mayor said he acted after consulting the state’s newly elected governor, lieutenant governor, and comptroller, to “reduce fear and anxiety” across the city and because “elections matter.” Police Chief J. Noe Diaz said he supported the change and the department had begun implementing it. The order reverses a policy the mayor adopted in April under pressure from Governor Greg Abbott, who lost his re-election bid on Tuesday; department figures carried in the accounts filed below show officers contacted the agency nearly 200 times from April through September and handed people directly to it at least 36 times. The order does not bar federal agents from operating in Houston on their own authority. It is the first such order of the post-election week on the shelf, and it was made by a mayor of the president’s opponents’ party in the largest city of a state his party swept on Tuesday—filed as the local record, under the same roof as the appointment above it, because the evening file is the day whole.

Iran at Evening: The Range Limit Lifted, Progress Claimed on Both Sides, No Decision Announced

Tehran’s announcement of the day is a policy ending, stated by the Guards in one sentence. Tasnim’s file reports that Brigadier General Hossein Mohebbi, spokesman for the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, announced at a press conference for the force’s Aerospace Week that restrictions on the range of Iran’s missiles have been lifted, saying the country must demonstrate its expanded capabilities in practice rather than merely make statements. The fuller account adds that the remarks followed Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei’s order of an immediate and effective response to any threat without geographical limits, and a review of the military’s target database to include American and Israeli assets. The wire’s account puts the change plainly: the self-imposed limit of 2,000 kilometres, in place since 2017 under the former supreme leader, is ended, suggesting Iran may seek weapons able to reach more distant targets. Every account on the shelf carries the same caution, and the desk repeats it as part of the fact: no file shows Iran possessing or demonstrating an operational missile beyond the old limit, and General Mohebbi himself said the capability must be shown in practice before it is claimed. A restraint is lifted; a weapon is not thereby in hand.

Against that announcement, the diplomatic file gained words from both principals and no paper. The president, speaking to reporters in West Palm Beach on Friday in the account filed below, confirmed the limited strikes on Iranian air defences near Qeshm Island—“We do minor strikes”; “we knocked it out in about 15 minutes, maybe five”—and said repeatedly that his administration is speaking with Tehran’s representatives: “We’re dealing with them now. They want to make a deal. We’ve made a lot of progress with Iran.” He gave no details of the progress. Qatar’s prime minister said the same day, in the midday file, that significant progress had been made and the aim is a good agreement rather than a perfect one, and Qatar’s media office denied reports that the talks had failed. The Qatari proposal, as reported in the accounts filed below, would have Washington lift its blockade and release some frozen Iranian funds, Iran admit international inspectors to damaged sites, and the Strait of Hormuz reopen on the arrangement’s second day, without Iran surrendering its nuclear stockpiles; Tehran’s deputy security chief said Iran is not satisfied with paper commitments and must see practical steps, and Tehran’s own seven conditions stand unmet and unwithdrawn. The explosions reported from Qeshm in the night remain unconfirmed as to origin and damage, and the United States has not announced any new attack. The larger decision promised on Thursday—whether to resume the war in full—had not been announced at 1800 on Saturday. Progress is claimed in Washington and in Doha; it is described in neither capital; and the missile announcement in Tehran is the day’s only new fact with a text behind it.

Kyiv and Abu Dhabi: Missiles in the Night, a Delegation Named, Three Days Out

Kyiv was struck in the night between Friday and Saturday, and the counts of the injured do not agree. The Associated Press file reports missile explosions in Kyiv early Saturday leaving at least five people injured, damaging homes and commercial properties, closing traffic on a bridge over the Dnipro River, and triggering new power and water outages. A second account puts the injured at two and records Mayor Vitali Klitschko reporting explosions in the suburbs as missiles targeted infrastructure, after President Zelenskyy’s Friday warning that Russia was preparing strikes on energy facilities, bridges, and civilian infrastructure. Mr. Zelenskyy’s Saturday statement, in the accounts filed below, gives the barrage at 36 ballistic missiles and 172 drones over Kyiv and twelve other regions, four missiles intercepted, and nine people wounded including a child; Russia’s defence ministry confirmed it targeted the Northern Bridge, saying it facilitated the transport of weaponry and military equipment. The desk prints the three injury figures as three figures—two, five, and nine—from three hours and three sources, and does not choose among them. No deaths in Kyiv had been confirmed in the accounts taken in by 1800.

The talks the missiles precede acquired their cast on Saturday. The Kremlin spokesman told Interfax, in the Saturday accounts filed below, that Russia’s delegation to Abu Dhabi will be led by Kremlin aide Vladimir Medinsky and will include Deputy Foreign Ministers Sergei Ryabkov and Mikhail Galuzin, military intelligence chief Igor Kostyukov, and a General Staff officer, Yuri Zhigarlovsky. The American president’s Thursday position stands: after meeting the Russian envoy Kirill Dmitriev privately, he predicted a big agreement involving more than Ukraine, possibly all of Europe, and gave no details and said he had not met the Ukrainian delegation. Ukraine’s president, in the interview account filed below, said he does not know many details of the framework being floated, warned that a deal between Russia and Europe would not be simple and could be dangerous, and held that ending the war comes first and guarantees second—the same position as his Thursday demand that guarantees be clear on paper. The talks open Tuesday, November 10, the first trilateral meeting since February. In his Saturday evening address, Mr. Zelenskyy also spoke to a second file: the corruption investigation, Operation Carthage, in which anti-corruption authorities have charged senior security and law-enforcement officials with shielding a criminal ring. Reuters’ Kyiv file reports him calling the alleged crimes “frankly shameful, absolutely unacceptable,” saying dismissals at the security service have already occurred and more personnel decisions will follow, and proposing legislation requiring all security-service employees to publicly declare their assets. A court on Friday ordered a former general of the service arrested pending bail. The allegations are allegations; the dismissals and the bill are acts.

The Count at Evening: A Fourth Day Without a Call

Neither Senate race was called on Saturday, and the day’s contribution to the file is an explanation of the wait. The explainer filed below sets out the machinery: in Mississippi, mail and affidavit ballots are still being processed under a ten-day window, counties certify by November 13, and the secretary of state counted roughly 3,600 mail ballots outstanding in his Wednesday statement beside an unknown number of affidavit ballots; in Alaska, ranked-choice tabulation is not published until November 18, fifteen days after the election, and nearly a quarter of the vote remains uncounted. The Saturday account of the two races carries the standing figures: Scott Colom ahead of Cindy Hyde-Smith by some 2,500 votes in Mississippi in the wire’s tally, with Republicans estimating roughly 20,000 ballots outstanding—an estimate ten thousand below the figure in circulation at midday—and voters able to resolve ballot problems through November 10; and Mary Peltola ahead of Dan Sullivan by 637 votes in Alaska, with the legal fronts in that race opened by both sides, the Justice Department’s suit before the voting and the Peltola campaign’s records suit on Friday. Democrats will hold at least 54 Senate seats in the 120th Congress whatever the two races decide; what remains open is the size of the majority, for a fourth day. Colorado’s 4th District stands where Friday left it, Eileen Laubacher ahead of Lauren Boebert by 3,278 votes, uncalled. Tuesday, November 10 now carries the next Alaska update, Mississippi’s ballot deadline, both congressional parties’ leadership elections, consumer prices, and the opening of the Abu Dhabi talks.

Europe’s Hoaxes and the Week’s Settled Markets

Friday’s third wave of hoax bomb threats is the evening’s European file, and its totals also disagree. The fullest account, filed below, counts at least 18 flights disrupted on Friday to or from nine countries, while the European transport commissioner put Friday’s total at 11; nine of the flights used Prague’s airport, Hungary scrambled fighter jets to escort three threatened aircraft, and four aircraft over France landed at Paris Orly for inspection. Every threatened aircraft landed safely and no explosives were found on any plane or at any site. The threats spread on Friday to newsrooms: Ireland’s broadcaster evacuated its Dublin headquarters and ran pre-recorded programming for hours, other Irish outlets emptied their offices, and a British broadcaster confirmed it had received a threat. Friday’s emails were signed with a name claiming leadership of the group behind the week’s hoaxes; a different signature on Thursday’s threats named a movement whose founder denied involvement and said the group has not existed since 2021. Investigators have found no evidence that a hostile state or a terror group is directing the hoaxes. Spain’s foreign minister said the identical methods across so many countries could not be coincidence and called the most reasonable assumption “an attempt at hybrid aggression and a form of psychological warfare”; Ireland’s prime minister said the episode showed how relatively simple it can be to disrupt the normal operation of society. Both statements are on the record; the authorship of the threats is not. The markets add nothing to the day: the exchanges are closed, and the week stands settled as filed at midday—the S&P 500 down 2.7 percent, Brent crude at $98.32, down 13.3 percent, and Berkshire Hathaway’s third quarter, operating earnings down 5.8 percent and cash at a record $377.4 billion, as the day’s one scheduled report. Monday’s open waits on the Iran file, as it waited on Friday.

The Cocked Hat

Three bearings never give a point; they give a small triangle, and this paper prints the triangle. On Kraków’s wounded, the afternoon’s official count is fifteen and the interior minister’s evening figure is sixteen taken to hospital; the desk prints both and notes the hour of each. On Kyiv’s injured, the figures are two, five, and nine, from a city account, the wire, and the president, and no confirmed death stands in any of them. On the hoax flights, the counts are eleven and eighteen for the same Friday. On Mississippi’s outstanding ballots, the estimates in circulation on Saturday run from roughly 20,000 to roughly 30,000. Against the triangle, the evening’s load-bearing facts are single-valued: six people are dead in Kraków and the only known perpetrator is in police custody; Peña and Carvallo stand charged, detention and an asset freeze are asked and not yet granted, and $160 million has been paid to nearly 1.2 million savers; the missile-range limit has been lifted in words, with no missile shown beyond it; progress on Iran is claimed in Washington and Doha and described in neither; Russia has named its Abu Dhabi delegation and the talks open Tuesday; Stephen Miller takes the chief of staff’s office on Monday; Houston’s order is in effect; no race was called; and the Shenzhen meeting of three presidents, eleven days out, received no new word. Saturday’s file therefore closes as it opened in the brief: one atrocity, answered by an arrest and an investigation; one presidency, answered by a charge sheet and a payment ledger; two wars, answered by announcements and preparations; and an election, answered so far by an appointment, a mayor’s order, and a count that will not move until Tuesday. The log proceeds to midnight.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
