Weekend Features—Sunday Review of Reviews

Objective

Anita Brookner Is the Book of the Week, Haymitch Divides the Critics and Delights the Audience, and Two Detectives Cross the Sea

The week's review lists were tallied, and the tally gave the desk a book with no dissent in its count and a screen week divided by audience. The book du jour is Anita Brookner: Art and Life by Hermione Lee, published by Knopf on November 17: first in the week's nonfiction tally with twelve rave reviews and four positive, and no mixed or negative verdict recorded—a biography of a novelist who destroyed her papers, said she would leave no trace, and would, by every reviewer's account, have hated being biographied. On screen, The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping opened November 20 with the critics at 67 to 72 percent and verified audiences at 95 percent, the two camps agreeing on Joseph Zada's young Haymitch and dividing on whether the prequel needed to exist; and The Blue Road, the Korea-Japan crime serial on Netflix, released the same day in eight episodes, drew 9 of 10, 8 of 10 and 4.5 of 5 for the chemistry of its two detectives, a Seoul man and a Tokyo man working one serial killer across the sea between them. The desk states its position plainly at the head of each part: it has not read the book, and it has not watched the film or the serial. What follows is a review of reviews.

Part One—The Book: Anita Brookner: Art and Life, by Hermione Lee

The desk has not read this book. It says so plainly, as the house rule requires: everything in this part is what the reviewers report, agree on, and dispute, and nothing in it is the desk's reading. Anita Brookner: Art and Life is a biography. Hermione Lee is the biographer of Virginia Woolf, Edith Wharton, Penelope Fitzgerald and Tom Stoppard. Her subject, Anita Brookner (1928–2016), was an art historian at the Courtauld Institute who published her first novel at 53, won the Booker Prize in 1984 for her fourth novel, Hotel du Lac, and wrote 25 novels in all. This book was published in the United States by Knopf on November 17, 2026: 544 pages, $45, ISBN 9780593321508.

On what the book must overcome, the reviewers agree completely, and their agreement is the week's firmest fact. Brookner disposed of her letters, left no private papers, warned interviewers she would stamp on any attempt at a biography, and said, in the words the reviews repeat, “I shall leave no trace.” She died in 2016 at 87. Lee herself, in the line every review quotes, concedes the position: “How she would have hated having a biography!” The reviewers also agree on the life the biography assembles from what remains: born in 1928 in Herne Hill, the only child of Polish Jewish émigré parents—her father in the tobacco business, her mother a failed singer—formed as a postgraduate researcher in postwar Paris, and established first as an electrifying art historian before the fiction began. And they agree on the caricature Lee writes against: the melancholy, cigarette-smoking recluse in a long grey cardigan, wistfully yearning for an absent or unreliable lover—the “aloof spinster” of the reductive notices, churning out the same novel every July.

On what the book is taken to be, the favorable reviews—sixteen of the sixteen counted—agree in substance and differ only in temperature. Leigh Haber in the Boston Globe, in the passage the tally carries, calls it a passionate case for studying a complex, compelling, now obscure figure, a connoisseur of women's interior lives who could see what many did not, and holds that for readers who have felt similarly dismissed the biography may spark a new passion for her. Kathryn Hughes in Literary Review calls it a flawless biography, fond but not foolish, whose rigour never simply equates the heroines with their creator: Brookner emerges spikier, sharper, cooler than anyone had noticed. The Guardian's Lucy Atkins, in the praise filed with the book, calls it an astute and tireless biography that succeeds, against the odds, in unearthing an author who would be appalled by the scrutiny. Kirkus, in its starred notice, calls it majestic, a deeply researched, empathetic portrayal; Publishers Weekly, also starred, calls it masterful, a captivating reevaluation of a singular and enigmatic novelist. The shared claim under the praise is Lee's own, as the reviews report it: Brookner was not her heroines but their inventor—a deeply romantic and passionate writer whose armour was an austere, controlled manner, a dry formal vocabulary and a scathing wit—and her 25 novels are read, in Lee's proposal carried by Bookforum, not as repetitions but as versions of one long Proustian book, as Monet repainted Rouen Cathedral.

The split in the file is about length, and it is filed by the two largest American notices against the British consensus. The New York Times review, as Book Marks excerpts it, holds that Brookner is the subject of a new, 500-page book from the tenacious and indefatigable Lee, and that at half the length Anita Brookner: Art and Life would probably have been twice as good: it is padded in ways that add drag to its stately propulsion, forced into detours and workarounds because relatively little is known of Brookner's private life, and consuming it can be like reading the introduction to a reissue that goes on for hundreds of pages—while conceding, in the same notice, that the book is very, very good in places, with terrific vividness. A second American account filed with the tally calls the biography deft and adulatory, and names the challenge the Times presses: what is a biographer to do when the subject does nothing much but write? The Times of London files a narrower version of the same reservation from inside its praise: Lee brings rigour and authority, refuses the melancholy caricature, and the narrative suffers in the second half, through the dutiful summaries of the 25 novels, where Lee herself observes that some later works burn on a low flame and can be repetitive and prolix. The desk files the disagreement as it stands. No reviewer in the week's file disputes Lee's research, her subject's worth, or the portrait's direction; the dispute is whether 544 pages is the portrait's frame or its padding.

The fiction leader is filed in one paragraph, as the tally's second book. The Taper Man, Nick Harkaway's second George Smiley novel after Karla's Choice (2024), is set in 1965: a British agent is shot in Helsinki, the Circus's prize asset in Moscow is imperilled, and the trail leads Smiley to Los Angeles and a sleeper agent—the taper man, left behind to keep the candle lit—poised to expose the asset. Publishers Weekly, in the passage the tally carries, holds that the conclusion is anything but conclusive, as in most of the Circus novels, and that Harkaway exhibits his father's knack for slow reveals, complex storylines and salient observations about human nature: George Smiley is in good hands. Ron Charles, in his Tuesday Review, records being captivated even when lost, credits the book with exposing the prejudices that infected America's intelligence establishment—a brilliant African American woman sidelined for her skin colour, and the strategic cost of it—and files Smiley wincing through the heat and ugliness of Los Angeles as terrific fun. The tally counts five rave, one positive and one mixed.

Part Two—Screen: Haymitch at 95 Percent, and Two Detectives Cross the Sea

The desk has not watched any of the screen works in this part. It has not sat in a cinema for The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, and it has not streamed The Blue Road or I Play Rocky. Their status here is the same as the book's: the reviews, reviewed.

The television pick of the week is The Blue Road, and it is a Korea-Japan serial, and it opened the same day as the week's film. The eight-episode crime serial—Pureungil in Korean—was released on Netflix on November 20, directed by Han Jun-hee, who also directed D.P. and the recent Dead-End Job, and co-written with Cho Yu-jin. It is adapted from the 2002 comic Blue Road by Keishi Edogawa and Kwon Ka-ya, and it is set in 2002, the year Japan and South Korea co-hosted the World Cup. Son Suk-ku plays Kim Seok, a Seoul detective; Eita Nagayama plays Hiroshi Inose, a Tokyo detective; a serial killer is killing in both countries, and the two men, who meet in a chase and dislike each other on sight, must work one case across the sea. Kim Shin-rok, Choi Sung-eun, Jung Jae-young and Kentaro Sakaguchi stand in the cast the reviews name. The first three episodes screened at the Busan International Film Festival ahead of release, to positive reviews in the accounts filed below.

The reviewers agree on the pairing, on the grip, and on what the case is carrying. Every review in the file credits the same centre: the chemistry of Son and Nagayama, a hard-boiled, no-nonsense Seoul detective carrying a violent past and a more open, easygoing Tokyo detective whose passion for the work keeps breaking through, the contrast turning an uneasy collaboration into a bond the reviewers root for. Every review records the same construction under the case: the wounds of Japan's historical impact on Korea, still lingering, worked through the partnership itself, so that the investigation carries the two countries' history as well as their killer. And every review records the victims' staging and the messages left with them as genuinely unsettling, the horror delivered, in the opening murder, mostly through sound. The scores print the agreement without ornament: But Why Tho? at 9 of 10, calling the serial rich and compelling and a must-watch for crime drama fans; Pixel Twelve at 8 of 10 for the opening three episodes; K-Waves and Beyond at 4.5 of 5; Leisurebyte calling it one of the best crime thrillers its reviewer has watched this year.

The split is small, and the desk files it at its true size. One review names the score as its one complaint—generic thriller work sitting in the background, partly redeemed by the killer intro. But Why Tho?, in its 9 of 10 notice, records a last-minute callback in the final episode that is more sentimental than earned. Leisurebyte records that the opening episode needs a few minutes to build momentum before the serial becomes relentless, and that the killer is identified by the fourth episode, the interest thereafter lying in the complexities the case opens rather than in the whodunit. No review in the file faults the two leads, the period setting, or the handling of the history. The desk files the week's television record as the screen week's one consensus: a brutal serial-killer case carried, by agreement, on a partnership.

In cinemas, the week's film is The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, and its reviewers agree on its Haymitch and divide on its existence. The film opened November 20, directed by Francis Lawrence, rated PG-13 and running 158 minutes—the longest in the franchise, in the weekend accounts—with Joseph Zada as the young Haymitch Abernathy, the District 12 victor of the 50th Hunger Games, the Second Quarter Quell, 24 years before the original films, in the story Suzanne Collins published in 2025. Jesse Plemons, Ralph Fiennes, Glenn Close, Kieran Culkin and Elle Fanning stand in the cast the reviews name. The agreement in the file is exact and runs through reviews that agree on nothing else: Zada is the film's asset. The Hollywood Reporter's David Rooney calls his Haymitch a breakout performance—invested with tenacity, kindness and heartrending vulnerability—and a far more poignant and involving origin story than young Snow's in the previous prequel; IndieWire's Ryan Lattanzio, against his own verdict on the film, credits Zada in the same role; RogerEbert.com's account lists Zada alongside Woody Harrelson as the actors defining the character across the saga. The craft agreement runs beside it: Jo Willems's muscular camerawork, James Newton Howard's soaring score and Uli Hanisch's imaginative production design, credited by name in the favorable and the mixed notices alike.

The division is the prequel itself, and the counts print it in two currencies. Rooney calls the film a worthier prequel that delivers on emotion and brutal spectacle. Against him, the Guardian's Peter Bradshaw, at one star, holds that the film has no actual interest and no authentic reason to exist other than to gouge an income stream out of its fanbase. Between the poles: the New York Times' Brandon Yu finds a film both bloated and limited, cloistered in its vision of the games and the wider world, its formula named as dystopian pulp, doomed romance, teenage heroism and rebellion; Variety's Owen Gleiberman finds it meandering and more than a bit thin, directed in the lavish mode he calls Hunger Games Classic; IndieWire finds it overlong, visually soupy and narratively morose. The critics' counts moved as the file arrived: 75 percent in one early count, 72 percent from 71 reviews in a second, 69 percent from 62 in a third, 67 percent on opening day and 68 percent on Saturday, with Metacritic at 56. The audience count did not move in the same way: 95 percent from verified moviegoers—a franchise record, ahead of Catching Fire at 89 percent—with an A-minus CinemaScore and PostTrak at 93 percent positive. The box office filed the same shape in dollars: $56 million on Friday including $21.25 million in Thursday previews, a projection of $120 million or more for the weekend, and the No. 1 position in 66 of the 67 markets it had opened in. The desk files the split as it stands: the people who review films respect this one more than they love it, and the people who bought tickets love it more than any Hunger Games film before it.

The week's second film is filed in one paragraph, because its scores run the other way from the week's headline film in degree, not in shape. I Play Rocky, the account of how Sylvester Stallone made Rocky, expanded wide on November 20 to about 2,000 theatres, one day before the 50th anniversary of the original film's 1976 release, after a limited run that averaged $17,024 per theatre. The weekend guide filed below records it at 87 percent certified fresh with critics and 98 percent with audiences—this weekend's best scores by a wide margin, in that account—with tracking toward an opening of about $12 million. The desk has not seen either film; it files the week's cinema record as two audience leads of different sizes, one of them a franchise record.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
