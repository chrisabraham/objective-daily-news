Venice Closes in Minor Keys, Abu Dhabi Opens Under Frieze, and Ayutthaya Lights Its First Krathongs

Three closings stand on one Sunday and four openings stand within four days of them: Venice ends six months in minor keys, Abu Dhabi ends its first four days under a new name, and Tallinn ends its thirtieth festival, while Tokyo opens a museum by sound, Dublin opens a sea borrowed from Sharjah, Sharjah opens an architecture triennial and closes a calligraphy biennial down to its smallest dot, and Thailand floats the first krathongs of its full-moon week at Ayutthaya. The desk files the week in that order.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0410 ET, Saturday, November 21, 2026. The third Saturday Culture Feature of this paper, filed ahead of the day's watches under the Weekend Features ruling of November 4, 2026. International by house rule: no country's framework is applied to another country's culture. The clippings the report was drawn from are filed beneath it, each stamped subjective.

The Week in Brief

Three of the year’s longest cultural runs end on the same Sunday, and the week’s openings are dated to the day on either side of them: the 61st Venice Biennale, open since May 9, closes on November 22; the first Frieze Abu Dhabi, open since November 19, closes on November 22; and Tallinn’s 30th Black Nights festival, open since November 6, closes on November 22. Against those three endings the file carries four beginnings inside four days: Tokyo’s Museum of Narratives opened its first exhibition, Feel the Sound, on Friday, November 20; Dublin’s Irish Museum of Modern Art opened A Sea for Borrowed Time, drawn from the Sharjah Art Foundation collection, on Thursday, November 19; Sharjah’s third Architecture Triennial, open since November 14, closed its six-day opening programme on November 19 and runs to April 14, 2027; and in Thailand three Loi Krathong festivals open today, November 21, at Ayutthaya, Tak and Samut Songkhram, inside a national calendar that ends at the full moon on November 24.

Venice: Six Months End on Sunday, at Five Euros

The 61st International Art Exhibition of La Biennale di Venezia, titled In Minor Keys by Koyo Kouoh, runs from May 9 to Sunday, November 22, at the Giardini and the Arsenale, in locations across Venice and at Forte Marghera, and this Saturday stands in its final weekend. The Biennale’s own page, filed below, counts 110 invited participants and 100 National Participations, 29 in the historic Pavilions at the Giardini, 25 at the Arsenale and 46 in the city centre, with seven countries participating for the first time and El Salvador participating for the first time with its own pavilion; 31 Collateral Events stand across the city. The exhibition is the one Koyo Kouoh conceived: the Biennale records that, with the full support of her family, it carried out her exhibition following the project just as she conceived and defined it. The final ten days, from November 13 to November 22, carry a public programme of performances, lectures, discussions and roundtables at the Arsenale, with an Assembly of ten artist-led organisations and collectives. The closing is priced for the city that hosts it: admission today is €5 for residents of the Comune di Venezia, the metropolitan area and the Veneto region, and on Sunday it is €5 for all visitors, in the Biennale’s own listing filed below. Two Visitors’ Lions stand established for this edition in the same listing; the desk files the establishment and does not file a result, because none stands in the record at the stamp.

Inside the Giardini, one installation states the edition’s method in built form. Chimera brings together contributions from approximately 150 artists associated with Denniston Hill, the artist-founded cultural laboratory founded in 2004, presented as part of In Minor Keys, in the university account filed below. Its structure is the Exquisite Corpse: alumni artists made works for parts of a collective body, the head for dreams and invention, the torso for voice and transformation, the legs for movement, migration and journeys into the future, without seeing the whole. The setting, designed by Koray Duman, centres on an arena-like gathering space with the works on totem-like armatures, in a passage between the bookstore and the restrooms that the account’s subjects describe as a social condenser. The team also ran an indigo-dye workshop with artists who work with dyers in rural northeast Thailand, and the account records that the magazine frieze named the contribution among the exhibition’s most compelling realisations of Kouoh’s vision. A six-month exhibition ends, in this corner of it, as a body assembled by 150 people who never saw it whole.

Abu Dhabi: A First Frieze, Sold by the Hour

The inaugural Frieze Abu Dhabi runs at Manarat Al Saadiyat from November 19 to Sunday, November 22, and its first public days have produced a record of sales made inside hours. The fair is organised by the Department of Culture and Tourism, Abu Dhabi, in partnership with Frieze, and it replaces Abu Dhabi Art, founded in 2009; it was opened by Sheikh Khaled bin Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan, Crown Prince of Abu Dhabi, in the Media Office account filed below, which presents the edition as building on Abu Dhabi Art’s 17 years and as the emirate’s entry into Frieze’s network of fairs in London, New York, Los Angeles, Chicago and Seoul. The ground reports count 89 exhibitors against 142 at Abu Dhabi Art last year, in the Artnet report and in the Ocula report, both filed below; the disagreement in the fair’s counts is filed whole in the Cocked Hat. Around 40 percent of exhibitors are from West Asia, North Africa and South Asia, and the fair’s director, Dyala Nusseibeh, who previously directed Abu Dhabi Art, calls the partnership a step change in how art from the region is understood, in the Artnet account.

The Masters section sold the fair’s oldest objects first. David Aaron, one of nine Masters galleries showing under the theme of the Indian Ocean as a historic avenue for trade, sold a 2,000-year-old funerary stele naming a woman from Qataban, in modern-day Yemen, for seven figures within the first few hours, the work’s first return to the Arabian Peninsula in 70 years, in the Artnet account; the Ocula account describes the same object as a yellow-calcite alabaster stele from ancient Yemen, its sale pre-agreed, and adds a second seven-figure-class sale, Daniel Crouch Rare Books’ first map to name Abu Dhabi, dated 1832, at an asking price understood to exceed $1 million. Around those sales the reports price the contemporary floor: Timothy Taylor sold out its presentation of Hilary Pecis at $65,000 to $205,000; a Sheila Hicks textile went at $350,000; Pace prices Emily Kam Kngwarray up to $750,000; and Efie Gallery anchors its booth with El Anatsui hangings at $525,000 to $1.8 million. The fair’s own commission stands outside the booths: Dala Nasser’s Qubbat Duris, the Artist Award work, encases a recreation of an architectural ruin in southern Lebanon in earthen-hued textile paintings. Both ground reports also file the conditions: flight paths disrupted and some plans cancelled in the Ocula account, attendance better than expected in the same account, and the fair’s chief executive on the record in the Artnet account that the fair is in it for the long haul. The edition closes on Sunday, two days shorter than its predecessor ran.

Tallinn: 489 Titles End on Sunday

The 30th Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival, POFF, runs in Tallinn and Tartu from November 6 to Sunday, November 22, and its arithmetic is exact: 230 feature films and 259 shorts sum to the 489 titles its programme release announces, from 87 countries, with 107 world premieres and 47 international premieres across the festival and its Just Film and POFF Shorts strands, in the programme release filed below. The anniversary edition places the region at its centre: Focus: Baltics brings Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania into sharper view, presenting recent Baltic films and the teams behind them, in the Baltic Review account filed below. The festival began in 1997 and has held the International Federation of Film Producers Associations’ accreditation since 2014, in the same account, which records its standing as one of Northern Europe’s largest festivals and the region’s only accredited competitive feature festival. Its industry arm has already closed: Industry@Tallinn and Baltic Event, in its 25th edition, ran from November 9 to 20 and expected nearly 2,000 guests from more than 50 countries, in the programme release. The anniversary stands in the city as well as in the cinemas: the Estonian animator Priit Pärn made its design, and a commemorative stamp and a tram carrying the festival’s artwork mark the year, in the Baltic Review account. What remains at the stamp is two days of screenings in two cities, and then the festival’s thirtieth year is a record.

Tokyo: A Museum Opens by Sound

MoN Takanawa, the Museum of Narratives in Minato, Tokyo, opened Feel the Sound on Friday, November 20, as its opening commemorative programme, and the exhibition runs to March 22, 2027. The museum stands inside Takanawa Gateway City at 3-16-1 Mita and is operated by the East Japan Railway Foundation for Cultural Innovations, in the MANA account filed below. The exhibition is co-produced with the Barbican Centre in London, where it ran from May 22 to August 31, 2025; this presentation is its first in Japan, and the Tokyo edition carries new adaptations of existing works and three new works made for the occasion, in the MANA account and in the NiEW account filed below. Its premise is stated in its poster’s tagline, rendered in the MANA account as: that sound reaches the body before the ear. The installations combine sound, vibration and light, and the featured list opens with Sensing Streams, invisible, inaudible: focused edition, 2025, by Ryuichi Sakamoto and Daito Manabe, which turns electromagnetic waves beyond human perception into sound and light; Max Cooper, Miyu Hosoi, Ei Wada and Yuri Suzuki stand among the featured artists. The museum keeps late hours for the opening weekend’s second day: 10:00 to 19:00 ordinarily, and until 21:00 on Fridays and Saturdays, with regular tickets at 2,500 yen, 1,100 yen for visitors aged 25 and under, and 550 yen for elementary to high-school students, in the MANA account. Where Venice closes a six-month exhibition this weekend, Tokyo opens a four-month one, on the same Saturday, at the opposite end of the same trade in attention.

Sharjah: A Triennial in Its First Week, a Biennial in Its Last Room

Sharjah holds two positions in the week at once: a beginning and an ending, in two different art forms. The third Sharjah Architecture Triennial, Architecture Otherwise: Building Civic Infrastructure for Collective Futures, curated by Vyjayanthi Rao with associate curator Tau Tavengwa, opened on November 14 and runs to April 14, 2027, across the Emirate; its opening programme ran from November 14 to 19, bringing 35 international participants together in installations, participatory projects, performances, film screenings, lectures and panel discussions, and closed with a two-day international conference on November 18 and 19, in the ArchDaily listing filed below. The Triennial’s proposition, as the listing states it, is that architecture fosters collective life through social relationships, shared knowledge and civic engagement, and the exhibition now stands open for five months on that proposition. The ending is calligraphic. The 12th Sharjah Calligraphy Biennial, organised under the theme Balance, closed its main run on November 15; its final stop, the exhibition In Due Measure: From Dot to Meaning at the House of Wisdom, opened on November 11 and runs to January 24, 2027, in the UAE News account filed below. The exhibition builds its subject from its smallest unit: five sections carry the visitor from the dot that measures every letter, through the letter, the word and the sentence, to contemporary practices beyond measure, with the pioneers of the proportional system, Ibn Muqla, Ibn al-Bawwab and Yaqut al-Musta’simi, introduced beside the contemporary hands, and tables where visitors draw the dots and practise the letters themselves. One emirate therefore files both ends of the season in the same week: an architecture exhibition with five months to run, and a calligraphy exhibition reduced, deliberately, to the dot it began from.

Dublin: A Sea Borrowed From Sharjah

The Irish Museum of Modern Art opened A Sea for Borrowed Time in its House Galleries on Thursday, November 19, and the exhibition runs to July 4, 2027, with free admission. It is a major exhibition of contemporary Palestinian art made in partnership with Sharjah Art Foundation and drawn from that foundation’s collection, curated by Hoor Al Qasimi, in the museum’s announcement filed below. Eight artists stand in the show, Tarek Al-Ghoussein, Basma Al Sharif, Simone Fattal, Shadi Habib Allah, Rula Halawani, Hazem Harb, Khalil Rabah and Abdul Hay Mosallam Zarara, spanning three generations and working in photography, sculpture, installation, film and painting; the exhibition moves between testimony, memory, lived experience and imagination rather than following a chronology. Its objects carry their histories in plain terms in the announcement: Fattal’s lava stone inscribed with locations in Palestine and Lebanon associated with massacres; Harb’s work centred on the suitcase he carried when he left Gaza in 2006; Halawani’s photographs of everyday movement through military checkpoints, commissioned by the Sharjah Art Foundation in 2017. At the centre of the Zarara focus stands a relief connected to Ireland, discovered in the artist’s studio shortly before his death in 2020 and shown publicly for the first time in this exhibition. A collection assembled in Sharjah thus opens in Dublin in the same week Sharjah itself opens an architecture triennial and closes a calligraphy biennial: the week’s map has Sharjah at three of its points.

Thailand: Three Festivals Open Today

Thailand’s Loi Krathong calendar reaches its full moon on Tuesday, November 24, and three of its festivals open today, Saturday, November 21. The festival centres on floating decorated krathongs as an expression of gratitude and respect for water, observed on the full moon of the twelfth Thai lunar month, in the nationwide calendar filed below. The principal venue this year is Ayutthaya, the former royal capital and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, where the festival runs from November 21 to 24 at the Ancient Palace grounds near Wat Phra Si Sanphet, in the Tourism Authority’s announcement filed below. Ayutthaya revives Chong Priang, an ancient royal tradition of ceremonial lantern-lighting, and centres its nights on Saeng Nathi Si Sanphet, a 45-minute production of light, sound and theatre animating 417 years of the city’s history, performed nightly at 7:30 p.m. before 1,000 complimentary seats; admission is free from 4:30 to 10:00 p.m., a period-style market carries more than 70 local vendors, and today’s opening ceremony recreates an Ayutthaya-era lantern procession. At Tak, the Loi Krathong Sai festival opens today on the Ping River and runs to November 24, its signature a competition of 1,000 illuminated krathongs floating in continuous streams. At Samut Songkhram, the Mae Klong festival opens today on the Tourism Authority’s dates and will float more than 100,000 krathongs made from banana stalks on the Mae Klong River; the nationwide calendar’s dating of the same festival is filed in the Cocked Hat. Around the three openings the calendar is already running: Sukhothai’s candlelight festival has run at the Historical Park since November 15 and ends on the full-moon night; Bangkok’s Rattanakosin Waterways programme runs from November 17 to 29; Chiang Mai’s Yi Peng follows from November 23 to 25, with authorised sky lantern releases on November 24 and 25; and the Authority states its expectation for the period at approximately 6 billion baht of domestic tourism revenue, up 8 percent, on an estimated 1.83 million domestic trips, up 6 percent, in the announcement. The expectation is the Authority’s; the openings are today’s.

UAE News 24/7 — In Due Measure, the Calligraphy Biennial's closing exhibition, at the House of Wisdom

Sharjah, November 11: the House of Wisdom in Sharjah has opened In Due Measure: From Dot to Meaning, an exhibition exploring the geometric and aesthetic foundations of Arabic calligraphy and the precise principles of measurement and proportion that shape Arabic letters, from the dot, the alif and the circle to the formation of words and sentences; running until January 24, 2027, the exhibition marks the final stop of the 12th Sharjah Calligraphy Biennial, organised by the Sharjah Department of Culture under the theme Balance; it presents calligraphic artworks, reproductions of historic Qur'an manuscripts and traditional writing tools across five sections, The Dot: Unit of Measurement, The Letter: Geometry of Beauty, The Word: Rhythm and Composition, The Sentence: From Composition to Meaning, and Beyond Measure: Contemporary Practices; it introduces the pioneers of Arabic calligraphy, Ibn Muqla, associated with establishing the foundations of proportional calligraphy, and Ibn al-Bawwab and Yaqut al-Musta'simi; its works are by Sheikha Khawla Bint Ahmed Bin Khalifa Al Suwaidi, calligraphers Khalid Al Jallaf, Khalifa Al Shimi and Humaid Al Saadi, and Ali Al Hammadi, alongside a sculpture by Nasser Al Salem from the Barjeel Art Foundation collection; and at its Dot and Pen Table and Letter-Writing Table, visitors can draw dots of different sizes to explore letter proportions and follow video demonstrations into guided writing exercises.

IMMA — A Sea for Borrowed Time opens in Dublin, November 19 to July 4

Dublin, November 9: the Irish Museum of Modern Art presents A Sea for Borrowed Time, a major exhibition of contemporary Palestinian art in partnership with Sharjah Art Foundation, in the House Galleries from November 19, 2026, to July 4, 2027, with free admission; bringing together works by Tarek Al-Ghoussein, Basma Al Sharif, Simone Fattal, Shadi Habib Allah, Rula Halawani, Hazem Harb, Khalil Rabah and Abdul Hay Mosallam Zarara, drawn from the Sharjah Art Foundation collection, the exhibition examines how contemporary artists have responded to histories of political resistance, displacement, occupation, memory and solidarity through photography, sculpture, installation, film and painting; it moves between testimony, memory, lived experience and imagination rather than following a chronological approach, spanning three generations; Simone Fattal's In Our Lands of Drought the Rain Forever is Made of Bullets, 2006, inscribes lava stone with locations in Palestine and Lebanon associated with massacres; Hazem Harb's Sustainable Waiting, 2012, centres on the suitcase he carried when he left Gaza in 2006; Rula Halawani's photographic series The Bride is Beautiful but she is married to another man, 2017, commissioned by the Sharjah Art Foundation, documents everyday movement through military checkpoints; the exhibition includes a major focus on Abdul Hay Mosallam Zarara, 1933 to 2020, whose relief connected to Ireland was discovered in the artist's studio shortly before his death and is shown publicly for the first time; and the exhibition is organised by IMMA and Sharjah Art Foundation and curated by Hoor Al Qasimi.

TAT Newsroom — Loi Krathong 2026, led by Ayutthaya, November 21 to 24

Bangkok, November 18: the Tourism Authority of Thailand has announced its Loi Krathong 2026 celebrations, led by the Ayutthaya Loi Krathong Festival 2026 from November 21 to 24, with support for five other provincial celebrations in Chiang Mai, Sukhothai, Tak, Samut Songkhram and Roi Et, complementing celebrations across Thailand during the full moon of the twelfth Thai lunar month; Ayutthaya, a UNESCO World Heritage Site and former royal capital, is the principal venue, and the festival is staged at the Ancient Palace grounds near Wat Phra Si Sanphet in Ayutthaya Historical Park under the theme Chong Priang Loi Khom Chom Wang Si Ayutthaya; it centres on Saeng Nathi Si Sanphet, a 45-minute light, sound and theatrical production animating 417 years of Ayutthaya's history, performed nightly at 7:30 p.m. with 1,000 complimentary seats, 500 reservable online and 500 available at the venue from 5:00 p.m.; admission is free from 4:30 to 10:00 p.m. daily; the festival revives Chong Priang, an ancient royal tradition of ceremonial lantern-lighting, alongside the floating of krathongs, with a period-style market of more than 70 local vendors, and its opening ceremony on November 21 features a recreation of an Ayutthaya-era lantern procession led by Anna Sueangam-iam, Miss Universe Thailand 2022; the Tak Loi Krathong Sai Festival, from November 21 to 24 along the Ping River, features a signature competition of 1,000 illuminated krathongs floating in continuous streams; Samut Songkhram's festival, from November 21 to 24, will illuminate the Mae Klong River with over 100,000 krathongs made from banana stalks; Sukhothai's festival runs from November 15 to 24; Chiang Mai's Yi Peng Festival runs from November 23 to 25, with authorised sky lantern releases on November 24 and 25; Roi Et's festival runs November 23 and 24 with a mass traditional dance of 11,101 participants on November 23; and the Authority expects approximately 6 billion baht in domestic tourism revenue during the Loi Krathong period, an 8 percent year-on-year increase, on an estimated 1.83 million domestic visitor trips, up 6 percent.
