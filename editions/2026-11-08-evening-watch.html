Seven Dead on the Railway at Al-Ayyat, the Six of the Square Are Named in Kraków, and Eisenkot Sets His Four Conditions in Jerusalem

The day’s developments whole, at the evening hour: a railway collision south of Cairo supplied the day’s new dead, its toll rising in steps all afternoon; Kraków’s mourning acquired names and ages for its six, a spokesman’s “highly likely” joining the square to the house in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, and a political challenge to the minister who read the attack aloud on Saturday; a drone fell on rescue crews working the worst single bombing of Ukraine’s week; the Gulf file added projectile impacts reported on Qeshm, projectiles reported fired from Sirik, and a gas field halted by a drone in Iraqi Kurdistan; Jerusalem’s mandate-holder published his four conditions and met no coalition; Asunción’s case widened to at least twenty-seven charged while the repayments to savers reached $160 million; and the American count, the quake count apart, ended the day exactly where Friday left it.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1800 ET, Sunday, November 8, 2026. This edition continues the log from Edition No. 21, the Midday Watch, collected at 1200 today. Where the evening figures differ from the midday’s, the difference is stated here, in the open. The midday edition stands unrevised, as a log stands.

The Evening in Brief

Sunday evening is a file of tolls revised upward, names supplied, and positions stated for the record, and the desk files it in that order. In Egypt, a train running from Giza to Fayoum struck the rear of a Cairo-to-Aswan train standing in the Al-Ayyat district, and the Health Ministry’s count climbed through the day—four dead and 88 injured in the first official figures, seven dead and 112 injured in the ministry’s later count, with one account carrying 125 injured—in the ministry’s figures as reported through the day filed below. In Kraków, officials identified the six killed in the Main Market Square by age and nationality, prosecutors’ spokesman Daniel Prokopowicz said it was “highly likely” the same person carried out the killings in the square and at the suspect’s home, and the square itself reopened late Sunday, in Reuters’ evening account filed below. In Ukraine, a Russian drone struck the rescue crews at Komyshuvakha, where Monday’s bombing of a former school and a shelter killed at least 55 people and about 50 remain missing. In the Gulf, Iranian media reported projectile impacts on Qeshm Island late Sunday and Iranian state television reported three projectiles launched from Sirik toward vessels; in Iraqi Kurdistan a drone attack halted production at the Khor Mor gas field, in the live file filed below. In Jerusalem, Mr. Eisenkot, mandate in hand, set four conditions for joining his government and challenged the parties that called for unity to form it under him; no faction accepted on Sunday, and no coalition exists. In Asunción, the Ueno case widened—at least 27 people face charges, and the central bank has paid $160 million to nearly 1.2 million savers. In the United States the Senate count did not move at all: Mississippi stands at 2,574 votes on a tally stamped Friday, Alaska at 637, and both wait on Tuesday. That is the evening whole: one new disaster counted, one case named, one mandate conditioned, and a week whose every open question still falls on the same two days, Monday’s handovers and Tuesday’s deadlines.

Al-Ayyat at 1800: Seven Dead on the Railway

The day’s new event happened on the railway south of Cairo, and its numbers are a staircase. Train No. 134, running from Giza to Fayoum, struck the rear of Train No. 2010, running from Cairo to Aswan, between Kafr Ammar and Raqqa stations in the Al-Ayyat district of Giza province, the National Authority for Egyptian Railways said; several carriages left the track and some overturned. The chief prosecutor’s office said its initial inspection found the Giza–Fayoum train had hit the last carriage of the Aswan train, which was stationary on the same track, and that a train conductor was among the dead, in the collated account filed below. The Health Ministry’s first count, carried by the wires at midday, was four dead and 88 injured. By the evening the ministry’s toll stood at seven dead and 112 injured, reported by Sky News and Al Jazeera; a further account of the ministry’s figures carries 125 injured, 132 cases received by hospitals in all, 71 of the injured discharged and 43 still in hospital—23 at Al-Ayyat, 13 at Al-Wasta and 7 at Atfih. Seventy-two ambulances took part in the evacuation. The two injured counts, 112 and 125, are both in the record under the ministry’s name and the desk prints both; the dead, at seven, agree in every later account. President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi ordered an investigation and, in his words, that anyone found to have been negligent be held accountable; the prosecutor general sent a team to examine the site, assess the damage and fix the exact toll; railway crews and cranes were dispatched to clear the line. No cause has been stated by any authority. Al-Ayyat carries a record in this file: a fire on a holiday train there in 2002 killed more than 370 people, the worst disaster on Egypt’s railways. The dead of Sunday have not been named.

Kraków at 1800: The Six Are Named by Age, and the Minister Is Challenged

The mourning acquired its particulars on Sunday. Officials counted the six killed in the Main Market Square as a 51-year-old Austrian woman, a 32-year-old British man, three Polish women aged 47, 53 and 61, and a Polish man of about 50, in the evening brief compiled from the Polish and German reporting filed below; five foreign nationals—from Austria, Britain, Germany, Portugal and Russia—are among the wounded, Reuters reported. The wounded count itself did not settle: 15 in some accounts, 16 in others, and Reuters’ evening wording counts 16 wounded in addition to those killed, including the two police officers and the suspected gunman, who remains in critical condition in hospital. The two officers were woken from drug-induced comas after surgery, the evening brief reports, on the same day the prosecutors announced they had regained consciousness. At the suspect’s home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, the three bodies had gunshot wounds and firearms and a significant quantity of ammunition were recovered from the property, prosecutors’ office spokesperson Daniel Prokopowicz said; it was “highly likely,” he said, that the killings there and in Kraków were carried out by the same person, and the investigations are ongoing. Highly likely is the strongest joining word any official has used, and the desk prints it as a spokesman’s assessment, not a finding. The suspect has still not been questioned, and no charge has been laid.

The day’s considered response in Poland was an argument about the reading, conducted in the open. Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński said on Saturday that everything points to the attacker having held “very, very radical views” and being “fascinated by far-right ideologies.” The nationalist opposition questioned the grounds: Przemysław Czarnek, deputy head of PiS, wrote that if Mr. Kierwiński “has misinformed the public, he should face political consequences,” and demanded immediate explanations and access to the information the statements rested on. On Sunday the minister declined to retreat: “We are not passing judgement,” he told the broadcaster TVN24. “Always, there are initial hypotheses, information and evidence… These are things now being checked by the police and I will not elaborate. Nobody is walking anything back.” The desk files the exchange whole: a minister’s hypothesis, an opposition’s demand for its basis, and a refusal to withdraw it, with the investigation that alone can settle the question still running and the man who could answer it unable to speak. The city’s own acts closed the day: the square and its surrounding streets, closed since Saturday, reopened late Sunday; the hourly trumpet call from St. Mary’s tower, suspended for twelve hours overnight, was, the news agency PAP reported, silenced for the first time since 1939; and police tightened security in Warsaw and other cities. National mourning runs through Monday. One figure in this file moved against the paper’s earlier record and the desk states it plainly: the ages of the three found at home are now given as a father of 52 and a mother of 51 in the evening brief, against 53 and 52 in the police statement quoted by Reuters, while the sister stands at 21 in both—and at 20 in the prosecutors’ account the midday edition filed. Three ages, three triangles; the desk chooses in none of them.

Komyshuvakha and Abu Dhabi: A Drone on the Rescuers, and a Table Still Growing

Ukraine’s Sunday entry is a second strike on the site of the week’s worst one. A Russian drone struck emergency workers on rescue missions at Komyshuvakha, near the front line in the Zaporizhzhia region, hitting the former school and the nearby shelter that guided bombs had blasted on Monday as residents waited for humanitarian aid, in the Associated Press account filed below. No one was injured in Sunday’s strike, Ukraine’s State Emergency Service said, but specialized rescue equipment was damaged. The Monday attack’s toll now stands at at least 55 dead, and around 50 people are still listed as missing, Ukraine’s military intelligence agency, HUR, said; many of the bodies recovered have not been identified and DNA analysis is ongoing. HUR said Saturday it had identified some of the Russian service members involved in what it calls the “double tap” strike—the second blow aimed at the same place after the first—and Ukraine has routinely accused Russia of striking the same site in quick succession to target the crews who respond. On Saturday the community buried its dead from the first strike: a farewell ceremony in Zaporizhzhia for six police officers killed at Komyshuvakha, three of them near the former school, and three residents buried near one another where a memorial is planned. No Russian statement on either strike was taken in by the evening. The accounts of both attacks rest on Ukrainian officials and the Associated Press’s reporting from the region; no party that did not issue them has verified the toll.

Two days before the Abu Dhabi table sits, its shape and its claims both grew. France’s confirmation that Europeans will take part acquired its details in the afternoon accounts: Mr. Barrot said he would not personally be at the negotiating table and that Paris had not yet named its representative, in the Euronews account filed below; Russia’s delegation will be led by presidential aide Vladimir Medinsky, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said, in the confirmation account filed below; and the G7 foreign ministers meet in Paris on Monday and Tuesday. The American president’s expectation is also on the record: Mr. Trump predicted a “very big” deal with Russia extending beyond Ukraine to “all of Europe,” in the Associated Press account filed below, and Mr. Barrot said he did not know what the American president had in mind. Russia has suggested there would be no place for European representatives at the table; France says this time they will participate. Both statements stand. No agenda, draft or text has been published by any side, and the talks have not begun.

The Gulf at 1800: Qeshm Again, Projectiles From Sirik, and a Gas Field Halted in Iraq

The strait’s file, which at midday stood at three claimed tanker fires and no names, added reports in both directions on Sunday evening and confirmed none of them. Late in the day, Al Jazeera Arabic cited Iran’s Tasnim agency as reporting that projectiles it attributed to the United States had come down on Qeshm Island, following an earlier report by the state agency IRNA of explosions on the island and sites possibly targeted; American involvement, any damage, and whether the impacts were separate from Saturday’s blasts all remain unverified, in the live file filed below. Iranian state television separately reported that three projectiles were launched from Sirik, on Iran’s southern coast, toward vessels it said were violating Iran’s navigation restrictions; whether any vessel was hit is not established. The Guards’ own statement of the morning, in the Guards’ outlet’s account filed below, holds the three tankers to have exploded and caught fire along an unauthorized route with their systems off, and attributes the burning to the “mischief” of the American army; the second account of the same statement, filed below, notes what the statement does not do—it does not say who caused the explosions and claims no strike by the Guards. No tanker has been named by any party at the evening hour, and the confirmed facts beside the claims stand where the midday edition left them: one rescue of 22 crew by the Emirates, one disabling of the M/V Huda explained by Central Command, neither joined by any authority to the Guards’ tally.

The evening’s one confirmed new fact in the region is in Iraq, and it is an interruption, not an explosion count. A drone attack on Sunday night halted production at the Khor Mor gas field in Sulaymaniyah province, in Iraqi Kurdistan: the drone hit a liquefied petroleum gas storage tank and set it alight, two sources in the regional electricity ministry told Reuters; Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi said an initial assessment found no casualties; the Kurdistan Region’s electricity ministry said the interruption cut gas supplies to five power stations and estimated electricity generation would fall by 2,500 to 2,800 megawatts. No group had claimed responsibility at the evening hour. The land file moved in words at the same time: Mr. Trump said Task Force Iron Gate was operating at full capacity alongside the naval blockade, and the American Treasury has asked Baghdad to close all but two of its border crossings with Iran; Iraq’s foreign minister, Fuad Hussein, said Baghdad was still discussing the request and warned that widespread closures would hurt Iraq’s economy. Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, said Tehran’s two-day proposal remained on the table, calling on Washington to accept Iran’s conditions, including an end to the war on all fronts, and warning of retaliation if the United States resumed fighting. Mr. Trump, to CNN, supplied the other half of that exchange: fresh American strikes were “highly unlikely” if negotiations continued to advance, and the United States had identified 38 bridges as potential targets if military action resumed. No agreement has been announced by any party.

The Saudi–Yemen ledger also acquired its evening entries, each with its owner attached. The Saudi-led coalition said it had destroyed two ballistic-missile launchers in Yemen’s Saada region that it said were used in Sunday’s attack on Riyadh. The Houthi military spokesman, Yahya Saree, claimed the movement had hit Riyadh’s financial district and targeted King Khalid International Airport; Saudi authorities did not confirm the airport was hit, and the Energy Ministry attributed the damage to the Aramco Tower to falling debris and reported no casualties there—against witness accounts, filed at midday, of broken glass on several floors and part of a facade torn away. The Houthis’ tally of coalition attacks in Yemen over the previous 24 hours stands at 91 in the evening’s live file and at 105, with six operations of their own claimed against Saudi sites, in the second account filed below; the two tallies are both the movement’s, taken at different hours, and neither has been verified. The Sanaa figure itself did not move: the Houthi-run health ministry’s count for Saturday’s strikes on homes near the capital stands at at least 33 killed, among them eight women and thirteen children, and 39 wounded, with Saudi Arabia’s Center for International Communication saying it was investigating the report. Three officials told the Associated Press that Syria was preparing to send fighters to Saudi Arabia for a possible deployment along the Saudi side of the border with Yemen, an initial contingent of about 600 by one official’s account; a Syrian source had told state media on Saturday that Damascus had no plans to send troops into Yemen and described its cooperation with Riyadh as training and capacity-building. Both statements are in the file; neither has been acted on in any report taken in.

Jerusalem at 1800: Four Conditions, and the First Meetings Set

The mandate’s first evening produced conditions, not a coalition. After receiving the mandate at the 5 p.m. ceremony, Mr. Eisenkot said he would approach all Knesset factions and set four conditions for joining his government: accepting Israel as a Jewish and democratic state, upholding the values of its Declaration of Independence, supporting universal military, national or civilian service, and recognising Hamas as a terrorist organisation, in the live file filed below. He challenged Likud and Religious Zionism—the parties of the prime minister he would replace and of the unity government Mr. Netanyahu proposed on Saturday—to back a government under his leadership if they stood by their calls for unity. No answer from either party was taken in by the evening. The week’s meetings are set: Mr. Eisenkot was expected to meet Naftali Bennett on Sunday evening and Avigdor Liberman on Monday. The Joint List told the broadcaster N12 it was willing to help end Mr. Netanyahu’s tenure without demanding a place in the government or the coalition, and that it had not yet been asked to commit to voting for an Eisenkot government. The arithmetic stands as the midday edition printed it: the four-party change bloc holds 48 of 120 seats, the Arab-led lists 19 between them, 61 are required, and the clock runs 28 days from today.

The argument that will decide the arithmetic was joined on Sunday, and it is the conscription argument. Mr. Liberman’s stated position, from Friday, is that he is prepared for “painful compromises”—including on the conscription law—to build a coalition of 61 without the Arab lists, and that he has not abandoned conscription, in the account of his announcement filed below. The assessment against it was filed Sunday afternoon: the commentator Amit Segal argued that the price of an arrangement among Liberman, Eisenkot, Bennett and the ultra-Orthodox parties was already established—the ultra-Orthodox would not be drafted—in the afternoon file filed below. The other stated positions stand against any such price: the Democrats’ chairman, Yair Golan, wrote that a genuine partnership requires mutual responsibility and that the ultra-Orthodox must be drafted; the chief executive of Mr. Bennett’s party told its activists that silence does not imply consent, and restated the goal of a revolution in military service. The desk files the positions without choosing among them: a compromise offered, a price assessed, a draft demanded, and a silence that its owner says is not consent. The first test is Monday’s meeting.

Asunción at 1800: Twenty-Seven Charged, and $160 Million Paid Back

Paraguay’s file on its second day is a file of scale: the case’s width and the repayments’ width, printed side by side. At least 27 people face criminal charges in connection with the Ueno collapse, including the bank’s lead shareholder, Miguel Vazquez; former central bank chief Carlos Carvallo is charged with breach of trust, producing false public documents and administration for personal gain, in addition to the counts he shares with the former president; and each of Mr. Peña’s two charges—breach of trust and administration for personal gain—could carry a sentence of up to ten years, prosecutor Nancy Salomón said, in the evening account published at 15:39 ET and filed below. The sums are now in the record: Ueno administered around $850 million in public money, including roughly $355 million in state pension funds, on the Reuters figures the account carries; the public and social-security funds it held in September 2026 are put at about 3.6 trillion guaraníes, with $163.8 million of the Itaipú hydroelectric dam’s funds among them, in the wire account and the evening brief filed below. The central bank began liquidation proceedings on November 2 after the bank’s solvency ratio fell to less than half the required minimum, and intervened the next day. On the other side of the ledger, the repayment has begun at scale: the central bank said $160 million had been paid to nearly 1.2 million savers under the deposit guarantee scheme, small depositors first, in sums up to 10 million guaraníes—about $1,600—with larger claims to be covered gradually, and banks opened weekend branches to serve customers. The state oil company Petropar has filed a complaint seeking protection for its assets. Mr. Peña declined to testify at Saturday’s hearing; his lawyer, Ricardo Preda, maintains the plea of not guilty and his own forecast of the case: “We will submit to the investigation and I am sure that when this investigation is over the result will be the dismissal.” The opposition’s stated position is Senator Yolanda Paredes’s: the former president “must go, but not home. He must go to a maximum-security prison.” He remains, on the judge’s order, at the Presidential Escort Regiment. President Pedro Alliana is naming a new cabinet to serve through 2028. The charges are charges; no court has ruled on any part of the case, and the presumption of innocence is printed with them.

Washington at 1800: The Handover Comes Monday, and the Responses Are In

The White House change announced on Friday takes effect on Monday, and Sunday was the day the considered responses were filed. Stephen Miller, 41, deputy chief of staff for policy and the president’s homeland security adviser, becomes chief of staff on November 9; Susie Wiles, who gave the president her resignation on Thursday night and whose last day in the post was Friday, moves to the Trump Political Operation, in the account of the announcement filed below. The president’s words on his choice are in the record: “Stephen’s been with me since the beginning. He gets it,” and, departing the White House on Friday, “Stephen’s been great, and sometimes in life, you gotta make moves.” The Senate Democratic leader’s answer supplies the opposition’s reading in one sentence, as the account’s headline carries it: Senator Chuck Schumer’s verdict is that Mr. Trump “has learned nothing from his historic defeat.” From inside the president’s own party, Representative Marlin Stutzman of Indiana, whose vote share fell from 65 percent in 2024 to under 55 this year, called the promotion a mistake: “If your team takes a beating as badly as we took this last week, you have to fire people,” in the evening brief filed below. The reading from Paris, filed Friday, is that the appointment is a doubling down—a retreat into the inner circle after a majority of voters punished the president’s choices at the midterms. The desk files the four readings without adopting one: the president’s loyalty, the opposition’s verdict, a congressman’s dissent from his own side, and an ally-capital’s interpretation. What is not in dispute is the sequence—a resignation offered Thursday night, an announcement Friday, a handover Monday. The Federal Register published nothing on November 7 or November 8, in the Sunday situation report filed below; the weekend’s Washington record is personnel and statements, and the desk files it as such.

The Count at 1800: Frozen at Friday’s Figures, the Estimates Widen

Neither Senate race moved on Sunday, and the correction this watch owes is that nothing has moved since Friday evening. Mississippi’s tally stands where it stood at midday: Scott Colom, the Democrat, at 467,930 votes to the incumbent Republican Cindy Hyde-Smith’s 465,356—a margin of 2,574 on 953,314 counted, 97 percent of the estimated total—and the Sunday situation report, filed below, dates that tally precisely: it is stamped 5:02 p.m. on Friday, November 6, and the results page updated at 3:16 a.m. ET on Sunday shows the same two totals, so no votes have been posted since Friday evening. What moved on Sunday is the estimate of what remains, and the estimates do not agree: the state Republican chairman, Mike Hurst, told CNN that between 23,000 and 25,000 affidavit or mail-in ballots were outstanding, a wider figure than the 10,000 to 15,000 provisional ballots carried on Saturday and wider than the roughly 3,139 mail ballots the Sunday video roundup counts as outstanding. The desk prints the three figures with their owners and chooses in none of them; the calendar is the settled part—mailed ballots must be received, and affidavit voters must cure a missing identification, by 5 p.m. on Tuesday, November 10, counties certify by November 13, Mississippi law provides no recount for this race, and a plurality wins. Alaska stands at 637 first-choice votes, Mary Peltola ahead of Dan Sullivan at 48.2 percent to 47.9 with about 74 percent counted; the next count is expected Monday or Tuesday, and the ranked-choice tabulation is set for November 18 if no candidate clears 50 percent. The ledgers above the two races stand at 252 Democrats to 161 Republicans in the House, with 22 House races uncalled, and 54 to 44 in the Senate, in the Associated Press ledger the Sunday report reads. The prediction market’s prices, printed as the venue’s and not the desk’s, put Mr. Colom at 99.0 percent and Ms. Peltola at 98.6—a forecast standing beside a frozen count. At 1800 on the fifth day after the election, neither race has been called.

Shizuoka at 1800: The Count of the Hurt Settles at 22

Central Japan’s correction file, which at midday ran from 15 to 24 injured, settled in the afternoon reports. The afternoon official reports list 19 injured in Aichi Prefecture—one serious injury and 18 minor—and three minor injuries in Shizuoka, 22 in all, in the afternoon account filed below; the television count carried in the second file below gives the same division, 19 in Aichi including one serious case and three in Shizuoka, where an 80-year-old woman fell in Hamamatsu’s Hamana Ward. The morning’s lower figure, 15, was the count of an assessment in its first hours; the 24 carried by some accounts remains in the record as the highest figure printed, and the afternoon official division is the one the desk carries forward. The earthquake itself stands as filed: magnitude 5.7 by the Japan Meteorological Agency, 5.5 by the United States Geological Survey, at 1:04 a.m. local time beneath western Shizuoka at about 40 kilometres’ depth, upper 5 on the Japanese scale in Chita, Aichi, no tsunami threat and no deaths reported. The agency’s Sunday acts are also in the file: having regard to loosened ground, it lowered the thresholds for issuing landslide warnings for the municipalities of Aichi that shook hardest, and its standing guidance—caution for about a week against tremors up to upper 5, the risk highest in the next two to three days, and its assessment that this quake’s pattern is unlikely to lead to the Nankai Trough megaquake—stands unrevised. A second, separate tremor, magnitude 4.3 in the Kumamoto area at 10:53 a.m., produced no reports of serious damage or injury. Particulars of the hurt are in the accounts: in Tokoname a woman in her fifties was taken to hospital with a fractured ankle; in Toyota a man in his forties and a woman in her eighties fell and struck their heads and were conscious in hospital. The sleeper train Sunrise Izumo made a temporary stop; none of its approximately 290 passengers was reported hurt.
