The Evening in Brief: A Recovery Promised Whole by Sunday Night, a Meeting Announced Without an Agenda, and a Count That Did Not Move

Saturday was the recovery day, and by evening the recovery had a promise attached to it. The airlines cancelled more than 4,000 flights and delayed 3,000 more in the United States in the wire’s count, filed below, as the system worked off Friday’s nationwide ground stop; USA Today’s count, filed below, put more than 10,000 flights delayed or cancelled on the day together, and the Administration’s administrator estimated as many as 5,000 of the day’s 41,000 scheduled flights cancelled, with full schedule integrity promised by the airlines for the close of business on Sunday. The cause, in the Secretary’s Saturday account, was a contractor’s copper-to-fiber conversion at a remote location and a new router that flooded the network—not a cyberattack, not an artificial intelligence tool. Abroad, the day’s largest filing is a meeting that does not yet exist on paper: the three presidents in Shenzhen on Thursday, announced by the White House on Friday, with no agenda or format agreed in the wire’s account filed below. Europe’s team for the Ukraine talks stands named at the level of national security advisers, and Moscow’s two voices stand apart: the Kremlin will decide when Europe’s intentions are clear, and the Foreign Ministry says the subject was never discussed. In Bahrain nothing moved: the radar stands claimed and denied, and Tehran’s seven conditions stand unanswered. In Washington the Supreme Court’s Friday grant in the asylum case drew its first answers, the Medicaid enforcement clock moved six months, and Alaska produced no new total—138,240 to 127,828, the call withheld, the tabulation on November 18.

The day’s remaining filings are these. Prime Minister Carney, in Manila, reported progress toward a trade deal with Washington and no agreement, with the auto industry as Canada’s stated condition, in the Associated Press account filed below. The International Criminal Court’s closure of the American line of its Afghanistan inquiry stands appealed by its own prosecutors. France counted a fifth weekend of riots, with 121 arrests by 1800 in Paris. Spain’s campaign spent its first Saturday in Palma and on the road, its mean of polls unmoved. And Wall Street, which has no Saturday session, left Friday’s closes standing as the week’s verdict: the broad market up on the week, the chips sold on Friday, and the largest company in the file reporting on Tuesday without its founder. The desk takes the files in that order.

The Airlines at Evening: More Than 4,000 Cancelled, a Router Named, and Sunday Night as the Promise

The recovery was measured on Saturday in the thousands, against the hundreds the overnight file had expected, and the evening’s counts still do not agree with each other. The midday edition carried more than 3,700 cancellations by 1200 in the wire’s count. By evening the wire’s Saturday account, filed below, stood at more than 4,000 cancelled and 3,000 more delayed, with Delta, United and American each cancelling about 20 percent of their flights and JetBlue 40 percent. USA Today’s account, filed below, counts the day differently—more than 10,000 flights delayed or cancelled together—and names Atlanta, Chicago O’Hare, New York Kennedy and Dallas-Fort Worth as the hardest hit. The administrator’s own estimate, in the same account, is the day’s third measure: as many as 5,000 of 41,000 scheduled flights cancelled while crews and aircraft were repositioned. The three figures count different things at different hours, and the desk prints them apart, as it printed Friday’s three counts of Friday apart. What is single-valued is the direction: departures ran all day, no second stop was ordered, and the promise on the record is the airlines’ own, carried in the USA Today account—full schedule integrity by the close of business on Sunday.

The cause gained its mechanism on Saturday. Secretary Duffy said at his press conference that the contractor L3Harris was working at a very remote location to convert a line from copper to fiber; a router that was installed began flooding others on the system, in the USA Today account—instability in radar and communications beginning at 3:36 p.m. on Friday, the stop issued shortly before 4:00, and the skies reopened at 8:15 p.m. The Secretary said an artificial intelligence scheduling tool was not to blame and a cyberattack was not to blame; Secretary Mullin, in the Daily Caller account filed below, repeatedly emphasized there was no indication of a nefarious attack. The first considered responses arrived in the afternoon, and they are filed in the Arizona live file below in the order they came: at 1313 Eastern, Representative Stanton, who sits on the Transportation and Infrastructure Committee and its aviation subcommittee, urged the Secretary to testify under oath; at 1519, L3Harris said it was helping investigate the outage in the network it has been working to modernize. The money ask stands where Friday left it—$50 billion for the air traffic control system, against the $30.1 billion the White House put in writing on Thursday—and one passenger fact stands in the wire’s account: because the Administration was responsible for the delays, the airlines are not required to compensate the stranded. Friday’s totals, for the record the evening closes, stand as the morning corrected them: nearly 10,000 cancelled in the wire’s count of Friday, 9,138 cancelled and 7,068 delayed in the tracker’s, and 9,100 cancelled with 24,500 delayed in the weather service’s.

Shenzhen at Evening: Three Presidents Expected Thursday, No Agenda, and a Host Described as Wary

The week’s largest appointment was announced on Friday and spent Saturday acquiring descriptions, not an agenda. The White House said on Friday that President Trump will meet President Putin of Russia and President Xi of China at the end of his trip to China next week, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum in Shenzhen, and that he will discuss reducing nuclear weapons, in the accounts the midday and overnight editions carried. Saturday’s fullest account is the wire’s, filed below, and its first fact is a negative: down-to-the-wire negotiations over the meeting have not yet yielded an agenda or a format, according to two people familiar with the planning. Its second fact is a distribution of purposes. President Putin sees a chance to secure an agreement to end the war in Ukraine on his terms. President Trump, after his party’s electoral losses, sees a moment for the history books, with possible movement toward a three-way agreement on nuclear arms control. President Xi, who hosts, faces an unpredictable gathering full of risks, in the wire’s phrase, and is described throughout the account as the wary party.

The considered responses, such as they are at this hour, run against the announcement’s size. A senior Administration official said the United States does not expect to get to any kind of agreement, and a second American official said on Friday that the President was not likely to raise the war in Ukraine and that three-way talks were not the proper forum to do so—which removes, in advance, the Russian subject from the Russian president’s own meeting, in the wire’s account. On the American subject, the response came from the host’s side of the table: Chinese academics interviewed by the wire called three-way nuclear talks a nonstarter for Beijing, on arithmetic the account prints plainly—Russia and the United States each possess more than 5,000 nuclear warheads, and China has around 500, although it is rapidly expanding its arsenal. China’s own official word, in the account, is that it is in communication with relevant parties regarding a meeting between the three heads of state, and no more. The comparison the wire’s headline carries is to Yalta in 1945, where the American, British and Soviet leaders formalized spheres of influence; the account notes the comparison is drawn particularly in Russia. The desk records the meeting as expected on Thursday, November 19, in Shenzhen, with its three purposes stated by three capitals and its agenda unwritten at 1800 on Saturday. Nothing in the file gives the meeting a time, a duration, or a communiqué.

Dublin at Evening: Advisers in the Chairs, a Ceasefire Named as the First Priority, and a Subject Moscow Says Never Came Up

Europe’s team gained its level on Saturday, and Moscow’s answer gained its second voice. The team named in Dublin on Friday—the Union’s institutions and the five countries, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Italy and Poland—will sit at the level of national security advisers, in the Meduza account of the Bloomberg report filed below: the advisers will take part at the technical level, a senior European official said, and Europe has yet to appoint representatives for negotiations at the highest level. The Commission President’s Saturday statement, carried in the accounts filed below, names the European order of business: a ceasefire first, then security guarantees and postwar reconstruction, with the foreign policy chief’s November 12 position standing beside it, that a ceasefire should come before negotiations on a peace agreement. The chief’s own claim to the envoy’s chair, made on Friday, stands unanswered by the Dublin decision, which named institutions and countries and no person.

Against the team stand the two Moscow statements, and the evening’s work is to keep them separate, because they do not say the same thing. The Kremlin spokesman’s position, from Friday, is conditional: no decision has been made on an expanded format, and a concrete decision could come once the Kremlin understands the Europeans’ true intentions and willingness to help advance the peace process. The Foreign Ministry’s position, in the Zot account filed below, is a denial of the premise: spokeswoman Zakharova told the German press agency that, as far as the Ministry was aware, the issue of European representation was not discussed during the Moscow talks at all, and that no Foreign Ministry representatives were present—against envoy Kushner’s Thursday statement that President Putin had voiced willingness to have the Europeans join. The Institute’s November 13 assessment, filed below, reads the two statements together and adds the aide’s: Moscow, in its reading, will accept European participation on a new European security architecture and not on resolving the war in Ukraine, and hopes the Europeans will agree the parameters of that architecture, with discussions with the Americans to begin at once. The Baltic reading of the Dublin list, carried in the overnight file, stands as the team’s first internal response: Poland is the only eastern-flank state at the table, and the Baltic states and Finland sit in it only through the Union’s institutions. No date for a next round stands in any account at 1800.

Bahrain at Evening: The Pair of Sentences Restated, Seven Conditions Unanswered, and the Mediators Still Travelling

The Gulf file produced no new exchange on Saturday, and the evening’s entry is the state of the two claims and the one proposal. The Guards’ claim stands as made on Friday: its aerospace force targeted and destroyed the American early-warning radar on the island of Bahrain, in retaliation for the strike on the telecommunications tower in Sirik County. Central Command’s denial stands as made on Friday and restated in Saturday’s accounts, including the Radio Free Europe account filed below: two one-way attack drones were launched toward Bahrain, both were shot down by American forces, and there were no injuries and no damage. Neither side has produced evidence the other accepts, Manama has not spoken in the file, and the desk knows of no third account. The war around the claim stands where the week left it: the naval blockade in force, the strait’s traffic the subject of a mediation, and no ceasefire in effect.

The proposal stands beside the claim, unanswered. Tehran’s seven conditions for a ceasefire and the reopening of the strait were carried to Washington through Qatari mediators, in the parliament speaker’s account to Al Jazeera carried in the Zot account filed below—an end to the blockade, the lifting of sanctions and an explicit announcement of an end to the war among them, with the frozen assets and a halt to fighting on all fronts named in the other accounts—and the speaker’s word is that the ball is now in the American court. The American court’s last stated position is the President’s of November 12, restated in Saturday’s accounts: “We don’t need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed,” with the Vice President directed off the file to domestic work. The mediators, in the accounts, are Qatari in the speaker’s telling and Qatari and Pakistani together in the wire summaries; Qatar is described in one Saturday account as still trying to negotiate an interim agreement that would restore traffic through the strait while leaving the larger questions open. Tehran’s two voices, noted in the Radio Free Europe account, stand in the file unresolved: the Foreign Minister saying Iran will negotiate, and the security chief saying there will be no negotiations until America accepts the seven conditions. At 1800 the desk records a claim, a denial, a proposal and a refusal to need one, and nothing dated next in the Gulf except the mediation itself.

Washington at Evening: The Court Takes the Asylum Case, the Medicaid Clock Moves Six Months, and Congress Is Out Until Monday

Washington’s Saturday was a day of answers to Friday’s filings, taken in a capital whose legislature is out of town. Both chambers are out today, in the Washington day account filed below: the Senate returns Monday at 3:00 p.m. with two votes, the House returns Tuesday, the Supreme Court issues orders Monday from Friday’s conference, and the President’s public schedule listed executive time only. The Friday filing that drew the fullest Saturday answers is the Court’s grant in Mullin v. Refugee and Immigrant Center for Education and Legal Services, No. 26-239. The grant, limited to the first two questions in the government’s petition, puts before the justices the lawfulness of Proclamation 10888—the January 20, 2025 proclamation that suspended entry at the southern border and cut off access to asylum—and the question whether the statute bars the classwide relief the district court entered; the third question, on the class definition, was not taken, in the practitioner account filed below. The plaintiffs’ answer, in the Justice Center’s release filed below, states the record below: the district court and the Court of Appeals both ruled for the plaintiffs, holding the proclamation contrary to the laws Congress enacted, and argument is expected in the spring of 2027. The grant itself decides nothing and changes nothing on the ground: the asylum parts of the judgment have been on hold since August 2025 and remain in effect as a bar while the case is pending, in both accounts.

The second Friday filing is administrative and its clock is the news. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services announced on November 13 an optional six-month hold-harmless period on the new Medicaid work requirements: states that ask by December 11 may postpone coverage terminations and denials tied to the requirements until July 1, 2027, instead of January 1, while still assessing on January 1 whether enrollees meet the 80-hour monthly standard or qualify for an exclusion, in the PoliAlert account filed below. States ready to enforce may do so from January. The first considered response, in the Cancer Action Network’s release filed below, calls the delay encouraging and asks Congress for more: a full delay of the requirements in all states for two years. The same release records a mixed ruling the same day in Massachusetts v. Oz, the 25-state challenge to the interim final rule implementing the requirements. The requirements themselves—80 hours a month of work, community service or at-least-half-time education for most expansion enrollees aged 19 to 65—are unchanged; what moved is the date on which a failure to document them can cost a person coverage, and only in states that ask.

The Count at Evening: No New Total on Saturday, Two Callers Against the Wire, and November 18 as the Next Date

Alaska filed nothing new on Saturday, and the evening’s count entry is the state of the disagreement about a count that stands still. The official total stands as Friday night left it, at about 7:15 p.m. Alaska time: Ms. Peltola 138,240 votes, 49.84 percent; Senator Sullivan 127,828, 46.09 percent; Mr. Heikes 5,885; the second Mr. Sullivan 4,560; and 855 write-ins, on 277,368 reported, with the wire estimating 96 percent counted, fewer than 12,000 ballots left, and no call, in the Juneau account and the full table filed below. Ms. Peltola stands 445 votes short of the outright majority that would settle the race without a tabulation. Against the wire’s withheld call stand two calls made elsewhere, recorded in Saturday’s accounts: a vote-counting service and a partisan outlet have projected Ms. Peltola the winner, on the arithmetic that her 10,412-vote lead exceeds what the remaining ballots can plausibly return and that the ranked-choice projections from the division’s data produce her win in any case. Senator Sullivan’s campaign’s stated position, given to The New York Times on Friday night and standing at 1800, is that it is waiting for every ballot to be counted.

The governorship, by contrast, is called and dated: Mr. Kreiss-Tomkins, at 50.8 percent in the wire’s call of Friday evening, takes office on December 7, in the accounts the midday edition carried. The Senate arithmetic the race completes stands in the Washington day account filed below: 55 seats called for the Democrats, 44 for the Republicans, and Alaska alone uncalled—a 56th Democratic seat if the projection holds, and the ninth flip of the midterms in that event. The dates ahead are fixed and near: overseas ballots may arrive until November 18, which is also the day of the ranked-choice tabulation, and the target for certification is November 25. The desk records, as it has recorded since election night, that a lead is not a call, that two organizations have now called what the wire has not, and that the next fact in this file has a date on it.

The Day Elsewhere at Evening: Progress and No Deal in Manila, an Appeal in The Hague, and a Fifth Weekend in France

Three filings complete the day whole. In Manila, Prime Minister Carney told reporters on Saturday that he had held a lengthy, substantive conversation with President Trump before leaving for Europe and Asia—with the Trade Representative and the new chief of staff on the call—and that the two countries are making progress toward a trade deal after the August collapse, with the possibility of a bilateral deal and no agreement yet, in the Associated Press account filed below. His condition is stated as plainly as his progress: Canada will not accept a deal that undermines its auto industry, which has been battered by the tariffs—50 percent on about $20 billion of Canadian goods, imposed after the collapse and answered in kind in September. In the same appearance he announced that Canada had substantially concluded free-trade negotiations with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations and completed separate negotiations with the Philippines, which is the shape of the visit: progress claimed on the American file and signatures advanced on the Asian ones.

In The Hague, the International Criminal Court’s decision to close the original Afghanistan investigation—the line that could have examined American troops and intelligence personnel, dormant since 2021, and the affiliate inquiry beside it—stands made public on Friday and appealed by the prosecutor’s office, in the Zot account filed below. The judges’ stated ground is capacity: the prosecution had not shown it could effectively investigate the allegations apart from the Taliban inquiry. What stays open is the separate case on Taliban actions since 2021, and the four arrest warrants for Taliban leaders stand unaffected. The decision is not a finding on any alleged crime, and the appeal has no date in the file. In France, the fifth weekend of riots ran through Saturday in several cities, with fires set and masked protesters clashing with police, in The Sun’s account filed below: at 6 p.m. in Paris the Interior Ministry counted 121 arrests across the country. The Saturday demonstrations were against the high cost of living, joined by marches against police violence and violence against children, eleven months of Saturdays short of a presidential election in the spring of 2027—the account’s framing, printed as its own.

Spain at Evening: Palma in the Rain of Polls, 75 and 176 Restated, and a Debate on Monday

Spain’s campaign spent its first Saturday as it spent its first day, on the two numbers its Prime Minister carries from stop to stop. In Palma de Mallorca, before a crowd the video accounts put at nearly 5,000 at Son Fusteret, Mr. Sánchez rallied with the Balearic candidate, Ms. Armengol, and restated the call in the Demócrata account filed below: 75 percent participation on November 29 and the 176 seats of an absolute majority—to vote, to win, and to govern for four more years—with the argument that only that turnout stops a government of Mr. Feijóo and Mr. Abascal. He also asked Mr. Feijóo, in the day’s accounts, to accept the result of the election in advance. The paper’s mean of polls, printed beside the account and dated November 14, stands unmoved from the midday file: of the latest study from each of 15 institutes, the Popular Party 31.4 percent, the Socialists 26.9, Vox 19.1, Frente Amplio 6.4 and ERC 2.5. Against the mean stand the divergent houses the week has already filed—a survey for a conservative daily giving the Popular Party a clear advantage, and the state survey projecting a Socialist lead—and the opening survey’s seat arithmetic of 190 for the two parties of the right together, above the 176 line both campaigns are arguing about. The two leaders debate on Monday, November 16; the campaign runs to November 27 and the country votes on November 29.
