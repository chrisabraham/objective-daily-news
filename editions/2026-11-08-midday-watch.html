Prosecutors Open the Kraków Case as a Terrorist Offence, a Third Tanker Is Claimed in Hormuz, and the Mandate Is Handed Over in Jerusalem

The news at midday on a mourning Sunday: the Kraków case acquired its legal shape in the morning—an investigation opened, a terrorist-offence classification, six murder charges in preparation and possibly nine—while its human counts still disagreed by one in three places. In the Gulf, the Guards’ tanker tally grew from two to three without a single ship being named, and Tehran answered Washington’s new land-routes task force in one word. In Jerusalem the mandate changed hands at 5 p.m. as scheduled. In Paris the Abu Dhabi table acquired European chairs. The count did not move. The markets, closed in America and Europe, read the weekend off Friday’s settlements and one festive hour in Mumbai.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1200 ET, Sunday, November 8, 2026. This edition continues the log from Edition No. 19, the Overnight Watch, collected at 0400 today, and from the Election Integrity special report, filed at 0910 today. Where the midday figures differ from the morning’s, the difference is stated here, in the open. The overnight edition stands unrevised, as a log stands.

The Midday in Brief

Sunday at noon is a file of openings and standings, and the desk files it in that order. In Kraków, on the first day of Poland’s national mourning, prosecutors opened the formal investigation the overnight edition left unopened: the Saturday attack is classified as a terrorist offence, the investigation covers two acts—the murder of six people in the Main Market Square and the murder of three people at the suspect’s home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski—and prosecutors are preparing six murder charges that could rise to nine if the same hand is established for the three at home, in the prosecutors’ Sunday statement filed below. The suspect has not been formally arrested, because his condition in hospital does not allow it; he is under constant surveillance, and no motive has been established. In Jerusalem, the mandate the night announced was handed over: President Isaac Herzog gave Gadi Eisenkot the task of forming Israel’s next government at the ceremony set for 5 p.m. local time, 1000 ET, on the strength of 67 recommendations against 46 for Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu; Mr. Eisenkot has 28 days, and no coalition exists. In the Strait of Hormuz, Iran’s Revolutionary Guards raised their own tally in the morning from two tankers burning to three, still naming none of them, and their spokesman dismissed the American Task Force Iron Gate, announced Saturday, as wordplay. In Paris, Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said Europeans will be represented at Tuesday’s talks in Abu Dhabi, which had been presented until Sunday as a trilateral table. The Senate count did not move: Mississippi stands at 2,574 votes and Alaska at 637 in the Sunday-morning tallies, and both counts speak next on Tuesday. The markets are closed in the United States and Europe; the Sunday reading is Friday’s settlements—Brent at $98.32, West Texas Intermediate at $90.83—and India’s one-hour Diwali session, which closed up. That is the whole of the midday record: a case opened, a mandate handed over, a tally raised without names, a table enlarged, a count standing, and a week waiting on Tuesday.

Kraków at 1200: Prosecutors Open the Case

The morning’s completed act in Poland is a legal one. A prosecutor of the Małopolska Branch of the National Prosecutor’s Office for Organised Crime and Corruption has commenced an investigation into Saturday’s attack, classified under terrorist offences, spokesman Tomasz Waszczuk announced, in the statement carried by the Polish news agency and filed below. The investigation covers two distinct acts: the murder of six people with a firearm in the square, with the attempted murder of others including police officers on duty, and the murder of the three people in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski. Prosecutor General Waldemar Żurek noted that the division handles cases related to terrorist crimes and that both acts carry the potential penalty of life imprisonment. Mr. Waszczuk told ABC News that prosecutors are preparing six murder charges, potentially rising to nine if investigators establish that the gunman was also responsible for the deaths of his parents and sister, in the second account filed below. The three at home were found with bullet wounds, the police spokesman said, so it is likely they died of gunshot wounds; likely is the word in the record, and no finding has been made. The suspect himself has not yet been formally arrested, because his medical condition required hospital transport; he remains under constant surveillance and cannot yet be questioned, and prosecutors say that may not be possible for hours or days. One fact of care arrived with the legal file: both injured police officers have regained consciousness, the spokesman confirmed.

The corrections the midday watch exists to print are in this case, and the desk prints them as disagreements, not as choices. The wounded count does not agree across the Sunday files: the prosecutors’ statement carries 16 injured, including the two officers; the morning accounts from the square carry 15. The overnight edition carried both figures in its own files and printed the disagreement; at noon it stands unresolved. The suspect’s age does not agree either: the prosecutors and the police give 24, as this paper has filed him, while several Sunday accounts give 25, and one gives a birth year, 2002, that supports neither cleanly. The age of the sister found dead at home does not agree: the accounts the overnight edition relied on gave 21, following the police confirmation; the prosecutors’ account filed this morning gives 20, for a father of 53 and a mother of 52 on whom all accounts agree. The desk does not round a disputed fact into print; it publishes the triangle. What is settled at noon is the shape of the case: nine dead in one investigation, six in a public square and three in a private house, a terrorist-offence classification, an interior minister’s reading of radical, ultra-nationalist views that remains a reading, and a country in the first of two days of national mourning, with a Mass for the victims set for 4 p.m. local time at St. Mary’s Basilica and Kraków’s own mourning running through November 11. No motive has been established by any investigator, and no charge has yet been laid against a man who cannot be questioned.

Jerusalem at 1200: The Mandate Is Handed Over

The ceremony the night announced took place on its schedule. President Herzog was to hand the mandate to Mr. Eisenkot at 5 p.m. Sunday at the President’s Residence in Jerusalem—1000 ET, two hours before this edition collected—and the live file filed below heads its 5:07 p.m. Cyprus-time entry, 1007 ET, with the handover itself: Herzog hands Eisenkot the mandate to form Israel’s next government. The arithmetic under the ceremony stands as the overnight edition filed it. Factions representing 67 of the Knesset’s 120 members recommended Mr. Eisenkot, against 46 for Mr. Netanyahu, with United Torah Judaism’s seven recommending neither; the 67 are not a coalition—they are 48 members of Mr. Eisenkot’s four-party change bloc and 19 from the Arab-led parties, whose support as formal partners Mr. Eisenkot has ruled out, in the account filed below. The October 27 election gave Mr. Netanyahu’s bloc 53 seats and the change bloc 48, neither at the 61 required. Mr. Eisenkot’s own statement, posted Saturday evening, is on the record: “Now is the time for the formation of a national emergency government,” and, “I will do everything to make us worthy.” Mr. Netanyahu’s answer, in a video statement the same evening, urged the change-bloc parties to form a national unity government that must include his previous coalition allies—a prospect the accounts describe as unlikely. Mr. Netanyahu remains prime minister while the formation proceeds. The clock is now the fact: 28 days from today, with a presidential extension of up to 14 days possible, and no agreement of any kind announced by noon.

Hormuz at 1200: Two Tankers Become Three, and Iron Gate Is Answered

The Guards’ tally grew in the morning, and nothing else about it changed. The Revolutionary Guards Navy announced on Sunday that three oil tankers had exploded and caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz after travelling on a route it declares off-limits, raising the tally it gave in the night—two tankers, struck on mines, one east of the strait and one within it—by one, in the live file and in the Guards’ own outlet’s account, both filed below. The third fire’s cause is not separately stated; no tanker is named, no flag is given, and no crew is accounted for, by the Guards or by anyone else. The neighbouring facts stand unjoined, as the desk filed them at 0400: the United Arab Emirates National Guard rescued all 22 crew, all Arab nationals and all stable, from a burning cargo ship in the Gulf of Oman that it did not name; the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations agency reported a tanker on fire Saturday about eight nautical miles northwest of Khasab, Oman, its crew evacuated; and United States Central Command identified the ship its aircraft disabled in the same waters on Saturday as the St. Kitts and Nevis-flagged cargo ship M/V Huda, struck in the stern with precision munitions after its crew ignored repeated warnings and tried to run the naval blockade, in the morning account filed below. No authority has linked any of these three reports to any other, and the desk does not link them. Three fires claimed, one rescue confirmed, one disabling explained, zero tanker names published: that is the strait at noon.

The war of statements supplied the rest of the Gulf file. Central Command announced on Saturday a new unit, Task Force Iron Gate—about 20 civilian and military personnel in Tampa, Florida, directed by Admiral Brad Cooper to monitor, detect, and disrupt the movement of advanced weapons, dual-use technology, sanctioned materials, and illegal revenue across Iran’s land borders and the Caspian Sea, in the task force account filed below. On Sunday the Guards’ spokesman, Brigadier General Hossein Mohebbi, answered it to the Fars agency: “The Iron Gate plan is just wordplay. The Americans cannot achieve their goal of imposing severe economic pressure and causing Iran’s economy to collapse this way.” He added that Washington can never achieve the economic blockade it seeks, whatever form it takes, and warned of a harsher response if the United States attacks Iranian vessels. In the second Gulf theatre, the Saudi-led coalition’s Sunday-morning tally stood at five interceptions—two ballistic missiles toward Riyadh, one toward the Eastern Province, and two cruise missiles in the Riyadh and southern regions—with the Energy Ministry reporting limited debris damage to an Aramco office building in Riyadh’s King Abdullah Financial District and no casualties, in the live file filed below. Qatar’s Foreign Ministry condemned the Houthi attacks in the strongest terms on Sunday, citing the two ballistic missiles toward Riyadh, and called for an immediate halt, in the QNA statement filed below; Bahrain and Syria condemned the attacks as well in the accounts taken in. The other side of that ledger also moved: the authorities in Sanaa raised their toll for Saturday’s strikes on homes in Bani Hushaysh district, near the capital, from 27 killed and 36 wounded to at least 33 killed and 39 wounded. Each figure is printed with its owner; none has been verified by a party that did not issue it.

Abu Dhabi at 1200: Europe Joins the Table

The talks acquired participants on Sunday, two days before they open. Europeans will participate in the talks on Ukraine in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday and Wednesday, November 10 and 11, French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said on Sunday, in Reuters’ Paris file filed below: “The Europeans will be well represented; France will be in Abu Dhabi to take part in these discussions. I can’t tell you yet who will be representing France on this occasion, but France will certainly be there.” Mr. Barrot added that no one will speak in Europe’s name without Europe in the room. Until Sunday the meeting had been presented, by the American envoy who announced it and by every capital, as trilateral—Ukraine, Russia, and the United States, the first direct round in months, on post-war security arrangements, de-escalation, and humanitarian questions. Ukraine’s position going in stands as its president stated it Saturday: Russia is intensifying its attacks—36 ballistic missiles and 172 drones in his count of Friday night’s barrage—to lay the groundwork for pressure on Ukraine’s position and its partners’ at the talks, and Russia perceives diplomacy as a form of aggression, in the Arab News account filed below. Mr. Zelenskyy has also said he hopes the round opens the way to a meeting of leaders. No Russian statement taken in by noon answers either the pressure charge or the European chairs. What changed at midday is the table’s shape, not its agenda: no agenda, draft, or text has been published by any side.

Asunción at 1200: The Detention’s Second Day

Paraguay’s file did not move on Sunday, and the desk files it standing, with its papers in order. The detention order of Saturday stands through its second day: Judge Humberto Otazú ordered former President Santiago Peña held in pretrial detention at a military facility—the Presidential Escort Regiment in the local accounts—and former central bank chief Carlos Carvallo held in a detention centre, on charges of breach of trust and administration for personal gain in the investigation of the digital lender Ueno, whose collapse has left millions of customers struggling to access their accounts, in the Reuters account filed below. The judge said detention was necessary to ensure Mr. Peña remained in the country while the case proceeded. Mr. Peña’s attorney, Ricardo Preda, told journalists he is pleading not guilty. Prosecutors are reported to be seeking a freeze of the former president’s assets, a request no ruling taken in by noon has answered. The sequence stands as filed: the central bank resolved Ueno on Tuesday, November 3; the Senate removed Mr. Peña on Friday; Vice President Pedro Alliana holds the presidency for the term through August 2028; the charges were laid and the detention ordered on Saturday. The charges are charges. Mr. Peña is presumed innocent of them, and no court has ruled on any part of the case.

The Count at 1200: The Margins Stand, the Snapshots Are Dated

Neither Senate race moved on Sunday morning, and the correction this watch owes is about dates, not digits. The Sunday-morning tracker, updated at 8:16 a.m. Central, carries Mississippi exactly where the week left it: Scott Colom, the Democrat, at 467,930 votes to Cindy Hyde-Smith’s, the Republican incumbent’s, 465,356—a margin of 2,574 votes, 49.1 percent to 48.8, with about 97 percent of the expected vote counted and roughly 30,498 ballots estimated left, in the two-races account filed below. Alaska stands where it has stood since Wednesday: Mary Peltola, the Democrat, at 102,962 first-choice votes to Dan Sullivan’s, the Republican incumbent’s, 102,325—a margin of 637, with about 74 percent counted and the second candidate named Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. holding 3,600 votes, more than five times the margin. Against those standings, the desk notes a figure in circulation this morning: an explainer on the delays, republished Sunday, carries Mississippi’s margin as 1,138 votes—which was Wednesday night’s margin, in the explainer filed below. Both figures are true of their hours; only one is true of this one. A count is a dated instrument, and the desk dates it. The calendar around the standings is unchanged and triple-headed: on Tuesday, November 10, Mississippi’s mail ballots postmarked by Election Day must arrive and its boards of commissioners complete the provisional count, and Alaska publishes its next update; Mississippi counties certify by November 13; Alaska’s absentee ballots arrive through November 18, when the ranked-choice tabulation is set, no candidate having cleared 50 percent. Colorado’s 4th District stands uncalled with the two Senate races, the Democrat, Eileen Laubacher, ahead by 3,172 votes in Friday’s figures, no newer figure taken in by noon. Democrats hold at least 54 Senate seats whatever the two races decide. At noon on the fifth day, neither race has been called.

Shizuoka at 1200: The Count of the Hurt Settles Into a Range

Central Japan’s morning produced the day’s other correction file: not a new event, but a settling count. The earthquake itself stands as the overnight edition filed it: magnitude 5.7 by the Japan Meteorological Agency, 5.5 by the United States Geological Survey, at 1:04 a.m. local time beneath western Shizuoka Prefecture at a depth of about 40 kilometres, upper 5 on the Japanese intensity scale in Chita, Aichi—the strongest shaking in western Shizuoka in 107 years and in Aichi since 1997—with no tsunami threat and no deaths reported. The injured count rose through the morning and the accounts do not land on one figure. The Seoul account, drawing on the meteorological agency and NHK, counts 15 injured—12 in Aichi, hurt in falls and by blows to the head, and three in Shizuoka, in the morning file filed below. The wire account carried in India counts at least 22 injured, with shattered storefront windows and disrupted trains, in the second file below; local papers and broadcasters in the region have carried 24. The desk publishes the range, 15 to 24, and notes that the counts were taken at different hours of an assessment still under way. The agency’s standing guidance is the settled part: caution for about a week against tremors up to upper 5, the risk highest in the next two to three days, and its assessment that the quake’s pattern differs from, and is unlikely to lead to, the Nankai Trough megaquake.

The Markets’ Reading: Friday’s Settlements, One Festive Hour, and a Monday

There is no American or European session to report on a Sunday, so the desk reports the readings the weekend published. On the settlements, the Sunday accounts agree to the cent: Brent crude closed Friday at $98.32 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate at $90.83, in the Saudi Sunday-morning snapshot and in the Sunday markets newsletter, both filed below, the snapshot noting that Saudi markets open their week on Sunday with those Friday figures as the last confirmed settlements. On the week’s size, the accounts do not agree, and the disagreement is a matter of baselines: the newsletter measures Brent down about 17 percent from $117.64 at the end of the week before; this paper’s Saturday file measured the same barrel down 13.3 percent on the week from its own baseline, and a Sunday market table prints 12.7 percent for Brent and 11.9 for West Texas Intermediate. The settlements are the point; the percentages are the triangle, and the desk prints both. Underneath every measure sits the same fact the newsletter states plainly: expectations of de-escalation in the Iran conflict took the geopolitical premium out of the barrel during the week, and the tanker fires around the Gulf mean the supply risk has not disappeared—a sentence written before the Guards raised their tally to three this morning, and the first thing Monday’s open will reprice.

The one session the world did hold on Sunday was seventy minutes long and festive. India’s exchanges opened for the Diwali Muhurat session—pre-open at 2:05 p.m. in Mumbai, continuous trading from 2:20 to 3:30 p.m., 0350 to 0500 ET—marking Laxmi Pujan and the start of Samvat 2083, the Hindu financial new year. The session closed up: the Sensex rose 400 points to 81,406 and the Nifty crossed 25,100, with Tata Steel and Sun Pharma among the leading gainers, in the session’s account filed below. Regular Indian trading resumes Monday and closes again Tuesday for Diwali-Balipratipada. It is a ceremonial hour and the desk files it as one; its direction, on the day Poland mourned and the strait burned in claims, was up. The rest of the markets’ week is a calendar: Monday’s full reopening, Tuesday’s talks in Abu Dhabi and counting deadlines in two American states, and an oil price that ended Friday below a hundred dollars with a war premium half in and half out.

Devdiscourse, carrying Reuters — a judge orders Paraguay’s ex-president Peña held before trial

Updated November 8: a Paraguayan judge on Saturday ordered former President Santiago Peña held in pretrial detention at a military facility while prosecutors investigate his ties to the digital lender Ueno, whose collapse has left millions of customers struggling to access their accounts. Peña’s attorney, Ricardo Preda, told journalists that Peña was pleading not guilty. Otazú also ordered former central bank chief Carlos Carvallo to pretrial detention in a holding centre, and Peña had arrived at the Attorney General’s Office earlier on Saturday to appear before prosecutors following his removal from power on Friday.

Seoul Economic Daily — the Japan quake rattles Shizuoka with its strongest shaking in 107 years

Western Shizuoka Prefecture recorded shaking of upper 5 on the Japanese intensity scale for the first time in 107 years, and Aichi its strongest in 29 years. According to the Japan Meteorological Agency, a magnitude 5.7 earthquake struck western Shizuoka at about 1:04 a.m. on the 8th, its focus at a depth of 40 kilometres, with no tsunami threat identified. Injuries were reported across the area: according to NHK and other broadcasters, at least 12 people in Aichi Prefecture were hurt after falling or striking their heads, and three more were injured in Shizuoka—15 in all. At an early-morning news conference the agency said caution was needed for about a week against quakes of up to upper 5 intensity, the likelihood of a large earthquake particularly high over the next two to three days, while it assessed the quake as unlikely to lead to the Nankai Trough megaquake Japan has long feared, and train services were disrupted.
