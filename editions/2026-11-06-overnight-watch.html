The Night File — The Desk’s Report

Objective

Tokyo Closes in a Bear Market, Brent Crosses a Hundred, and the Count Waits

At 0400 the night’s work is finished in Asia and not begun in Washington. Tokyo and Seoul have closed about 6 percent lower, Brent has briefly crossed $100 a barrel on the tanker file and stands just under it, gold has fallen with the shares it usually shelters from, the president has said he will soon decide whether to resume all-out war in Iran, and no race was called in the night. Friday’s first American fact is set for 0830: the October payrolls report.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0400 ET, Friday, November 6, 2026. This edition continues the log from Edition No. 9, the Midnight Watch, which carried Asia’s Friday session in its morning state. The closes printed here replace the morning figures; the midnight edition stands unrevised, as a log stands.

The Night in Brief

No race was called in the night, in either chamber, in any state. The Senate stands where it has stood since Wednesday: 54 seats for the Democrats and 44 for the Republicans, with Alaska and Mississippi uncalled. The governorships stand at 28 for the Democrats, with Alaska and Kansas open. Colorado’s 4th District stands in its latest state with the Democrat ahead by 3,024 votes and the race uncalled. What moved in the night was not the count. It was the price of the election, taken a second time in Asia and closed there; the price of oil, carried back over $100 and held just under it; and the war, on which the president, meeting his national security team for the first time since the vote, said a decision is coming soon. The desk files those three in that order.

Asia Closes: Tokyo in a Bear Market, Seoul Off the Floor

Asia’s Friday session is closed, and the closes are the night’s largest settled fact. In Tokyo the Nikkei 225 closed down 3,666.99 points, or 6.00 percent—at 57,410.91 in the closing account carrying the market data filed below, at 57,434.70 in the Tokyo–Seoul closing account, and at 57,420.23 in the morning brief. The three figures differ in the last digits; the fall, the worst day since April 2025 per Dow Jones Newswires’ closing update, does not. The index had closed above 72,000 for the first time in late June; a close more than 20 percent below that record puts it in a bear market, and every closing account records it there. SoftBank Group fell more than 10 percent in the Dow Jones figures and 10.83 percent to 3,549 yen in the closing account; Kioxia fell 8.72 percent to 8,511 yen.

Seoul closed off its floor, after a halt and an emergency meeting. The Kospi fell more than 8 percent in the morning, to an intraday low of 4,911.28, broke below 5,000 for the first time since February 6, and triggered a Level 1 circuit breaker at 9:03 a.m. that halted trading for 20 minutes—the eleventh breaker of the year, per the Seoul closing account filed below. The government convened an emergency meeting to review market conditions, support buying followed, and the index closed down 324.11 points, or 6.02 percent, at 5,059.69; the closing account’s last figure is 5,059.65. Samsung Electronics closed down 6.35 percent at 188,700 won, and SK Hynix down 10.44 percent to 1,132,000 won. The Kosdaq closed down 5.03 percent at 644.27. Of the twenty largest stocks in Seoul, only Naver rose, up 2.87 percent. In Taipei the Taiex ended 4.5 percent lower, its largest daily fall since July, with TSMC down 4.7 percent; the Hang Seng Tech Index was 1.8 percent lower; Singapore’s Straits Times Index was 2.9 percent lower in the Dow Jones update’s last state. The reason the accounts give is the reason Thursday gave in New York: the Democratic victory, read as regulation and delay for the data-center buildout Asia supplies, priced into the same chip names on the next day’s date. No new American fact arrived in the night to change that reading, and none was needed.

The American futures spent the night in two directions, and the desk prints both states with their hours. In the early evening, carrying CNBC’s figures, the futures account filed below recorded S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 futures each down 0.4 percent and Dow futures down 201 points. In the early European hours, the later futures account recorded Dow futures down 0.85 percent at about 51,170, S&P 500 futures up 0.06 percent at about 7,390, and Nasdaq 100 futures up 0.90 percent at about 26,990. A bounce in the Nasdaq contracts and a fall in the Dow contracts is not a recovery and not a rout; it is a market waiting, and what it is waiting for is printed on the calendar. The October employment report is due at 0830 ET. The Dow Jones consensus stands at 59,000 jobs added, against 29,000 in September, with the unemployment rate expected to hold at 4.2 percent; the forecasts on the shelf run from about 55,000 to 65,000, and the private ADP report, already in, stands at 86,000 against 75,000 expected. The report is the first hard labor figure since the sell-off, and it lands in front of the Federal Reserve’s December 9 meeting, at which traders price the chance of a further 25-basis-point increase between 67 and about 70 percent in the files on the shelf.

Oil: Brent Crosses a Hundred, and Holds Just Under It

Brent crossed $100 in the morning trade in Asia and did not hold the round number. Reuters’ Beijing and Perth file records January Brent up $1.29, or 1.31 percent, at $99.58 a barrel as of 0504 GMT, briefly breaching $100 in morning trade, and West Texas Intermediate up 86 cents, or 0.95 percent, at $91.38. A later account, carrying CNBC’s figures at 0730 GMT, puts Brent at $99.90, up 1.67 percent, and West Texas Intermediate at $91.69. Thursday’s settlement stands beneath both: Brent at $98.29, up $3.67, and West Texas Intermediate at $90.52. The week’s bearing stands in the same files and does not move: both benchmarks remain on course for a fall of about 12 percent on the week, after Wednesday’s 8 percent fall on the election result. The rebound is two days old and is carried, in every account, by the same cargo: the tanker attacks in the Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz, and the finding, stated by ANZ’s senior commodity strategist in the files, that flows through the strait are running at under 60 percent of capacity. Against the rebound, JPMorgan’s estimate, carried in the oil account filed below, is that supply exceeded demand by 1.1 million barrels a day in September and by 1.4 million in October. Both facts are in the price at 0400: a strait at three-fifths flow, and a world producing more oil than it burns.

The Strait, and the Decision the President Says Is Coming

The tanker file did not grow in the night; the decision file did. The claims stand where the midnight edition left them: Iran reported three more tankers attacked in the strait on Thursday, on top of the Guards’ report of two tankers and a tugboat catching fire, and the British agency UKMTO’s count stands at at least five vessels reporting incidents since Sunday, at close to the record pace of October. No independent confirmation of the Thursday incidents appears in any file the desk took in the night. What the night added is the president, on the record, in the Oval Office on Thursday, after meeting his national security team on Iran for the first time since the elections. The account of the meeting, carrying Axios’s reporting filed below, records his words: “Now we will make a decision.” Asked whether to go further with strikes to ensure Iran’s nuclear program is dead and that “it will never happen again,” he said: “I would rather not destroy the rest of the country if possible, but maybe I will have to. You’ll know soon enough.” The options before him are stated in the same account as three: new strikes, continued economic pressure, or a deal carrying significant concessions. Central Command is finalizing preparations to resume major combat operations and has received no new directive, per an American official in the account; a third carrier group, led by the USS Theodore Roosevelt, is due in Singapore on Friday for a port call and cannot reach the region before mid-November.

The mediation now has a printed shape, and Tehran’s answer to it was delivered in Moscow. The Qatari mediators’ draft, in the same account, runs on two days: on the first, the United States lifts its naval blockade and releases some of Iran’s frozen funds, and Iran invites the International Atomic Energy Agency to send inspectors to the bombed nuclear sites; on the second, the Strait of Hormuz reopens, and nuclear talks resume on Iran’s stockpile of highly enriched uranium. Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, spent Thursday in calls with mediators from Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, the United Arab Emirates, Oman, and Egypt. Mohsen Rezaei, the secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, spent it at the Kremlin, delivering a message from the supreme leader to President Putin, who stated his readiness to help end the war. Rezaei’s statement after the meeting is the night’s second fixed point: “We told Mr. Putin that we announced seven conditions to the Americans regarding diplomacy, and that until the Americans fulfill our seven conditions, neither the Strait of Hormuz will be opened nor will there be any negotiations.” A separate file records that Iran returned a copy of one nuclear proposal to the United States through the Qatari mediators on Thursday. No agreement was announced. The two tracks—a two-day draft in Doha and seven conditions in Moscow—are both on paper at 0400, and they do not describe the same distance.

It is filed here because Friday will transact the first of it before the Midday Watch collects.

Gold Falls With the Shares; Europe Waits to Open

Gold did the unusual thing in the night: it fell while the shares fell. Spot gold slipped 0.6 percent to $4,063.39 an ounce by 0248 GMT and December futures fell 1 percent to $4,072.90, per the gold account filed below; the Dow Jones closing update records precious metals swept up as investors sold positions to cover losses elsewhere. The metal had touched $4,146.63 on Thursday, a high of more than two weeks, and stands about 4 percent higher on the week even after the fall. Goldman Sachs on Thursday cut its end-of-2026 target by $500, to $4,150 an ounce from $4,650. Silver rose 0.3 percent to $51.91. The accounts give the same two reasons for gold’s fall as for oil’s rise: crude back toward $100 carries an inflation reading with it, and an inflation reading carries the expectation of higher American rates, which is the weather gold does not like. Europe, at 0400, has not opened. Its futures have: contracts on the EURO STOXX 50 were down 1 percent at 0652 GMT, with German contracts down 0.8 percent and British down 0.9 percent, per the Reuters account of the European open filed below. The STOXX 600 stands, in that account, up more than 1.3 percent on the week and just over 1 percent from a record high, carrying less of the AI trade than the indexes that fell in New York and Tokyo. Whether that shelter holds is the European session’s question, and it belongs to the Midday Watch.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective.
