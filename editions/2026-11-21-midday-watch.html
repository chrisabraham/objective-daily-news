The Midday in Brief: Two More Tankers, a Guarantee in Warsaw, and a Count Corrected Twice

The midday’s news is a strait that was struck twice more before dawn, an alliance answer that arrived in person in Warsaw, and a Friday traffic count that has now been corrected upward twice since the evening stamp fixed it. The Maritime Trade Operations centre issued two warnings in 50 minutes in the small hours: Warning 215-26 at 0500 UTC, a tanker struck on its stern 11 nautical miles north of Khasab, and Warning 216-26 at 0550 UTC, a tanker struck in its engine room 17 nautical miles east of Khasab, in the warnings account filed below and in the wire account filed below; neither ship is named, no casualties are reported in either, and it is not established in the file whether the two warnings describe two ships or one. Secretary General Rutte, in Warsaw, guaranteed that the alliance will defend every inch of Polish territory, and a meeting of its North Atlantic Council stands set for Monday in Brussels, in the Warsaw account filed below. The strait’s counter now counts Friday at 13 crossings, in the strait count filed below, against 11 in its overnight revision and eight at Friday’s evening stamp; a second instrument, counting the ships that cross dark, puts Friday at 16, in the tracker account filed below. The desk takes the files in that order.

What the midday settled is the fact of the two strikes, the Secretary General’s words and the Council’s date, the bullring’s count in Valencia, and the week’s prices in the assets that trade on a Saturday. What it left open is the rest: whether one tanker or two was hit, every ship’s name, the bridges’ true state, Beijing’s word, the police law’s referral, the bulker’s refloating, and the router’s verdict, now nine days outstanding.

Warsaw at Noon: A Guarantee Spoken in Person, Adjustments Promised, and a Council Meeting on Monday

The alliance’s answer to Friday’s falls, which the overnight file left at a visit, arrived at midday as words spoken in the capital itself. “I can guarantee that NATO will defend every inch of Polish territory,” Secretary General Rutte said in Warsaw on Saturday, in the Warsaw account above, as the Polish Press Agency reported it; the visit came a day after Poland said Russia violated its airspace with three objects that were probably two missiles and a drone. Mr. Rutte said the head of the alliance’s forces in Europe, General Grynkewich, would do everything necessary to identify any threats to defence and deterrence capabilities and would implement appropriate adjustments if needed, in the same account. Friday’s intrusion came as Poland was hosting military exercises involving allies of Ukraine, a coincidence Polish officials described as not accidental, in the same account. The next step is dated: on Monday, a meeting of the alliance’s North Atlantic Council will be held in Brussels to discuss the violation of Polish airspace, the agency also reported.

What the guarantee does not contain, the file also records. No statement in the midday file names the alliance’s Article 4, and none announces new forces beyond the adjustments the Secretary General promised and the additional assets—airborne early warning aircraft and F-35s among them—that Poland formally requested on Friday under Operation Eastern Sentry, as the overnight file carried it. France’s call for a meeting of the Security Council stands as Friday made it, and Poland’s foreign minister’s signal that Warsaw intends to raise the incident before the Council as well stands beside it; no date for either meeting stands in the file. The non-interception stands explained as the commander explained it: the conditions did not meet the rules of engagement. Settled: the visit, the guarantee in the Secretary General’s own words, and the Council’s meeting on Monday. Open: what the Council decides, what the adjustments are, and whether the Security Council meets.

Washington at Noon: The Guardrails Stand at One Term, and the Chipmaker’s File Stands at Three Fronts

The guardrails’ file did not move at midday, and the desk records the standing as a standing. The one concrete term stands as Secretary Bessent stated it on Friday: humans always involved in military decisions the technology touches, and no major military operation launched without a human being involved, in the accord account filed below, dated today, which restates the Secretary’s words and adds nothing to them: no text, no timetable, and no negotiating agency stands in the file. Beijing’s own words remain the statement of November 19 carried in the watchdog account filed below: the two sides should strengthen communication and keep the technology under human control. That account adds the counterweight in Congress’s own machinery: the leaders of the United States–China Economic and Security Review Commission, citing their 2026 annual report, warned that China’s military could exploit advanced American technology, including through espionage and unauthorized access to computing infrastructure abroad, and cited an April assessment that China’s army was soliciting advanced Nvidia chips for military capabilities, in the same account. Mr. Huang has denied knowing of the alleged intelligence connections, and Beijing has rejected the espionage accusations, in the same account. Settled: the term, and the nearest parallel in Beijing’s words. Open: the text, and how the safeguard is to be applied.

The chipmaker’s file stands at midday where the overnight file left it, with its three fronts now fully stated in the file and no answer from the company in any of them. The first is California’s: Attorney General Bonta’s investigative subpoena, served on Thursday—dated November 19 in the dated account filed below—investigating whether Nvidia or Mr. Huang’s actions resulted in China obtaining advanced chips or confidential information, and whether the company misled investors and the public about the risk, in the subpoena account filed below and in the crash account filed below, which carries the Attorney General’s words. The inquiry remains at the evidence-gathering stage; California has announced no legal finding, and authorities have not publicly said that Nvidia technology reached China through Mr. Huang, in the dated account. The second front is the Commission’s: enforcement staff scrutinising potential accounting issues, on the word of two people briefed on the matter, in the accounting account filed below, with the Commission not publicly confirming the investigation, in the subpoena account. The third is the criminal file beside the company rather than on it: the Justice Department is running a criminal investigation of Megaspeed, the Singapore company whose former executive is at the centre of the matter, over whether Nvidia chips reached China in breach of export rules, in the accounting account. One document widened the ground under all three on Thursday: the House Foreign Affairs Committee released documents showing the United States authorized the sale of nearly 900,000 Nvidia and AMD chips to companies the Defense Department has designated as Chinese military companies, in the documents account filed below. No charges stand in the file on any front, and the standing caution stands: an investigation is not evidence of wrongdoing, and many close without enforcement action. The succession stands unaltered: a search for a permanent chief executive under way, and the board’s stated position that it will not reinstate Mr. Huang. Settled: the subpoena’s service, scope and date. Open: the company’s answer to any front, and each inquiry’s scope.

France’s file stands at midday exactly where the midnight file left it, and the desk records the standing. It has not been promulgated and has not entered into force; the Socialist, Ecologist and Republican group says it will refer the bill to the Constitutional Council before promulgation; no filing of the referral stands in the midday file, and promulgation should follow within 15 days unless the Council is seized, in the same account. Bars Across—France Approves Rebuttable Presumption for Police Firearms Use

France has approved a bill creating a rebuttable presumption that national police officers and gendarmes used their firearms lawfully. The Senate adopted it on November 20 by 205 votes to 110, with 27 abstentions and six senators not voting; the National Assembly approved the text in July. It amends Article L.435-1 of the Internal Security Code and permits firearms use in five situations, each still required to be absolutely necessary and strictly proportionate; the presumption covers national police and gendarmes, not municipal police. An opinion of November 5 from the Organisation for Security and Co-operation in Europe’s democracy office recommended reconsidering the measure. The Socialist, Ecologist and Republican group says it will refer the bill to the Constitutional Council before promulgation; no court has ruled on its constitutionality, and promulgation should follow within 15 days unless the Council is seized.

Surfer—554-Foot Cargo Ship Stranded on French Beach (Video)

The Miro G, a Malta-flagged bulk carrier of 554 feet, ran aground on France’s Atlantic coast early Friday, November 20, near Lit-et-Mixe, planted in the sand while Atlantic swell pounds its hull. Nineteen of its 22 crew were airlifted off; the captain and two crew members stayed aboard. The ship is not moving at present, but as the tide changes it could become unstable, and that is when the risk of capsizing arises, a maritime prefecture spokesman told France 3. The ship carries more than 100 tons of diesel fuel, and an initial aerial survey found no pollution. By Saturday, authorities warned a refloating attempt might take days or longer: the ship’s main generators were unavailable, complicating efforts to pump ballast water, and shallow water could keep a rescue tug nearly 3,000 feet away; the prefecture cautioned that the operation’s success cannot yet be guaranteed.

VesselTracker—Maritime Insights: the Miro G grounding

Ballasting from Bayonne to Rouen with 22 crew on board, the Miro G ran aground on the Landes coast near Lit-et-Mixe in the early morning of November 20, after reporting manoeuvring difficulties on the evening of November 19 and dragging both anchors in gusts of 48 knots from an emergency anchorage 1.8 nautical miles offshore, coming to rest at 44 degrees 02 minutes north, 001 degrees 21 minutes west. Nineteen non-essential crew were evacuated by helicopter and taken to shelter at Saint-Julien-en-Born; three remained aboard. The Maritime Prefect of the Atlantic activated the third level of the maritime response plan, the Franco-Spanish Biscay Plan was activated and the Spanish tug Sar Gavia sailed from Bilbao, though heavy swells prevented it intervening; the Brest tug Abeille Bretagne also sailed in anticipation of a refloating operation. A navy assessment team was deployed by helicopter to study refloating options, a surveillance aircraft identified no pollution, and it was expected the ship would have to be lightened, its diesel and lubricants removed, to be moved again.
