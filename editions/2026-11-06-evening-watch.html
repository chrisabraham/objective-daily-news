The Chips Bounce Back, the Count Does Not Move, and No Decision Comes

The day’s developments whole at 1800: the morning’s payrolls beat stands as the day’s largest settled fact, New York closes mixed with technology up and the Dow down, the semiconductor index recovers about a third of Thursday’s fall, a Federal Reserve president says more tightening is likely, Europe closes lower across the board, no race is called for a third day, and the two decisions the week is waiting on—Iran in Washington, the count in Mississippi and Alaska—are both still waiting at evening.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 1800 ET, Friday, November 6, 2026. This edition completes the day continued in Edition No. 10, the Overnight Watch, collected at 0400, and Edition No. 11, the Midday Watch, collected at 1200. Those editions stand unrevised, as a log stands. Where the evening figures differ from the noon figures, the difference is stated here, in the open.

The Evening in Brief

Friday corrected Thursday in the labor report and did not correct it in the count. The October payrolls figure printed at midday stands at the close: 165,000 jobs added, against a consensus near 60,000, unemployment at 4.1 percent, and the two prior months revised up by a combined 84,000. Around that fact the afternoon produced its considered responses. The chip shares that fell twelve percent on Thursday recovered about four percent. The Dow closed lower for the day and the Nasdaq higher, a split that held from the opening bell to the close. San Francisco’s Federal Reserve president, speaking after the payrolls, said the central bank will probably have to tighten further. Europe, which had no payrolls report of its own, closed down about one percent. In the count, Mississippi’s Democratic lead stands near 2,000 votes in the afternoon tallies, Alaska’s stands unchanged at 637 with its next update set for Tuesday, and Colorado’s 4th District stands wider, at 3,278 votes in the state’s afternoon figures. No race was called. On Iran, no decision was announced. On Ukraine, the Kremlin’s answer stands at no details, and Kyiv’s president asked for guarantees clear on paper. The desk files those in that order.

The Rate File at Evening: More Tightening Is Likely, in One President’s Expectation

The afternoon’s new voice on rates is San Francisco’s, and she spoke after the payrolls. Mary Daly, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, said on Friday that the central bank will probably have to tighten policy further. “My expectation is we’re going to have to tighten policy further,” she said in a television interview carried in the account of her remarks filed below, adding that exactly when and how much is still to be decided. She called the payrolls report good news for American workers, said she favors a gradual approach, and said she is prepared to act if the economy calls for it. Reuters’ account of the same interview adds her description of the week’s technology repricing as painful but orderly. Ms. Daly does not vote on the rate-setting committee this year; she votes next year. Her sentence is filed as her expectation, stated on the day the labor report beat every forecast on the shelf.

New York’s president, speaking in Frankfurt before the report, took the other half of the same position. John Williams said inflation is absolutely too high and has been too high for too long, and that it is essential to bring it back to the 2 percent target in a timely manner, per Reuters’ Frankfurt file. He did not discuss the next move, and said the committee’s last statement had correctly concluded that it was appropriate to collect more information. The two statements together are the central bank’s Friday whole: the goal stated without qualification in Frankfurt, the likely means stated as an expectation in San Francisco, and the timing left open by both. The market’s own reading stands where the midday edition left it, firmer after the payrolls: the probability of a quarter-point increase at the December 8–9 meeting above three in four in the CME FedWatch readings carried at noon, the range standing at 3.75 to 4.00 percent, and October consumer prices, due Tuesday, November 10, as the next bearing. Gold closed the week on the other side of that reading, down on the day in every account—at $4,064.50, down $38, in the closing newsletter, and at $4,061.30, down 1.17 percent against a prior close of $4,109.45, in the macro report timed 1800 UTC.

The Count at Evening: Nothing Called, Mississippi Near Two Thousand

No Senate race was called on Friday, for the third full day. The Associated Press Senate file in its latest state still lists Alaska and Mississippi as too early to call, and the chamber stands at 54 seats for the Democrats and 44 for the Republicans. The House count moved at the edges without a call of consequence in the afternoon. The live House file, updated at 1:28 p.m. EST, runs 254 seats called for the Democrats and 164 for the Republicans, with eight more led by the Democrats and nine by the Republicans, 418 of 435 races called. The tally carrying NBC News’ calls runs it lower: 250 for the Democrats and 161 for the Republicans, with 24 uncalled, and an estimated final of 259 to 176 if the leads hold. The wire’s tracker at midday stood at 249 won and 12 led. Three desks, three hours, one chamber, and agreement on the outcome and on nothing smaller—the sentence this paper has printed every day of the count, because every day of the count has made it true again.

Mississippi’s margin stands near 2,000 votes in the afternoon tallies, and the afternoon’s news in the race is money and procedure. The Caller’s account, carrying the Associated Press figures of early Friday afternoon, puts the Democrat, Scott Colom, at 49.1 percent and the incumbent Republican, Cindy Hyde-Smith, at 48.8 percent, with more than 95 percent counted and a lead of about 2,000 votes. The state account filed below puts the lead at 1,936 after a Friday-morning return from Bolivar County added a net 123 votes, and at 2,010 in a subsequent state report. The midday edition’s newest tally stood at 2,087. The range at evening is therefore 1,936 to 2,087, the leader the same in every state of it. The procedure stands as filed: mailed absentee ballots postmarked by Election Day must arrive by November 10, affidavit ballots are being verified at the county level, and counties certify by November 13. The new entries are the parties’ preparations for the finish: both Senate campaign committees have created recount funds, per the Caller’s account, and the Republican committee has written to Mississippi’s county election officials demanding that affidavit ballots be separated and preserved. The race is being prepared for a dispute before it has produced a loser.

Alaska did not move, and the argument about Alaska did. The tally stands where it has stood since Wednesday: the Democrat, Mary Peltola, ahead of the incumbent Republican, Dan Sullivan, by 637 first-choice votes, with the next major update expected Tuesday, November 10, when more early and absentee ballots are added, and the ranked-choice tabulation set for November 18 if no candidate holds a majority. The afternoon’s new material is the Sullivan campaign’s arithmetic, carried in the Caller’s account: if the ranked-choice tabulation were run today on the votes in hand, the campaign states, Mr. Sullivan would defeat Ms. Peltola 105,037 to 104,709, a margin of 328, because the second candidate named Daniel Sullivan’s voters are expected to rank the incumbent second. The statement is the campaign’s. It is printed beside the standing facts that cut the other way in the same account: about 20,000 early votes and 27,000 absentee ballots in hand and not yet counted, late mail and questioned ballots still to come, and a New York Times analysis, carried in the state account, suggesting Ms. Peltola could retain her advantage after tabulation. Two projections of one unrun tabulation, filed as projections. The count itself will speak on November 18, or sooner if the ballots arriving Tuesday settle the first-choice majority.

Colorado’s 4th District widened in the afternoon, and the trailing campaign’s position hardened with it. The state account updated at about 2 p.m. puts the Democrat, Eileen Laubacher, ahead of the incumbent Republican, Lauren Boebert, by 3,278 votes—48.78 percent to 47.92—wider than the 3,069 in the wire’s midday totals and the 3,024 in the state’s Thursday-night figures. The automatic recount line stands at about 926 votes, and the margin is more than three times it. What remains, in the same account: at least 5,000 ballots still to be counted in the district’s four most populous counties by its estimate, 474 ballots awaiting signature cure, and the cure and military-ballot deadline of November 12, with counties finishing November 13. The district account filed below carries the Boebert campaign’s lawyer’s statement of late Thursday—the campaign is investigating what it calls unusual voting patterns and expects a recount one way or another—and notes that a recount at this margin is paid for by the candidate who asks for it. The race is uncalled. The path the remaining ballots would have to take is narrower at evening than it was at noon, and the campaign’s stated intention has not narrowed with it.

The Two Capitals’ Files: No Decision on Iran, No Details on Ukraine

On Iran, the decision the president promised on Thursday had not been announced at 1800 on Friday, and the preparations reported around it are preparations. The fullest account of the Thursday meeting filed below records the president’s words in the Oval Office after his first post-midterm national security meeting: a decision is coming, the choice is whether to go further with strikes until Iran’s nuclear program can, in his word, be certified dead, or to stop short of destroying what is left of the country. “I would rather not destroy the rest of the country if possible, but maybe I’ll have to. You’ll know soon enough.” The same account and the day’s operations file record the standing military fact beside the words: Central Command is finalizing preparations to potentially resume major combat operations and has received no new directive. Readiness is not an order. The carrier arithmetic is also in the files: the Theodore Roosevelt strike group is stopping in Singapore from Friday and will not reach the region before mid-November. On the diplomatic track, the Qatari draft and Iran’s formal nuclear proposal, sent through Qatar on Thursday and under review in Washington, stand as the overnight and midday editions printed them, with Tehran’s seven conditions unmet and unwithdrawn. Rapidan Energy Group’s Bob McNally puts the chance of fighting resuming at 80 percent, in the operations file’s figure. That is an analyst’s probability, filed as one. At evening the war’s state is the state of Thursday night, one day older: a decision promised, not made; a proposal delivered, not answered; a strait claimed closed, with traffic under 60 percent of capacity.

On Ukraine, Friday added Kyiv’s asking price to Thursday’s distance between Washington and Moscow. The president’s Thursday description stands: an agreement that is going to be big, that could involve more than Ukraine and all of Europe, with no terms and no timeline given. The Kremlin spokesman’s Friday answer stands beside it, in the account of his briefing: there are no details; the envoy, Kirill Dmitriev, has not yet returned or reported to President Putin; and no European proposals on security guarantees have reached Moscow. The new statement is Ukraine’s. President Zelenskyy, in an interview aired late Thursday after two days of allied talks in Brussels on postwar security and reconstruction, said his government wants guarantees clear on paper: “If Russia starts attacking again, what will happen? Who will do what, with what means, and when?” per the Associated Press account filed below. He said he believes Ukraine’s chances of additional American assistance may now grow after the midterm results. The American envoys’ framework is also in the record: five goals, including permanent security consultations and no country holding a veto over another’s alliances, in the account of the envoys’ post filed below, with the Kremlin’s standing position that guarantees can only be mutual. Delegations from Washington, Moscow, and Kyiv meet in Abu Dhabi on November 10 and 11, the first trilateral talks since February, with Vladimir Medinsky leading Russia’s team. Three positions are now on paper where one description stood on Thursday: Washington’s big agreement without details, Moscow’s no details without an agreement, and Kyiv’s demand that whatever is agreed be written down in advance. Abu Dhabi is four days away.

The fine print, in brief. Objective, as this paper uses the word, means the desk is not driven by an agenda, is not an advocate, is not a politician, is not modeling behavior, and is not training up citizens: it is reporting the news as if it were objective. Subjective means the item comes from a source with its own position, as all sources have; it is printed because it is useful information, weighed by the reader. Objectivity is relative; everything is relative, even relativity is relative; absolutism is wrong. No source in these pages is more reputable than a press release issued directly from Langley.
