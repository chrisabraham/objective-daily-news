Three More Dead in Kraków, a Judge Jails Peña, and Tankers Burn in Hormuz

The last entry of the day, filed before the log turns: Saturday settled the detention question in Asunción, where a judge ordered the removed president and the former central bank chief held before trial, and it settled Berkshire Hathaway’s quarter on paper. It deepened the Kraków case, where police searching the suspect’s home found three more bodies, and it closed with explosions across Iran’s Hormuz coast, two tankers reported on fire, and an American disabling of a vessel in the Gulf of Oman—while the larger Iran decision, the Senate count, and the Abu Dhabi talks all stayed open, waiting on Tuesday.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0000 ET, Sunday, November 8, 2026. This edition closes the day continued in Edition No. 16, the Evening Watch, collected at 1800 on Saturday, and in the day’s earlier editions, Nos. 14 and 15. Those editions stand unrevised, as a log stands. Where the midnight figures differ from the evening figures, the difference is stated here, in the open.

The Midnight in Brief: What Saturday Settled, What It Left Open

Saturday settled a detention, a quarter’s accounts, and nothing else it was asked to settle. In Asunción, Judge Humberto Otazú ordered former President Santiago Peña held in pretrial detention at a military facility and former central bank chief Carlos Carvallo held in a detention center, a ruling the Evening Watch left pending at 1800; Mr. Peña’s attorney said he is pleading not guilty. In Kraków, the day’s count of the dead grew by three without a shot in the square: police searching the suspect’s home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski found two women and a man dead, and the prime minister told a late briefing there are strong indications the three are members of the suspect’s immediate family. The six dead in the Main Market Square stand as filed at evening—four women and two men, one of them a British citizen—with sixteen wounded in the police commander’s count, and national mourning begins today, Sunday, November 8, running through Monday, with the city’s mourning through November 11. In the Strait of Hormuz, Saturday evening brought explosions heard on Qeshm Island and at Jask, Sirik, and Bandar Abbas, a report from Iran’s Revolutionary Guards Navy that two oil tankers hit mines on an unauthorized route and caught fire, and an American statement that Central Command late Saturday disabled a St. Kitts-flagged ship that ignored warnings and tried to leave the region after visiting an Iranian port. No American statement by midnight claimed the explosions, and no Iranian statement by midnight explained them as a strike. The larger Iran decision promised Thursday was not announced. The count did not move: Mississippi and Alaska stand uncalled for a fourth day, Colorado’s 4th stands uncalled with it, and the talks in Abu Dhabi open Tuesday, November 10, with both delegations now named. Sunday is Diwali’s main day in India, and Poland’s mourning. The desk files the day in that order.

Kraków at Midnight: Three More Bodies, at the Suspect’s Home

The evening’s new fact in Kraków was found 120 miles from the square. CNN’s updated file, filed below, reports that police discovered the bodies of three people—two women and a man—during a search of the suspect’s home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, about 120 miles north of Kraków, and found many weapons and ammunition in his vehicle. Prime Minister Donald Tusk told the late Saturday crisis meeting in Kraków that there were “strong indications” the three are members of the suspect’s immediate family. That indication is filed as an indication; no identification of the three has been published, and no cause or time of their deaths has been stated by any authority in the files taken in by midnight. The square attack itself stands as the evening filed it: reports began at about 3 p.m. local time, the suspect drove into the Main Market Square firing at random, six people were killed, and police shot and arrested him. The wounded figures and the suspect’s age do not agree across the late files, and the desk prints the disagreement in the cocked hat below.

What Saturday night added on the record is care for the wounded and a country entering mourning. The same CNN file reports the suspect underwent multiple surgeries and is in stable but very critical condition; that the two wounded police officers both underwent surgery and are in stable condition, one shot once and the other three times; that the justice minister said one officer has a non-functioning lung and urgently requires AB+ blood; and that the injured include five foreign nationals, from Austria, Germany, Portugal, Russia, and Britain, in an Interior Ministry spokesperson’s account. The British account records the police commander’s statement that three Polish persons, one citizen of Great Britain, and two persons not yet identified make up the six dead, and the foreign secretary’s statement mourning the British national and the other victims. The interior minister’s reading stands where the evening left it: everything points to the shooter having been influenced by far-right ideology, a matter to be investigated. No charge had been announced by midnight, and no motive has been established. Poland’s president will declare national mourning for November 8 and 9; Kraków’s mayor has declared mourning in the city until November 11. Sunday therefore opens in Poland as a day of mourning over nine dead in one case—six in a public square, three in a private house—with the connection between the two sets of deaths stated by the prime minister as a strong indication and by no investigator yet as a finding.

Asunción at Midnight: The Judge Rules, Both Men Held

The question the Evening Watch left at the courthouse door was answered Saturday night. Reuters’ late Asunción file, filed below, reports that Judge Humberto Otazú ordered former President Santiago Peña held in pretrial detention at a military facility, saying detention was necessary to ensure he remained in the country while the case proceeded, and ordered former central bank chief Carlos Carvallo to pretrial detention in a holding center. The charges stand as filed at evening: breach of trust and administration for personal gain against Mr. Peña, and those charges plus producing false public documents against Mr. Carvallo. Prosecutors allege Mr. Peña used his position to influence agencies and state entities to keep large amounts of public money deposited at Ueno despite his financial ties to the lender; he was a shareholder in Ueno’s holding company until March 2025. His attorney, Ricardo Preda, told journalists Mr. Peña is pleading not guilty and said, “We will submit to the investigation and I am sure that when this investigation is over the result will be the dismissal.” Mr. Peña has denied wrongdoing and said the bank’s problems were a regulatory matter. Mr. Carvallo did not respond to a request for comment in the Reuters file. The two charges against Mr. Peña could carry up to ten years in prison each, the prosecutor said.

The rest of the Asunción file stands settled where the day left it, and the desk keeps the two ledgers separate. The central bank’s Saturday figure stands: the equivalent of $160 million paid out to nearly 1.2 million savers under the deposit guarantee scheme, against a lender that served about three million customers. President Pedro Alliana, sworn in Friday to complete Mr. Peña’s term through August 2028, governs; protests over the Ueno case continue in the accounts filed below, with some sectors calling for early elections. What changed at midnight is narrow and complete: detention, asked at midday and charged at evening, is now ordered. The allegations remain allegations, the plea is not guilty, and the case proceeds with both men in custody—the first former president in this file to pass from removal to a cell in two days, on the record as filed.

Hormuz at Midnight: Explosions on the Coast, Tankers on Fire, a Vessel Disabled

Saturday evening produced three separate reports from one waterway, and the desk files them as three reports, not one event. First, the explosions. Reuters’ Cairo file reports explosions heard Saturday evening in several areas in southern Iran, including on Qeshm Island; that state television suggested the explosions might be linked to Iranian military activities in the waterway; that officials have not confirmed any missile strikes; and that Tasnim, citing a source, said the explosions on Qeshm were not related to missile strikes—while Tasnim also said earlier Saturday that two missiles struck Qeshm Island, with no official information on damage. A second account widens the list of places to Qeshm, Jask, Sirik, and Bandar Abbas, and carries a report of casualties at Jask with no number given, a claim that stands unconfirmed in every other file. Second, the tankers. The Revolutionary Guards Navy said early Sunday, in the Reuters file, that two oil tankers were on fire after hitting mines as they attempted to pass through an “unauthorized route” in the strait; the Iranian military’s late Saturday account, in the Associated Press file filed below, says two tankers hit mines and caught fire and does not identify their flags or destinations. One tanker or two, and at which exit of the strait, are stated differently across the day’s Iranian statements collected in the files below; the desk prints both tellings in the cocked hat. Third, the vessel. Central Command said late Saturday it disabled a St. Kitts-flagged ship after it ignored multiple warnings and attempted to leave the region after visiting an Iranian port, in the Associated Press file—a confirmed American act, stated by the command itself, in the Gulf of Oman. The same file notes the disclosure follows at least two instances in which the command did not announce strikes it had taken in the region. No file taken in by midnight connects the explosions, the mines, and the disabled ship into one operation, and the desk does not connect them.

The day’s other Iran facts are announcements, and they point in opposite directions, as announcements can. In Tehran, the Revolutionary Guards’ spokesman said the self-imposed limit of 2,000 kilometres on ballistic missile ranges “has been lifted for us,” following the supreme leader’s order that threats be answered without geographical limits, in the wire account and the fuller account filed below; both carry the spokesman’s own caution that capability must be demonstrated in practice, and no file shows a missile demonstrated beyond the old limit. In Tampa, Central Command announced Task Force Iron Gate, about twenty military and civilian personnel directed by Admiral Brad Cooper to coordinate with other agencies against the movement of advanced weapons, dual-use technologies, sanctioned materials, and illegal revenue across Iran’s land borders and the Caspian Sea, in the account filed below; the command said its naval blockade has prevented more than $25 billion in Iranian crude exports, a figure that is the command’s own estimate. In Ankara, President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan told President Trump by telephone that tensions between the United States and Iran should be resolved through diplomacy and that Ankara would continue its peace efforts, and that contacts with Kyiv and Moscow should be intensified, in the readout account filed below; the Turkish readout announced no agreement and no new mediation role. The president’s Friday words stand as the latest American position on the talks—progress claimed, no details given—beside Qatar’s statement that significant progress has been made. The larger decision on resuming the war in full was not announced on Saturday. At midnight the Hormuz file therefore reads: a range limit lifted in words, a task force stood up on paper, a ship disabled at sea, tankers reported burning, explosions unexplained, and diplomacy urged by telephone. Only two of those six items are acts at sea, and both are stated by the side that performed or reported them, without independent confirmation in the files.

Kyiv and Abu Dhabi at Midnight: The Delegations Named, the Damage Counted Three Ways

The talks acquired their full cast on Saturday, and the war acquired a fresh damage ledger the same day. The trilateral talks open in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday and Wednesday, November 10 and 11, the first since February. Russia’s delegation will be led by Kremlin aide Vladimir Medinsky and will include Deputy Foreign Ministers Sergei Ryabkov and Mikhail Galuzin, military intelligence chief Igor Kostyukov, and General Staff officer Yuri Zhigarlovsky, in the Reuters account filed below. Ukraine’s team will be led by Rustem Umerov, with Presidential Office chief Kyrylo Budanov also participating, and the United States is expected to be represented by Special Envoy Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, in the second account filed below. The strikes of the night between Friday and Saturday stand as the evening filed them, with the figures unreconciled: President Zelenskyy’s account gives 36 ballistic missiles and 172 drones over Kyiv and twelve other regions, four missiles intercepted, and nine people wounded including a child; the wire’s account gives at least five injured in Kyiv; a city account gives two. Russia’s defence ministry confirmed it targeted the Northern Bridge over the Dnipro, saying it facilitated the transport of weaponry and military equipment. In Kyiv, Mr. Zelenskyy’s Saturday address also carried the corruption file forward: he called the alleged crimes in Operation Carthage “frankly shameful, absolutely unacceptable,” said dismissals at the security service have occurred and more personnel decisions will follow, and proposed legislation requiring all security-service employees to publicly declare their assets, in the accounts filed with the Evening Watch. No agreement, draft, or agenda for Abu Dhabi has been published by any side. Tuesday carries the talks’ opening beside the American inflation report and the count’s next deadlines, and nothing in Saturday’s file shortens that list.

The Count at Midnight: A Fourth Day Without a Call

Neither Senate race was called on Saturday, and the published figures for the two races did not move. In Mississippi, the Democrat, Scott Colom, leads the incumbent Republican, Cindy Hyde-Smith, by some 2,500 votes in the wire’s tally, at about 49.1 percent to 48.8 percent with the independent candidate just over 2 percent, in the Saturday account and the count update filed below. The estimates of what remains run where they ran: roughly 30,000 ballots possibly outstanding in the wire’s Friday estimate, roughly 20,000 in the Republican estimate carried Saturday, about 3,600 mail ballots in the secretary of state’s Wednesday statement, and an unknown number of affidavit ballots. Mail ballots postmarked by Election Day arrive through Tuesday, November 10, voters may resolve ballot problems through the same date, and counties certify by November 13. In Alaska, the Democrat, Mary Peltola, leads the incumbent Republican, Dan Sullivan, by 637 first-choice votes, at 48.2 percent to 47.9 percent with roughly three-quarters of the expected vote reported, unchanged since Wednesday; absentee ballots arrive through November 18, when the ranked-choice tabulation is set, no candidate standing above 50 percent. The legal fronts stand as filed: the Justice Department’s suit over the second candidate named Daniel Sullivan dismissed before the voting, with an appeal pledged, and the Peltola campaign’s Friday records suit over rejected absentee ballots. Democrats hold at least 54 Senate seats whatever the two races decide. In the House, Colorado’s 3rd stands called for Representative Jeff Hurd, and Colorado’s 4th stands uncalled, the Democrat, Eileen Laubacher, ahead of Representative Lauren Boebert by 3,172 votes at 6 p.m. Friday in the district account filed below—a margin outside the automatic-recount line of about 923 votes, with the cure deadline on November 12—while the Boebert campaign says it is investigating what it calls unusual voting patterns and expects a recount. The count speaks next on Tuesday.

The Week, Settled on Paper: Berkshire’s Quarter

The one scheduled report of Saturday arrived in the morning and closed the market week’s accounts. Berkshire Hathaway’s third quarter, in the accounts filed below: operating earnings of $12.705 billion, down 5.8 percent from $13.485 billion a year earlier; net earnings of $22.112 billion, down 28 percent; insurance underwriting profit down 43 percent; share repurchases of $9.6 billion in the quarter, more than double the second quarter’s $4.5 billion and about $14.4 billion for the year; and cash and Treasury bills at a record $377.435 billion at the end of September, up from $365.514 billion at the end of June. The exchanges were closed, and the week stands as filed at midday: the S&P 500 down 2.7 percent, Brent crude at $98.32, down 13.3 percent on the week. Monday’s open waits on the Hormuz file, as Friday’s close waited on it. Sunday carries no scheduled market item. It carries Poland’s national mourning, beginning today, and in India the main day of Diwali, the festival of lights, whose five days run from Dhanteras on November 6 to Bhai Dooj on November 11, with Lakshmi Puja at dusk today, in the festival account filed below. The desk records both observances in one line each, as the log records what the day is, beside what the day did.

The Cocked Hat

Three bearings never give a point; they give a small triangle, and this paper prints the triangle. On Kraków’s suspect, the police accounts give his age as 24 and the updated international file gives 25; on the wounded, the afternoon’s official count is fifteen, the police commander’s evening count is sixteen taken to hospital, and one later account counts the wounded suspect among the sixteen. On Hormuz’s tankers, the day’s Iranian statements collected in the files speak of one tanker ablaze at the eastern exit in one telling and two tankers on fire after hitting mines in two others; the desk prints the statements as statements, attributed, and notes that no name, flag, or independent confirmation stands in any of them. On Mississippi’s outstanding ballots, Saturday’s estimates run from about 3,600 mail ballots, in the secretary of state’s figure, to roughly 20,000 in the Republican estimate and roughly 30,000 in the wire’s; the lead is some 2,500 votes in every Saturday account. On Kyiv’s injured, the figures remain two, five, and nine, from a city account, the wire, and the president. Against the triangle, the midnight facts that are single-valued are these: three bodies were found at the Kraków suspect’s home, and six people are dead in the square, with mourning beginning today; Peña and Carvallo are ordered held before trial, on charges both files state the same way, and Mr. Peña pleads not guilty through his attorney; Central Command disabled a St. Kitts-flagged ship in the Gulf of Oman, by its own statement, and announced a twenty-person task force in Tampa; explosions were heard across Iran’s Hormuz coast and their cause is confirmed by no authority; the missile-range limit is lifted in words, with no missile shown beyond it; both Abu Dhabi delegations are named and the talks open Tuesday; no Senate race was called; and Berkshire’s quarter is on paper at $12.705 billion in operating earnings and $377.435 billion in cash. Saturday settled a detention, a quarter, and a week. It left open a war at sea, a count in two states, a motive in Kraków, and a negotiation in Abu Dhabi. The log turns to Sunday.

Nearly every major race of the midterms has been called, but not Mississippi and Alaska, where the ballots that will decide two Senate seats are still being sorted, scrutinized, and increasingly litigated; neither will change who controls the chamber, Democrats having claimed the Senate on election night with 54 seats to Republicans’ 44 in the called races; in Mississippi, Scott Colom leads Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by some 2,500 votes according to the Associated Press, which has not called the race, with thousands of affidavit ballots in play and Republicans estimating roughly 20,000 ballots outstanding and voters able to resolve problems through November 10; in Alaska, Mary Peltola leads Senator Dan Sullivan by 637 votes with roughly three-quarters of ballots counted, the Justice Department’s suit was dismissed before the voting, the Peltola campaign opened its own legal front on November 6, and absentee ballots can arrive through November 18, the day of the ranked-choice tabulation.

Election Central — Alaska and Mississippi Senate race update: they are still counting votes

Four days after Election Day, two Senate races remain unresolved and both are remarkably close, and neither can change which party runs the chamber, Democrats having secured at least 54 seats; in Mississippi, Scott Colom holds a lead of roughly 2,500 votes, at about 49.1 percent to 48.8 percent, with all Election Day precincts reported but thousands of absentee and affidavit ballots still being processed, the wire estimating Friday afternoon that roughly 30,000 ballots could still be outstanding, mail ballots arriving through November 10, and counties certifying by November 13; in Alaska, Mary Peltola leads Dan Sullivan by only 637 votes, at 48.2 percent to 47.9 percent, with roughly 74 percent of the expected vote reported, the remaining vote including absentee, remote, and overseas ballots, and a ranked-choice tabulation targeted for November 18 if no candidate reaches a majority.

Al Jazeera — what is delaying the Senate election results in Mississippi and Alaska

An explainer of the two undecided races: in Mississippi, mail and affidavit ballots are still being processed under a ten-day window, counties certify by November 13, and the secretary of state counted roughly 3,600 mail ballots outstanding in his Wednesday statement beside an unknown number of affidavit ballots; in Alaska, ranked-choice tabulation is not published until November 18, fifteen days after the election, and nearly a quarter of the vote remains uncounted; as it stands, the Democrats will usher in the 120th Congress on January 3 with a majority of at least 54 seats.

Focal News — the Boebert campaign cries foul as Laubacher’s lead narrows in Colorado’s 4th

Representative Lauren Boebert trails the Democrat, Eileen Laubacher, by 3,172 votes as of 6 p.m. Friday, a margin of 0.83 percentage points, with more than 97 percent of the vote counted and the race still too close to call; her campaign lawyer said the campaign is investigating unusual voting patterns and expects a recount, though Colorado law requires an automatic recount only at a margin of 0.5 percent or less of the leading candidate’s total—about 923 votes at Friday’s figures, less than one-third of the current gap; a campaign fundraising email misstated the recount law; Ms. Laubacher, a retired Navy rear admiral, led by more than 7,000 votes earlier in the count; and her campaign has not responded publicly to the allegations.

Shanti — Diwali, the Indian festival of lights and its traditions

Diwali, from the Sanskrit for row of lights, is the biggest festival of the Hindu calendar; its date follows the lunar calendar and falls on the new moon of the month of Kartik, the darkest night of the year; in 2026 the main day falls on Sunday, November 8, and the festival lasts five days, from Friday, November 6 to Tuesday, November 10 in that account’s span; the high point is Lakshmi Puja after dark on the main day, honouring Lakshmi and Ganesha, with dozens of lamps lit, incense, sweets shared, and gifts exchanged; and the account offers the festival to a European reader as a November evening of light against the early dark.
