The Markets at Midnight: A Morning Bought, an Afternoon Sold, and a Debut That Closed Seventeen Percent Up

The evening file carried the reversal; the midnight file carries its measure. The Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 1,101.11 points, or 2.19 percent, to 49,097.06; the S&P 500 lost 147.80 points, or 2.02 percent, to 7,175.83; and the Nasdaq Composite sank 550.69 points, or 2.25 percent, to 23,879.20, in the close account above, after the Nasdaq had been up 2.1 percent at its high shortly after the opening bell and traded in a range of more than four percentage points. The 10-year Treasury yield fell to 5.051 percent, the two-year to 4.777 percent and the 30-year to 5.400 percent, and gold futures fell $54.70 to $3,682.10 an ounce. The Russell 2000 retreated 2.2 percent, and the indexes hit fresh multimonth lows, in the futures account filed below.

Washington at Midnight: The War-Powers Vote, the Suit Dropped, and a Cause Still Promised

The Senate rejected the Iran war-powers resolution on Thursday, the first Iran vote in either chamber since the November 3 midterms. The resolution, which had passed the House, failed 51 to 48 in the vote account above; three Republicans voted for it — Senators Collins of Maine, Murkowski of Alaska and Paul of Kentucky — and Senator Fetterman of Pennsylvania was the only Democrat to vote against it. Senator Murphy of Connecticut did not vote. The resolution was H.Con.Res.93, passed by the House on September 15 by 220 to 204. Senator Kaine of Virginia said the midterm result reflected Americans’ unhappiness about the war and higher prices for food, fuel and other goods linked to the conflict. The war began February 28, in the vote account.

In Alaska, the government moved on Thursday to end its own case. Justice Department attorneys urged the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals to declare the suit to block certification of Ms. Peltola’s victory moot, and to vacate the lower court’s dismissal and remand for dismissal on that ground, two days after the race was decided, in the filing account above. The challenge centred on a second candidate with the same name as the incumbent, Republican Senator Dan Sullivan; the Alaska Supreme Court had already ruled the second Sullivan’s candidacy is legal. The updated count in the filing account stands at Ms. Peltola 144,486 votes, 50.13 percent, against 131,965, 45.79 percent, for Senator Sullivan, with Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. at 4,765, 1.65 percent, on 401 of 401 precincts reporting and 290,323 voters cast of 605,433 registered, 47.95 percent — against about 144,302 and 50.12 percent in the evening file, a difference the desk prints in the Cocked Hat. Ms. Peltola is the Democrats’ 56th senator and their ninth Senate pickup of the midterms. Certification is scheduled for November 25. The court had not ruled on the mootness request by midnight, and Mr. Sullivan has not conceded. In the House race, Mr. Begich won the ranked-choice tabulation on Wednesday evening, 51.35 percent to 48.65 percent over the independent Mr. Hill, in the House account filed below: the final round stood at 140,346 to 132,961, with Mr. Hafner taking 22,715 first-choice votes, 7.92 percent, and 11,465 of his voters’ ballots exhausted when he was eliminated. Mr. Hill conceded on Wednesday night.

In the House, Representative Thomas Massie of Kentucky spent an hour on the floor on Thursday under a rarely used rule that lets a member respond to attacks on his integrity, reading Speaker Mike Johnson’s denials aloud and accusing him of blocking the push to release the Epstein files, in the floor account filed below. A week earlier Mr. Johnson accused Mr. Massie of a “political stunt,” a day after Mr. Massie’s petition to force a vote on a second Epstein files bill reached 218 signatures. Mr. Massie said Mr. Johnson refused to schedule the Epstein Files Transparency Act and refuses to schedule the second bill, and that the House session was cut a day short, delaying the vote. Mr. Johnson called the first bill “reckless” in July 2025, then voted for it when it passed 427 to 1. Mr. Massie said his bill requires redactions to protect victims, their families and survivors, and that the Justice Department has committed to withholding 3 million files; he said $32 million was spent against him, that he lost his primary after President Donald Trump campaigned against him, and that he leaves Congress January 3. The account attributes its primary reporting to another outlet, and the desk files it as a secondary account.

In the air, the week’s root cause is promised, not yet delivered. The Federal Aviation Administration’s administrator said on Thursday that the government expects to announce this week the root cause of the router outage that led to the nationwide ground stop on Friday, November 13, which disrupted more than 18,000 flights and a million passengers, in the aviation account filed below. The Transportation Secretary has said a Juniper router installed by L3Harris in a remote Arkansas facility triggered the nearly five-hour stop, the longest since September 11, 2001, and the FAA has paused Juniper router installations while testing continues. The administrator said the FTI network that L3Harris has operated since 2002 was in desperate need of replacement, calling it a patchwork, archaic federal telephone network, and that 68 prior Juniper router installations had not led to any problems. The Secretary has asked Congress for another $50 billion for air traffic control and airport upgrades, against $12.5 billion approved last year. The Senate Commerce Committee is owed its technical answers by November 23. Settled: the vote, the suit’s end, and the House race. Open: the certification, the court’s ruling on the mootness request, and the router’s verdict.

Shenzhen and Berlin at Midnight: A Declaration With Four Points, a Call Told Four Ways, and a Bridge Struck a Third Time

The day’s first room has now been read in full, and its text is shorter than its occasion. The three presidents met in Shenzhen on Thursday in the first trilateral talks among the three countries, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum, in the summit account filed below. The talks lasted 90 minutes in that account, against about an hour in a second and 80 minutes including 10 minutes of opening remarks in a third — the desk prints the disagreement in the Cocked Hat. The joint statement, the declaration text filed below, pledges a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing proliferation, reform of the United Nations, increased production and supply of refined petroleum products, and the principle of state consent and the immunities of state officials, with the International Criminal Court and similar bodies not to abuse their jurisdiction. It names neither Ukraine nor Iran, sets no arsenal cuts and no timetable, and announces no lifting of sanctions. The three leaders reaffirmed that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought. President Xi, according to Chinese state media, described nuclear weapons as “the sword of Damocles hanging over humanity.” Chinese state broadcaster CCTV reported the Korean Peninsula situation was discussed, along with Ukraine and the Middle East, in the Seoul account filed below, though the declaration itself makes no reference to North Korea’s nuclear programme. The President, speaking to NBC after the meeting, rated the summit a 12 on a scale of zero to 10, and said on his way back to Washington that it was a great and highly consequential trip, with a news conference to come at noon on Friday, in the return account filed below. He told NBC that he and President Xi discussed artificial intelligence and agreed on guardrails “at least on a minimal basis,” and, asked about President Zelensky’s exclusion, said he would not make decisions about Ukraine’s future without Kyiv’s input. The APEC leaders adopted by consensus the 2026 Leaders’ Declaration, issued as the Shenzhen Outlook, with three outcome documents on trade, connectivity and innovation, in the APEC account filed below; China announced $20 million for APEC practical cooperation, and Vietnam will host APEC in 2027. On the trade truce, no new extension was reported on Thursday: the file stands at the November 9 extension, with the truce as agreed running to January 10, 2027.

Berlin’s reply stands as the evening printed it, and the call to Berlin has now been told a fourth way. The joint statement by Germany, France, Britain and Ukraine called on Russia to agree to an energy ceasefire throughout the winter and to a ceasefire allowing food exports from the Black Sea region, in the Berlin account filed below, and reaffirmed 70 billion euros in military assistance in 2026 and at least the equivalent in 2027, with legally binding security guarantees, defensive in nature and closely coordinated with the United States. The President telephoned the meeting from Air Force One, and the late account, published at 20:57 Eastern and filed in the call account below, adds a fourth telling: three officials said he voiced strong doubts about the coalition committing billions more to Ukraine; another participant described his remarks as general in nature, stressing the death toll and urging governments to stop pouring money into the conflict; two participants in a separate account described an angry outburst, and a third a savings argument. Against all of them stands Prime Minister Burnham’s denial, carried in the evening file, that the call ran that way at all. Chancellor Merz said at the joint press conference that funding for Ukraine’s defence was intended “to enable peace negotiations in the first place and to conduct them from a position of strength,” and said: “We are not providing this strong support to prolong the war. We want to end this war.” Mr. Kushner said Mr. Trump came away believing that peace is achievable. President Macron said the allies should reopen the question of Ukraine’s accession to NATO. Settled: the declaration’s text, Berlin’s text, and the disagreement about the call. Open: the truce’s length, the talks’ date, and the guarantees’ shape.

The war did not wait for either room, and the bridge is the day’s third front. A third Ukrainian aerial attack in four days on the Kerch Bridge halted rail traffic on Thursday, in the bridge account filed below, after strikes on November 16 and 17 shut down the road section. Some rail traffic resumed on Thursday afternoon, according to Russia’s transport ministry, and Russian officials said it will take about a week to determine how long repairs will last. Ukraine’s General Staff said it used Neptune coastal missiles and damaged the railway section, and Ukraine’s Defence Ministry said hits were confirmed near the arch and the railway tracks. President Putin, at his press conference in Shenzhen, said that after the strike on the bridge there would be no agreement on a ceasefire or on not hitting energy infrastructure, and that Russia’s response would be “much more painful” than what Kyiv had done. He said the crossing is used only for civilian purposes and not to transport military equipment or petroleum products, and said he had ordered the bridge closed to the transport of military hardware — against the accounts that describe it as a key military supply route. Russia’s Investigative Committee opened a criminal terrorism probe into the damage.

The day’s strikes ran wider than the bridge. Russia launched anti-ship missiles and 98 drones at Ukraine, with hits recorded at 18 locations, and a daytime drone attack on Kyiv killed one person, a 26-year-old man in the Podilskyi district, and injured five, in the Kyiv account filed below; the Dutch and Austrian embassies were damaged, St. Volodymyr’s Cathedral had its cornices damaged, and cars and a security post were damaged. A second death is reported in the Kyiv region, outside the city, in the bridge account. Russia said it hit two power substations in Odesa, a fuel storage facility in Chornomorsk and a cargo ship bound for Odesa, and a substation in Sumy. Settled: the third strike, the vow, and the day’s dead. Open: the repairs’ length, and what the promised response strikes.

The Gulf at Midnight: A Fire Under Control, Fourteen Attacks in Nineteen Days, and a Decision Deferred to January

Thursday added no new strike to the Gulf file, and the desk records the quiet as a quiet — with one tower struck in Riyadh. The fire aboard the Tara Gas is under control, two days after the Panama-flagged LPG tanker was hit in the engine room by a drone on November 16, in the tanker account above. The ship has been brought into a sheltered bay west of Shishah, on the basis of a heat signature detectable on thermal imaging yesterday but now dampened down, attended by the anchor handling tug Valiant Vortex. Twenty-four of 25 crew have been rescued, two injured sailors taken to Khasab Hospital, and one crew member remains missing. No new statement on Thursday from Oman, India, Iran or the operator was found in the file. The Guards’ claim of two supertankers struck by mines stands uncorroborated and unnamed, as the evening printed it, and a shipping monitor’s note that the figures match a claim from November 12 stands beside it. The crew count on the attacked ADNOC tanker stands unresolved: the company said one missing and 11 injured; India’s shipping minister said all crew are safe and accounted for; Jordan’s foreign ministry says its sailor, Mr. Al-Otaibi, is still missing.

The month’s count now has a number. Iran has mounted about 14 attacks on vessels in and near the Strait of Hormuz in the first 19 days of November, according to a United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations count cited by Bloomberg, in the Hormuz account filed below, including limpet mines, naval mines and anti-ship cruise missiles, which an unnamed American official described as “far more lethal than drones,” following the collapse of Muscat-hosted diplomacy between Iran and Oman on November 17. Brent crude futures have fallen approximately one-fifth in November, and total Middle East oil flows have largely recovered to near pre-war levels through alternative routes and ship-to-ship transfers. IMF PortWatch recorded just 11 tanker transits through Hormuz between November 1 and 15 — an average of 0.7 per day, compared with 665 in the same period of 2025, a 98 percent fall. Hormuz-specific flows fell from about 13.4 million to about 10 million barrels per day in the first 10 days of November. About 80 mines remain in the strait’s historic shipping lanes. Oil settled on Thursday at Brent $91.98 a barrel, up $0.03, and West Texas Intermediate $83.21, down $0.05, its lowest since August 26, in the oil account filed below, after moving through a roughly $2 range; nationwide crude inventories rose by 3.4 million barrels last week. The International Energy Agency said on Thursday that the war could reshape global energy markets for years.

On the question of strikes, the President said on Thursday that he is not rushing. He told Axios aboard Air Force One: “I am not rushing into any decision [on Iran]. We will see what happens after the beginning of the year,” in the decision account filed below, and he has not authorised another major operation. The New York Times reported that Prime Minister Netanyahu is pushing for a joint operation lasting four to seven days, citing concerns that Iran is rebuilding missile and drone capabilities and advancing chemical and biological weapons programs, and Mr. Netanyahu reportedly told Israeli security officials on Wednesday evening that an operation against Iran was “unavoidable.” The White House has requested Pentagon and intelligence assessments of how Iran might retaliate against American strike scenarios, particularly against Gulf oil and natural gas infrastructure, and potential targets under consideration include Iran’s Pickaxe Mountain nuclear complex. Iran said it will not engage on the nuclear weapons issue until the question of the Strait of Hormuz is settled. Inflation in Iran exceeds 90 percent and the rial is plunging, in the decision account.

In Riyadh, the tallest skyscraper in the capital was struck on Thursday. A Houthi attack on a tower in the King Abdullah Financial District caused material damage to several floors and nearby buildings, with no injuries reported, in the Riyadh account filed below; the statement did not identify the tower, but a photograph published by the state news agency showed the PIF Tower, headquarters of the Saudi sovereign wealth fund, its windows blown out across seven to eight floors. The Houthis said they had targeted the financial centre twice on Thursday, first at dawn and again in the evening, and also said they had attacked King Khalid International Airport, Khamis Mushait airbase and a petrochemical company in Jubail; there was no immediate Saudi confirmation of those other attacks. This was the second time in less than two weeks that a Houthi attack damaged a landmark in Riyadh’s financial district, with Aramco’s building hit in a November 8 attack. Saudi aircraft carried out airstrikes on Sanaa early on Thursday. Saudi officials now foresee the campaign stretching on for up to six months. In Gaza, the Israeli military said on Thursday it had completed its ground operation north of the Nuseirat refugee camp in central Gaza and reopened Salah al-Din Street, in the Gaza account filed below; about 6,000 people had fled their homes on Wednesday. Four Palestinians were killed in Israeli strikes on Gaza City on Thursday. More than 1,600 Palestinians have been killed since the start of the ceasefire, according to an AFP tally, and the Israeli military says seven of its soldiers and one civilian contractor have been killed in the same period. The Board of Peace, in its first annual assessment delivered to the Security Council on Tuesday, warned that the agreement risks collapse if its terms are not fully implemented, in the Board account filed below: “A ceasefire that delivers nothing visible cannot be sustained indefinitely.” At the centre of the stall is the technocratic civil administration the board created to run Gaza on an interim basis, which Israel has still not allowed to take up its job. Settled: the fire under control, the oil settlement, and the decision deferred. Open: the missing men, the mines, and the January question.

The World at Midnight: A Debate Without a Winner, a Vote Delayed, a Court’s Word Answered, and Two Counts Unresolved

Spain’s Thursday is one room, and at the midnight stamp the room has finished talking without choosing a winner. Mr. Sánchez and Mr. Feijóo faced each other at 10 p.m. Madrid time in the only head-to-head debate before the general election on November 29, in the verdict account above. El Plural said it ended “without a clear winner” amid accusations of disinformation and dirty tactics. A Sociómetro quick poll of 1,019 people, non-representative, put Mr. Sánchez ahead 58 percent to 42 percent; pre-debate polling averages had Mr. Sánchez about five points behind. Housing dominated, mentioned more than 20 times by each man, in the debate account filed below; Mr. Sánchez mentioned Vox or Mr. Abascal at least 16 times, and Mr. Feijóo interrupted Mr. Sánchez on more than 29 occasions, 10 in the social-policy block. In the defence block Mr. Sánchez put the cost of Mr. Feijóo’s 5 percent of GDP defence commitment at €80 billion a year. Mr. Sánchez in his golden minute: “Spain does not bow to powerful forces” and “Vote with all your soul and all your strength.” Mr. Feijóo: the country “knows how to manage” and “needs a change.” Mr. Sánchez afterwards told Socialist Party members at Ferraz that the debate was “good politics and poor education.” A second debate has been agreed for around November 26.

France’s Thursday ends with its bill still unvoted. The Senate delayed a final vote on the police firearms bill until Friday after left-wing senators used procedural tactics to extend debate past a Thursday night deadline, in the Senate account filed below; the government secured an emergency slot on Friday’s agenda, with 76 amendments still on the table. Senators from the Socialist, Ecologist, and La France Insoumise groups said they would jointly refer the bill to the Constitutional Council if adopted. The Interior Minister, Mr. Nuñez, accused the left of “obstruction” and said the presumption “will simply lift the automatic custody of the police officer or gendarme who used their weapon.” A senator of the left said that tomorrow it will no longer be for the officer to justify his shot, but for the courts and the families to prove he should not have fired. Outside the Senate, several hundred opponents gathered, and a mock cemetery of 35 tombs was installed before the Luxembourg Gardens gates, representing as many victims of fatal police shootings since 2017, according to the organisers. A petition against the bill has gathered more than 700,000 signatures in this account, against 732,086 in the evening file. The retirees marched in Paris from the Montparnasse station forecourt to Place Vauban near Les Invalides, in the march account filed below; the organisers had aimed for about 30,000 in Paris, and no final turnout figure was found in the sources read — the only figure in the file is “more than a thousand people.” The Senate adopted its version of the Social Security budget on Wednesday, November 18, by 201 votes to 124, with 15 abstentions.

The Hague’s word on Myanmar drew its first answers on Thursday. The International Court of Justice ruled that Myanmar’s security forces’ killings and widespread sexual violence against the Rohingya in 2017 amounted to state-sponsored genocide — the first time the court has held a state responsible for genocide. The reactions stand in the reactions account filed below: the UN human rights chief, Mr. Türk, said “This judgment clearly concludes the State of Myanmar stands responsible for one of the most egregious crimes known to humankind”; Ms. Ullah, a Rohingya activist in Gambia’s delegation, said “Today is a day of justice. I am a survivor of genocide… Today the world’s highest court has looked at what was done to the Rohingya and named it for what it was. Genocide”; Sheikh Hasina said “Bangladesh played a central role in the investigations… This is a proud moment for Bangladesh.” By a majority of its 14 judges, the court found six violations of the 1948 Genocide Convention, in the ruling account filed below; the ruling runs 191 pages, the session lasted more than three hours, and the court’s estimate, supported by the evidence, is that 10,000 Rohingya were violently killed in clearance operations, 1.2 percent of the targeted population in northern Rakhine, with 392 villages destroyed and 700,000 fled in 2017. The United Kingdom’s Foreign Secretary, Mr. Miliband, announced the expulsion of Myanmar’s defence attaché and called urgently for a UN Security Council session. Gambia welcomed the ruling with “profound satisfaction”; Bangladesh urged implementation without delay; the United States said it was studying the ruling. Myanmar had not responded as of Thursday evening. Myanmar has two years to submit a report on restitution and compensation; the Court will decide the amount. The ruling is final and cannot be appealed, but the court has very limited means of enforcement.

Off Senegal, the boat’s count stands unresolved at midnight. A pirogue capsized off Yenne, south of Dakar, at about 2 a.m. local time on Wednesday, and dozens are missing, in the boat account filed below. One survivor told police about 100 people were aboard, another told Reuters about 50; bodies recovered stand at one, two and four in different accounts. Nearly 600 people have died this year trying to reach the Canary Islands from West Africa, according to the United Nations refugee agency, and nearly 8,200 migrants using the route had reached the Canary Islands in the first 12 days of November. In Bangladesh, the banned Awami League’s nationwide dawn-to-dusk lockdown brought fresh violence on Thursday, in the lockdown account filed below, with mixed success: the attempted shutdown worked mostly in the southern districts where the League is strongest, and garment workers demanding higher wages also blocked a highway into Dhaka. Police said two members of the ruling Bangladesh Nationalist Party were killed on Wednesday night; houses of two BNP lawmakers in Gopalganj were vandalised and set alight; at least six buses and pickup vans were set on fire along with a section of railway line; crude bomb blasts were reported across six districts overnight. Ninety-seven platoons of Border Guard Bangladesh fanned out across seven districts. In Dhaka, police arrested 107 people suspected of links to the Awami League in 24 hours, including former education minister Nurul Islam Nahid — against 565 arrested in the capital in one account and 537 in another, a difference the desk prints in the Cocked Hat. Prime Minister Rahman blamed India for the unrest, without naming it: “the country across the border, where they have fled to, is their master.” At least four people have been killed over the past two days. Ms. Hasina, 79, plans to return in December; Tuesday marked a year since a court sentenced her to death.

In Brussels, the European Commission confirmed on Thursday that it was targeted by an AI-generated cyber campaign, detected on October 22 and confirmed after investigation on October 26, in the cyber account filed below. Several internal applications were taken offline as a precaution and most restored; whether staff data was compromised is not established, and the investigation has not detected any evidence of a data breach affecting Commission staff. Politico, which reported the incident first, said systems taken offline included Sysper, the human resources platform for payslips and time management, and a system for medical reimbursements; other accounts add a project-management system and translation services, with more than 10 days of disruption. Politico’s sources said attackers used ARTEX, a Chinese AI penetration-testing tool; the FBI noted on November 13 that more than 1,600 ARTEX consoles were exposed to the internet. No perpetrator was named. In Copenhagen, Denmark’s parliament approved a deal with Greenland and the United States to strengthen security in the Arctic and North Atlantic, 109 to 0, in the Greenland account filed below; Greenland’s parliament approved the deal on November 3, 16 to 0 with 7 abstentions, and it enters into force once Denmark formally notifies the United States by diplomatic note. The United States can modernise and expand Pituffik Space Base in the northwest, and set up defence areas at Narsarsuaq in the south and Mestersvig in the east; only NATO members can establish manned or unmanned military installations on Greenland; the text allows the “Golden Dome” missile-defence system. In Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia’s parliament approved the resignation of President Khurelsukh Ukhnaa after accusations by another politician in the ruling party that he had benefited financially from a government railway project seven years ago, in the Mongolia account filed below; he announced his resignation on Tuesday, denying the allegations, the vote passed with 92.3 percent of members present backing the measure, the parliamentary speaker assumes presidential duties, and a presidential election must be organised within four months. In Japan, a magnitude-5.7 earthquake struck Kumamoto Prefecture at about 02:22 local time, in the quake account filed below, at a depth of about 10 km, with a maximum intensity of lower 5 on Japan’s scale in five municipalities; at least two people were injured, eight Shinkansen services were suspended, affecting about 5,600 passengers, and the weather agency urged vigilance for about a week. Settled: the debate’s verdict, the Court’s word and its answers, the Greenland vote, and the resignation. Open: the boat’s count, the lockdown’s toll, and the breach’s extent.

Le Monde — Rohingya: Historic International Court of Justice ruling finds Myanmar guilty of genocide

By a majority of its 14 judges, the court found six violations of the 1948 Genocide Convention; the ruling runs 191 pages, and the session lasted more than three hours. The court’s estimate, supported by the evidence, is that 10,000 Rohingya were violently killed in clearance operations, 1.2 percent of the targeted population in northern Rakhine; 392 villages were destroyed, and 700,000 fled in 2017. Myanmar had not yet responded as of Thursday evening. The United Kingdom’s Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband announced the expulsion of Myanmar’s defence attaché and called urgently for a UN Security Council session. Gambia welcomed the ruling with “profound satisfaction” and called on Myanmar to comply “fully and without delay.” Bangladesh urged implementation without delay. The United States said it was studying the ruling. Myanmar has two years to submit a report on restitution and compensation; the Court will decide the amount.

Military.com, carried from the Associated Press — Denmark Approves Security Deal With US and Greenland, Seeking to Finally Ease Tensions With Trump

Denmark’s parliament on Thursday approved a deal with Greenland and the United States to strengthen security in the Arctic and North Atlantic, aimed to defuse months of tensions over the Trump administration’s pressure about a possible US takeover of Greenland. The trilateral deal paves the way for more US bases in Greenland, a semiautonomous territory of NATO ally Denmark, while keeping the island under Danish control. The vote in the Folketing was 109 to 0, all 109 deputies present voting in favour. Greenland’s parliament approved the deal earlier in November — Nov. 3, 16 to 0 with 7 abstentions. The deal enters into force once Denmark formally notifies the United States by diplomatic note that both parliaments have consented; Thursday’s vote alone does not put it in force. Signed in New York on Sept. 22 by Mr. Trump, Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen and Greenlandic Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen, it builds on a 1951 defence agreement. The United States can modernise and expand Pituffik Space Base in the northwest, and set up defence areas at Narsarsuaq in the south and Mestersvig in the east, with further areas possible; only NATO members can establish manned or unmanned military installations on Greenland; the text allows the “Golden Dome” missile-defence system.
