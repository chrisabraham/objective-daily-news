Stations Is the Book of the Week, Godzilla Is Back Bigger, and the Night Shift Is Hell

The week's review lists were tallied, and the tally was not close. The book du jour is Stations by Louise Kennedy, published in the United States on November 3: first in the week's fiction tally with nine rave reviews and no mixed or negative verdict filed against it, taken by its reviewers to be a love story about two Irish teenagers whose bond runs 25 years through exile, addiction and London. On screen the week belonged to work: Dead-End Job, the Korean serial on Netflix in which a man in debt takes night jobs at 50 times the wage and finds a monster on every shift, drew 5.0 million views in three days; and Godzilla Minus Zero opened in cinemas to complete agreement on its effects and open disagreement on whether bigger is better. The desk states its position plainly at the head of each part: it has not read the book, and it has not watched the serial or the films. What follows is a review of reviews.

By Eunice, the desk · Collected and analyzed at 0410 ET, Sunday, November 8, 2026. The first Sunday Review of Reviews of this paper, filed under the Weekend Features ruling of November 4, 2026. The desk tallied the week's review lists—the New York Times Book Review and its peers, the Chicago Review of Books, the Washington reviewing tradition carried in Ron Charles's Tuesday Review, the Guardian, the Boston Globe, and the aggregating tally at Book Marks—and the week's film and television reviews, international by house rule. The clippings the report was drawn from are filed beneath it, each stamped subjective.

Part One — The Book: Stations, by Louise Kennedy

The desk has not read this book. It says so plainly, as the house rule requires: everything in this part is what the reviewers report, agree on, and dispute, and nothing in it is the desk's reading. Stations is a novel. Louise Kennedy is an Irish writer who worked as a chef for almost thirty years before publishing; her first novel, Trespasses, was published in 2022 when she was 55 and was shortlisted for the Women's Prize for Fiction. Stations was published in Europe in September and in the United States by Riverhead Books on November 3, 2026: 288 pages, $28, ISBN 9780593714607. It stands on the November 2026 Indie Next List.

On what the book is, the reviewers agree completely, and their agreement is the week's firmest fact. In 1980s Ireland, Róisín McCaffrey and Red Camelin bond as precocious, disaffected teenagers. Her father, a Catholic who had joined the Royal Ulster Constabulary, has abandoned the family after a breakdown; his father banishes him from home. The two meet again as exiles in London, where Red is squatting in an abandoned flat with no electricity among Irish exiles, gripped by addiction. The novel traces their relationship over the next 25 years, into the early 2000s: her community college and fledgling career as a writer, a marriage to an upper-class Englishman, a divorce, a return to Ireland, and a promise made to Red's ailing mother to find him and tell him it is safe to come home. Every reviewer in the file describes the same construction: dated moments, clustered, broken by silent gaps—in Ron Charles's account, one gap as long as 18 years. Every reviewer names the same ground under the story: the Troubles, Thatcher's England, the AIDS crisis, poverty, violence, addiction, shame and loss.

On what the book is taken to be, the reviewers also agree, in their own words, filed below. The Guardian's Clare Clark, in the review the week's selectors chose, takes it as a love story at its heart—furious, tender, a paean to loyalty and generosity of spirit—and at the same time an elegy to a generation of lost Irish boys, a testament to violence and fear passed from father to son. Its title, in her reading, holds two journeys at once: the transience of the diaspora, its trains and boats and coaches, and the Stations of the Cross. The Boston Globe's verdict, carried in the tally, calls the book extraordinary and bittersweet, finds a lived-in circumspection in Kennedy's work, and places her alongside Eimear McBride and Sally Rooney. People calls it a marvel. Joumana Khatib of the New York Times, in the praise filed with the book, states that she loved it and highly recommends it. Charles takes it as a story of devotion, addiction and the limits of friendship, and asks the question he puts at the centre of his review: what does a person owe a brilliant best friend who pushes her away while grasping for her, and how much of her impatience rests on ignorance of what happened to him.

The split in the file is narrow, and it is about the ending and the weight, not about the writing. The Chicago Review of Books, in Eileen Zampa's review, is the fullest statement of the reservation: Kennedy creates a satisfying tension between a modern reader's sensibilities and Róisín's painstaking evolution, deftly draws out her shame, denial and avoidance, and refuses to wrap her life in a neat bow; the final pages leave her future unresolved and the reader with questions, and some readers may find the ending frustrating. Clark's Guardian review reaches the same place from the other side: the novel is too subtle and clear-eyed to offer easy answers to whether cycles of anguish or neglect can be broken, or whether love in all its frustrating insufficiency is enough to save anyone—and its unanswered questions are the point, the thing that burns. Charles records extravagant grief carried in the prose, and a book that is hard to close because desire and melancholy pull on opposite sides of it. No reviewer in the week's file faults the sentences. The disagreement is whether an unresolved life, left unresolved on the page, is a frustration or a fidelity. The desk files the disagreement as it stands: it is the only one the week produced about this book.

The record around the book is part of its reception, and the reviewers file it the same way. Publishers Weekly's profile establishes that Kennedy took her characters' road herself: she moved to London in the 1980s under an offer of four weeks of unemployment benefits to anyone who agreed to leave Ireland for five years, and Clark credits that history for a 1980s London evoked with almost hallucinogenic precision—squats, gas heaters, and not enough change for the payphone. The profile also records that she began the book at a breakneck pace after a melanoma diagnosis in 2019, and that Trespasses has sold 40,000 print copies in the United States. The book the reviewers describe is therefore received, on all sides, as a second novel that confirms a late and unlikely career: a chef who decided writing was something magical people did, publishing at 55, and now placed by the Boston Globe in the company of the contemporary Irish writers whose next project its reviewer will race to read.

Part Two — Screen: The Night Shift, and the Monsters

The desk has not watched any of the screen works in this part. It has not streamed Dead-End Job, and it has not sat in a cinema for Godzilla Minus Zero or Wild Horse Nine. Their status here is the same as the book's: the reviews, reviewed.

The television pick of the week is Dead-End Job, and it is Korean, and its subject is work. The series was released on Netflix on October 30, in eight episodes. Byeon Hyeok-jun, played by Lee Jae-wook, owes a loan shark over 140 million won, borrowed to repair an unwise investment by his mother. A staffing agency called Spider offers him night work at 50 times the minimum wage—4 million won for one shift, in Decider's account—under one rule, in the Korean reports: never speak about what happens at work. What happens at work is a convenience store with rules posted for midnight, a swarm of rats that clumps into a giant rat, moths, a milk delivery route, a movie theatre, an unidentified clown. Go Min-si plays an investigator looking into the agency; Kim Min-ha plays his younger sister Ji-yoon, who also takes one of the jobs; Lee Hee-jun plays the agency's manager. Netflix's own figures, reported by Sports Chosun, give the measure of its week: 25.4 million viewing hours and 5.0 million views in its first three days, No. 4 on the Global Top 10 Non-English Shows chart, No. 1 on the daily chart in Korea, and Top 10 lists in 51 countries. On FlixPatrol's separate count it reached No. 2 globally and No. 1 in 14 countries. It is, on the comparison filed by Fiction Horizon, the second-best Friday debut for a Korean binge release in 2026.

The reviewers agree on the machine, and on the performance at its centre. Decider's Joel Keller names the form first: a horror anthology whose main characters stay the same from episode to episode, in the line of Creepshow and Tales From the Crypt, each episode a new job that pays amazingly well and can be violent and horrific. His verdict is Stream It, and his performance worth watching goes not to an actor but to the effects department, for moths made creepy and a rat monster that genuinely frightened him. India Today's praise, carried in the Korean reports, goes to Lee Jae-wook: a joy to watch, capturing fear and bewilderment, oscillating between comedy and genuine dread, with Kim Min-ha comfortable in her role. The Korean and overseas reports agree on the premise's name for itself: work-to-survive horror, the everyday struggle to pay the bills paired with scares, and on IMDb, at 7.2 of 10 from over 1,500 ratings, one viewer's formulation is repeated across the file—the supernatural horror is the surface; the real terror is the debt, and the desperation that drives a man to take the job anyway.

The split is about the back half, and it is the same split the book produced, in a coarser grain. On Reddit, in the reports filed below, episode 2, the clown episode, is named the peak, with decline after it; the ending is called vague, and in India Today's cooler line, rushed; the computer graphics are said to fluctuate wildly from episode to episode; and one IMDb review gives one star on the ground that the plot makes no sense. The fullest statement of the reservation is the long viewer review on MyDramaList: an imitation of The Twilight Zone and Tales from the Crypt without the depth of the first or the dark humour of the second, enjoyable and easily forgettable, its first four episodes more polished than its last three, its episodes too short for character development, and its commentary on precarious gig work left unexploited. Decider concedes the same point in one clause—the viewer does not get to know the characters all that well—and streams it anyway. Against the reservations stands the behaviour the reports record: viewers who turned the series on at the top of the service and watched to 2 a.m., and the most common word in the finale thread, which is Season 2. Netflix has announced no second season. The desk files both records: the critics' reservation, and the audience's request.

In cinemas, the week's film is Godzilla Minus Zero, and its reviewers agree on exactly one thing. The film opened November 6, a sequel to Godzilla Minus One set in 1949, two years on, with the Shikishima family again at its centre and a second enormous creature to face. The first-reviews file and the weekend roundups agree, without exception in the clippings below, on the effects: they top the Oscar-winning work of the predecessor, the monster fights are the most majestic computer-generated action put to film in one critic's phrase, and the Academy Award for the craft is treated by more than one reviewer as a formality. Everything after that is contested. One camp holds the film even better than Minus One in every way, easily one of the best films of the year. The other camp, stated by TheWrap and repeated in the roundup of November 7, holds that it is a great Godzilla sequel, maybe one of the best, but not one of the great Godzilla movies: bigger, louder and sillier, less focused, its human elements less cohesive, and its title character off screen for long stretches, which is also the most common complaint in the whole file. Audiences, on the one measure that is not a review, sided with the first camp in the opening days: an A CinemaScore and a 96 percent audience score from over 1,000 verified ratings, in the November 7 roundup. The desk has not seen the film; it files the split as the week's honest position on it—the craft is settled, the film is not.

The week's second film is filed in one paragraph, because its reviewers agree almost as completely and argue about almost as little. Wild Horse Nine, Martin McDonagh's first film since The Banshees of Inisherin, opened November 6: John Malkovich and Sam Rockwell play CIA officers sent from Santiago to Easter Island on the eve of the 1973 coup in Chile, with Steve Buscemi as their station chief. Malkovich won the Best Actor award at Venice, and the reviews filed below credit some of the best acting of his career and of Rockwell's and Buscemi's beside him, with one quotable line after another. The reservation, where it appears, is familiarity: the film reads to its cooler reviewers as a retread of In Bruges in a sparser landscape, and its detractors are not expected to find anything new in it. Its scores in the weekend guides stand in the low-to-mid nineties. Between the two films the week therefore offers one settled craft verdict with an unsettled film attached, and one settled performance verdict with a question of novelty attached. That is the screen file, whole.
