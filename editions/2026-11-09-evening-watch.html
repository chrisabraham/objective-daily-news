No Readout From the Oval Office, a Best Day Since April on Wall Street, and a Speaker Agreed in Jerusalem

The day whole, at evening: the mediator came, met the president and the vice president, and left no public word behind him, and the war he mediates acquired a sentence from Tehran instead—its president said the Supreme Leader had ordered an end to the state of neither war nor peace. The House acquired a deadline: eight articles against the secretary of defense, refiled at mid-afternoon, with action required by Wednesday. The markets acquired a close: the best day since April, priced on oil down near six percent, with the two closing prints differing and both printed below. Kraków acquired a limit: its prosecutors said no ideology for the dead gunman had been established, as the national mourning ended on schedule. Jerusalem acquired an agreement: a speaker from Yashar, the ultra-Orthodox talks to be exhausted first, and full confidence stated by both men. Nothing was called in the Senate count. Tuesday holds the rest—the Abu Dhabi table, the inflation print, the next Alaska count, and the Knesset’s first sitting.

The Evening in Brief

Monday ended with its meetings held, its markets closed, its one new legal sentence imposed in London, its one new parliamentary clock started in Washington, and none of its open questions answered. In Washington, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, Qatar’s prime minister and foreign minister, met President Trump in the Oval Office at noon and Vice President Vance separately, and by 1800 no readout had been issued and no agreement announced, in the afternoon account and the evening account filed below. Tehran spoke for itself the same day: President Masoud Pezeshkian said Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei had instructed officials to end the state of “neither war nor peace,” that Iran could negotiate with dignity, and that it would not surrender to force, in the Tehran account filed below, and Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei defended Tehran’s two-day proposal as the most reasonable offer on the table. In the House, Representative Thomas Massie refiled eight articles of impeachment against Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth as a privileged resolution; House leaders have two legislative days to act. In New York the Dow closed up more than eleven hundred points on the Associated Press print and the S&P 500 had its best day since April, as Brent crude fell 5.8 percent to $92.57. In Kraków the National Prosecutor’s Office spokesman, Przemysław Nowak, said investigators could not yet link the dead gunman responsibly to any ideology, in the evening brief filed below. In Jerusalem Mr. Eisenkot and Mr. Liberman agreed the next speaker of the Knesset will be a member of Yashar, in the speaker account filed below. In London Mr. Justice Bennathan sentenced Russell Brand to ten years and six months in prison. The desk files the day in that order.

Washington at Evening: The Mediator Leaves No Word, and the House Gets a Clock

The noon meeting produced, by evening, a fact of attendance and nothing else. The Qatari prime minister met the president behind closed doors and the vice president separately, having visited Tehran last week; the White House released no readout, and Fox News reported it had sought comment and received no response before publication. The terms on the table stand where the midday edition printed them: an end to the American blockade of Iran’s ports, the lifting of some sanctions and a halt to bombing, in return for Tehran stopping its own closure of the Strait of Hormuz, with nuclear talks to follow—a plan Washington has repeatedly rejected, and which Secretary Rubio, in Paris for the G7, again said was not near a breakthrough, its demands in part unacceptable, outrageous and not realistic. Tehran’s considered response arrived Monday in two voices. Mr. Baghaei defended the plan. President Pezeshkian, at a ceremony in Tehran, gave the day its fullest Iranian sentence: the Leader had ordered an end to the neither-war-nor-peace state; “we can negotiate with dignity and resolve the issue with dignity”; and Iran would not surrender in the face of force, intimidation or bullying. The president’s own Sunday sentence stands unwithdrawn beside them: fresh attacks are highly unlikely if talks produce a strong agreement. No side moved a term on Monday. The mediation moved a man between capitals, and the record of the day is the movement and the statements, not a settlement.

At mid-afternoon the House acquired the week’s hardest deadline. Representative Massie of Kentucky, a Republican, read a 34-page resolution on the floor re-filing eight articles of impeachment against Secretary Hegseth, as a privileged resolution, in the Louisville account filed below. The articles charge violations of the War Powers Resolution in the Iran war, failure to minimize civilian casualties, extrajudicial killings in the boat strikes, suppression of Senator Kelly’s speech, the seizure of Nicolás Maduro without authority, and an unlawful war in Yemen. The procedure is the fact: leaders must act within two legislative days—an up-or-down vote, a motion to table, or a referral to committee, each itself a vote—with a vote expected by Wednesday. The answers on the record are these: Mr. Hegseth said he is not worried and will defend his record at a Pentagon press conference on Tuesday, his first in 180 days in the Associated Press count; the president has backed him, and said on Sunday’s broadcast interview that he has confidence in him; Speaker Johnson opposes the effort; Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said he supports the articles. Mr. Massie first filed the same articles on September 15; the House left for its campaign recess the next day and the notice expired. If the House impeaches, Mr. Hegseth would be the third Cabinet secretary impeached in the republic’s history, and removal would require two-thirds of the Senate. The articles are allegations in a charging document; they are findings by no court. Mr. Miller’s first day as chief of staff, the morning’s other Washington fact, closed without a statement from him in the file.

The Markets’ Close: The Best Day Since April, in Two Prints

New York kept the morning’s reading and enlarged it, and the close is the day’s largest single number. On the Associated Press print, carried in the closing account filed below, the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 1,104.02 points, or 2.2 percent, to 52,389.75; the S&P 500 rose 173.08 points, or 2.3 percent, to 7,549.94, its best day since April, sitting 3.4 percent below its all-time high; and the Nasdaq Composite rose 673.71 points, or 2.7 percent, to 25,253.14. On the Reuters print, carried in the second closing account filed below, the Dow rose 1,131.81 points, or 2.21 percent, to 52,417.54; the S&P rose 177.81 points, or 2.41 percent, to 7,554.67; and the Nasdaq rose 656.06 points, or 2.67 percent, to 25,235.49. The two prints differ in the third figure on every index; the desk prints both, each under its wire, in the Cocked Hat. The driver named in both accounts is oil: Brent crude fell 5.8 percent to $92.57 a barrel, from above $112 at the month’s start, on the president’s stated unlikelihood of renewed strikes on Iran—the same diplomacy that produced no readout at noon was priced in New York as de-escalation. Inside the rise Nvidia fell against the market all day on its board’s inquiry into whether its chief executive was the target of a foreign intelligence effort: down 4.27 percent to $178.67 at the close in one account, down 6.2 and 6.3 percent in two others, each at its own hour, all filed below; the board said it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised, Mr. Huang remains chief executive and has pledged full cooperation, and Beijing rejected the espionage allegations. Tuesday’s test is named in every account: the October consumer price report at 8:30 a.m. Eastern.

Kraków at Evening: No Ideology Established, and the Mourning Ends

Poland’s file acquired its limit on Monday evening, stated by the prosecutors themselves. Przemysław Nowak, spokesman for the National Prosecutor’s Office, said investigators do not have elements that allow them responsibly and categorically to state that the gunman was inspired by a particular ideology, in the evening brief above. Government spokesman Adam Szłapka said such assessments should be withheld until the services have full information. Those two sentences stand against the weekend’s government reading—the interior minister’s far-right and ultra-nationalistic reading of the emblems on the man’s clothing, and the justice minister’s statement that he acted to intimidate a large number of people, making the offence a terrorist offence in the minister’s words—and the desk prints the limit and the readings side by side, unresolved. The day’s facts stand as the midday edition filed them: the suspect, 24, died at about 4:30 a.m. after at least three operations; autopsies of the nine victims—six in the Main Market Square and three at the family home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski—began Monday and will take about two days; fifteen wounded remain in hospital, fourteen stable and one officer, Artur Morański, in serious condition after, in the evening brief’s figure, twelve hours of surgery; eleven firearms were recovered in the searches. National mourning, declared for November 8 and 9, ends Monday night; Kraków’s own mourning runs to November 11, which the city marks without a parade. School sessions with psychologists ran in Ostrowiec on Monday and continue Tuesday. The investigation proceeds on forensics, witness testimony, telephones, computers and weapons; the man who could have answered it is dead, and the prosecutors’ Monday sentence is that the evidence has not yet answered it either.

Paris and the Eve of Abu Dhabi: The Allies Apart in Public, the Guns Loud in Private Fact

The G7’s first day in Paris ended with the differences stated, not settled. The Associated Press account, in the Paris account filed below, records that on the Russia-Ukraine war, on Iran and on international organizations the sides appeared far apart, while agreeing the conflicts should end as quickly as possible. Secretary Rubio faced the allies’ questions on whether the midterm defeat changes the administration’s approach—his first trip abroad since the elections. German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul, meeting Mr. Rubio separately, welcomed the Abu Dhabi talks and stressed that Europe must be part of any resolution; Canada’s Foreign Minister Anita Anand said it remains an open question how Washington will proceed; and in a sideline meeting with Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot, Mr. Rubio pressed the administration’s case against European Union trade preferences. France has said the meeting is not expected to produce a joint communiqué. The talks continue Tuesday, with a dedicated Ukraine session.

The table Paris is the antechamber to sits on Tuesday, and on Monday its membership was itself disputed. The Abu Dhabi round—the fourth of the American-brokered process and the first since February—brings Mr. Witkoff and Mr. Kushner for Washington, Mr. Medinsky for Moscow and Mr. Umerov for Kyiv, on security guarantees, de-escalation and humanitarian questions, in the talks account filed below. On Monday Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov reiterated that the negotiations would take place exclusively between the Russian, Ukrainian and American delegations; Mr. Barrot had said on Sunday that officials from France, Britain, Germany, NATO and the European Commission would be present, and a European Union official said Monday that national security officials would be on site, with their role unclear. Both statements stand. The eve was marked, as eves of talks in this war are marked, by fire: Russia launched 317 drones overnight, 105 of them jet-powered, and struck four electrical substations in the Kyiv, Dnipropetrovsk, Cherkasy and Vinnytsia regions by its defence ministry’s account, leaving more than 600,000 people in Dnipropetrovsk region without power in the operator DTEK’s figure; Ukrainian long-range drones set fire to the KINEF refinery at Kirishi, one of Russia’s largest, in the Leningrad governor’s account; and the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant spent ten hours on emergency diesel after losing its last working external power line on Sunday, before power was restored through a single line, in the strikes account and the energy account filed below. President Zelenskyy said the European Union had agreed to activate a special emergency mechanism to speed funding for grid repairs. No ceasefire attends the talks; the talks are not one.

Jerusalem at Evening: A Speaker Agreed, the Ultra-Orthodox First, and Confidence Stated by Both

The mandate’s second day produced the file’s first agreements, and they are procedural in the way that decides governments. Mr. Eisenkot and Mr. Liberman met Monday and agreed that the next speaker of the Knesset will be a member of Mr. Eisenkot’s Yashar party, in the speaker account above—a shift from Mr. Liberman’s earlier demand that the post go to Mr. Lapid, which had caused friction in the bloc. Yashar’s statement after what it called a substantive and good meeting said the two “expressed their full confidence in the ability to form a stable government for four years,” in the statement account filed below; the account notes the statement did not say whether their differences were resolved. The Channel 13 report carried in the second Jerusalem account gives the meeting’s substance: Mr. Liberman told Mr. Eisenkot, “You will be prime minister. I’m not asking for that position for myself,” demanded time to work and the bloc’s preparation for compromises, and asked him to assist in contacts with the ultra-Orthodox parties; the two agreed those talks must be exhausted first, and only afterward should a minority government resting on the Arab-led parties be examined—which puts Mr. Liberman’s ruled-out option last rather than off the table, and negotiations with United Torah Judaism are reported already begun. The new Knesset convenes Tuesday. Mr. Eisenkot has until December 6, with a fourteen-day extension available; the bloc stands at 48 seats, 61 are required, and Mr. Netanyahu remains prime minister while the days run.

Hormuz and Khor Mor at Evening: No Unauthorised Transit, an Export Claim, and a Field Still Dark

The strait’s Monday record is a claim of control from Tehran, a tracker’s count from midday, and an export figure from Abu Dhabi, and the three do not sum. An unnamed Guards Navy official told Iranian media that navigational aid systems in the south of the strait were destroyed and that no unauthorised vessels transited overnight, in the Reuters account filed below. The tracker’s midday count stands as the midday edition filed it: two crossings visible on Monday, both inbound, against a seven-day average of 7.0 and a pre-crisis average of 84.8 a day. Against both, the Emirates’ account of its own bypass: an Emirati official at the ADIPEC conference said the country exported almost 4.4 million barrels a day now, against 3.1 to 3.2 million before the war began on February 28, through routes including the Fujairah pipeline outside the strait, in the Fox account above; Energy Minister Suhail Al Mazrouei had said on November 2 that exports had at times exceeded pre-war levels, without dates or volumes, and the pipeline’s own ceiling is 1.5 million barrels a day in the analysis filed below, so the figure, if it stands, runs substantially through the strait itself and the shuttle system around it. The president’s Sunday sentence—that the strait is open—stands beside the tracker’s severely restricted. At Khor Mor nothing moved by evening: production remains halted after Sunday’s drone attack, the operator’s damage assessment has produced no figure and no restart date, 2,800 megawatts stand off the Kurdistan grid, no group has claimed the attack, and Baghdad’s joint investigative committee has not reported.

London at Evening: Ten Years and Six Months

The day’s one completed legal act was a sentence. At Southwark Crown Court on Monday morning, Mr. Justice Bennathan sentenced Russell Brand, 51, to ten years and six months in prison: nine years for one count of rape, and eighteen months—the longest of three terms of nine, eighteen and eight months, running concurrently—for three counts of sexual assault, the two sentences running consecutively, in the sentencing account and the wire account filed below. A jury convicted him on Thursday, November 5, on four of seven counts, for offences against four women between 2004 and 2009, and acquitted him on three. The judge told him his offences were fuelled by “staggering arrogance,” named him a sexual predator, reduced the rape term by one year for his lack of previous convictions, the seventeen years since the last offence, and his work with people in addiction, and ordered that he serve two-thirds of the sentence in custody before release on licence. Detective Superintendent Andy Furphy of the Metropolitan Police made a statement on Monday on the investigation. Files on two further women are with the prosecutors, and a New York civil case proceeds; both stand open. The full record of the case is filed on this paper’s special report page of today’s date.

For the fourth day running no Senate race was called and no new vote figure was published for either. Mississippi stands at Scott Colom 467,930 to Cindy Hyde-Smith 465,356, a margin of 2,574, with just over 2,800 absentee mail ballots still out in the secretary of state’s accounting; mail ballots postmarked by Election Day are due by 5 p.m. Tuesday, and counties certify by November 13. Colorado’s 4th District stands uncalled with them. Democrats hold at least 54 Senate seats whatever the two races decide, and control of neither chamber is in question. The standing files did not move: at Al-Ayyat the investigations ordered Sunday own the cause of the collision that killed seven, the driver of Train No. 134 among them, and no authority has stated a cause; in Asunción former President Peña’s pretrial detention stands, with no ruling on any charge, and he is presumed innocent.
