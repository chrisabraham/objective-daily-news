Part One — The Book: No Such Thing As Monday, by Siân Hughes

The desk has not read this book. It says so plainly, as the house rule requires: everything in this part is what the reviewers report, agree on, and dispute, and nothing in it is the desk's reading. No Such Thing As Monday is a novel. Siân Hughes is a British writer who began as a poet; her novel Pearl, published in 2023 and longlisted for the Booker Prize, was written in response to the medieval poem of the same name. This book was published in the United States by Henry Holt and Company on November 10, 2026: 224 pages, $26.99, ISBN 9781250459572.

On what the book is, the reviewers agree completely, and their agreement is the week's firmest fact. Stephanie Cartwright—Steffie—is a chain-smoking, prickly woman in middle age who works at a dry cleaner's in contemporary working-class England and tends to her father. The novel opens with Steffie finding that father, Stan, dead in his favorite chair. Stan dominated her childhood near Birmingham in the 1970s and 1980s: a violent bully who ran dogfights at the local pub, whose wife died before him, and whose favorite Steffie always was—a favoritism that left her carrying guilt for her older sister, Caroline, who fled home at 16 and has not been seen since. With Stan dead, Steffie sets out to find Caroline. The reviewers describe the same construction from every side: a life told in episodes across childhood to middle age, in no straight order, each chapter standing nearly on its own—in the Chicago Review of Books' account, each chapter a set of pages in a photo album, the picture widening as the album fills. And every reviewer who addresses the book's machinery names the same source under it: the medieval poem called Cleanness, or Purity, from the same 14th-century manuscript that holds Pearl and Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, which gives the novel its governing figure of the stain—the laundry Steffie cleans by day, and the past she cannot clean at all.

On what the book is taken to be, the reviewers also agree, in their own words, filed below. Leah Greenblatt of the New York Times calls it fractious and darkly funny: an ouroboros of ongoing traumas that somehow manages not to be head-smashingly bleak, because Steffie on the page is a pretty great hang—spiky, sharp-witted, blazingly self-aware—and because Hughes has a canny, lyrical ear for her misfits and underdogs, and a touch tender enough to nudge their fate toward better things. Kirkus, in its starred notice, credits an alchemy like Douglas Stuart's: Hughes transforms the dross of Steffie's life into literary gold. The Chicago Review of Books calls Steffie compelling—not always likeable, and certainly never unlikeable—and the novel a powerful story that unveils itself with every page. The Bookreporter review reaches the same resting place from the record of the plot: a profoundly sad novel, and a surprisingly hopeful one, whose message is that it is never too late to gain perspective, to make amends, or to try to start anew. The publisher's record places the book in the working-class British realist tradition of Douglas Stuart and Andrea Arnold, and the trade notices filed with it name Hughes an exceptional and compassionate chronicler of precarious and chaotic lives.

The split in the file is about the comedy, and how far it reaches into the childhood chapters. The Times of London, in the coolest review the week filed, grants the premise its full force—humour is how Steffie copes with loss, and how the reader copes with an otherwise harrowing tale of penury and hurt—and then records a falling off: as the novel dives into Steffie's violent childhood in the West Midlands, the dark comedy recedes, and Hughes could have gone further and funnier to wring the last drop of absurdity from each scene. Where the author excels, in that review's judgment, is in meandering through the slippages of memory to reveal what is gnawing at her narrator. Its verdict is measured: a decent follow-up to Pearl, even if by design it seems to position itself as the lesser work—a book that, like Steffie, cannot escape the taint of disappointment. The desk files the disagreement as it stands. Seven of the tally's eight counted verdicts are favorable, and the favorable reviews praise the humour as the book's engine; the one cool review praises the memory work and asks for more of the humour. No reviewer in the week's file faults the sentences, and no reviewer disputes Steffie herself.

Part Two — Screen: Scrooge, Split in Half, and the Empress Takes Her Leave

The desk has not watched any of the screen works in this part. It has not streamed The Empress, and it has not sat in a cinema for Ebenezer: A Christmas Carol or How to Rob a Bank. Their status here is the same as the book's: the reviews, reviewed.

The television pick of the week is The Empress, and it is German, and it is finished. The third and final season of the serial—Die Kaiserin in its own language, on Netflix—was released on November 12. The story it closes is the story the serial has told since 2022: Elisabeth of Austria, played by Devrim Lingnau Islamoğlu, the Bavarian duke's daughter who married Emperor Franz Joseph, played by Philip Froissant, for love and spent her reign fighting the walls of the Viennese court. The final season opens a few months after the second season's close, with Franz returned from commanding his army at the front, a changed, cold and distant man, and the imperial marriage on shaky ground; it carries the story through to the establishment of the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy. On the measure that is not a review, the serial's standing is a matter of record: its first season in September 2022 became, in the reports filed below, Netflix's second-most-watched series globally on its debut, and it stands in those reports as the service's most successful German original since Barbarians.

The reviewers agree on the season's materials, and on the bargain its ending strikes. Every review in the file records a darker and more politically tangled season; every review credits the same visible strengths—the chemistry of the two leads, the costumes, in the Absolute Geeks account the work of costume designer Gabriela Reumer standing among the best in streaming period drama, and production design that no reviewer faults. And every review that reaches the ending describes the same transaction: the finale blends fiction and fact, chooses the fairytale the serial has always carried over the history book, and leaves the harrowing later record of Elisabeth's life—the loss of her son Rudolf among it—outside the frame, her assassination handled, in one account, as onscreen text before the credits. The ending turns the founding of the dual monarchy into a fresh start for the marriage, grounded in the understanding that this empress needs the freedom to be herself.

The split is about whether that bargain is the season's achievement or its evasion, and the file runs the full width of the scale. At one end, ScreenRant's November 14 account calls the third season a true masterpiece and the serial a show with no bad season, each installment better than the last. Collider calls the ending a satisfying, poignant conclusion for one of Netflix's best hidden gems, and Absolute Geeks, at four of five, calls the season a strong, confident farewell that sticks the landing where it counts—while recording, in the same review, a rushed and underdeveloped Hungarian subplot and a life-changing relationship with Count Andrássy given too little room. At the other end stands the Midgard Times review, at 3.5 of 10: a rushed finale that turns history into bullet points, a season that never allows any moment, scene, feeling or action to linger, speedrunning its material until investment fails—filed by a reviewer who holds that the serial peaked in a first season that was itself not all that good. Between the poles, the audience record filed on IMDb runs warm: breathtaking, heart-wrenching, viewers who waited two years and binged the season in one go. The desk files the whole width: the materials are agreed, the farewell is contested, and the contest is between a romance that has earned its ending and a history that was owed more time.

In cinemas, the week's film is Ebenezer: A Christmas Carol, and its reviewers agree on its ghosts and divide on its Scrooge. The film opened November 13, directed by Ti West and written by Nathaniel Halpern, rated PG-13 and running 116 minutes, with Johnny Depp as Scrooge in his first studio lead in eight years, Ian McKellen as Jacob Marley, Tramell Tillman, Andrea Riseborough and Daisy Ridley as the three ghosts, and Rupert Grint as Bob Cratchit. The agreement in the file is exact: the production design and the photography—Eve Stewart's design and Checco Varese's dark, fogged London, credited by name in reviews that agree on little else—and the ghost work above all, Tillman's Ghost of Christmas Present named the film's diamond in TheWrap's account and the spirits given layers, in Empire's, that outclass the picture around them. The Rotten Tomatoes consensus, as the service prints it, holds the same two facts in one sentence: evocative production design, a Depp filled with the bah-humbug spirit, and a telling that plays too safe among its long line of remakes. The division is the lead. The Daily Telegraph's Robbie Collin gave the film four of five; the Guardian's Peter Bradshaw found Depp in fact rather good; Deadline recorded a man having a blast, and The Hollywood Reporter a creepily effective comeback vehicle. Against them: the Boston Globe's Odie Henderson, at one of four, called Depp easily the worst Scrooge he had ever seen; IndieWire's David Ehrlich found him unengaging in a film named for his character; the Associated Press, at one of four, called the whole a horror-tinged holiday slog. The counts print the division without ornament: 35 fresh and 34 rotten in one count of 69, and an audience mark, when it posted, of 89 percent against the critics' half.

The week's second film is filed in one paragraph, because its split runs between the critics and the audience rather than through either camp. How to Rob a Bank, David Leitch's heist picture, opened November 13: Nicholas Hoult leads a Pittsburgh crew that livestreams its bank robberies, with John C. Reilly as the veteran FBI agent on the digital trail, and Zoë Kravitz, Pete Davidson and Anna Sawai in the cast; it runs 122 minutes and is rated R. The critics' count stands at 31 to 33 percent across the weekend's tallies—the lowest mark of Leitch's career, in more than one account—under a consensus that calls it frenetic entertainment whose ideas never cohere into a satisfying message; the A.V. Club, at C+, found two or three movies smashed together, and Time Out, at three of five, found a fun, throwaway action-thriller. Verified audiences scored it 77 to 80 percent in the same counts, a gap of more than 45 points, with a B-minus CinemaScore between the two. The desk has not seen either film; it files the week's cinema record as two splits of different shapes—one film divided against itself at its centre, one divided between the people who review films and the people who bought tickets.

Worth Watching — The History of Concrete

The desk has not screened this film. The publisher has, and the publisher is the source of this item; it stands here on his tip, treated by the house method. The History of Concrete is John Wilson's documentary feature—his directorial debut in the form, after his HBO series How To with John Wilson—premiered at Sundance on January 22, 2026 and released by Magnolia Pictures on September 18, 2026, at 100 minutes in the reviews' count. It was reviewed by the New York Times, Time, and IndieWire, among others. The record the reviews converge on is this: a deadpan inquiry into concrete—its Roman endurance, its American crumbling, the oldest concrete street in the country at Bellefontaine, Ohio, a Brooklyn-Queens Expressway described to Wilson as a ticking time bomb—turns, in its final movement, into a portrait of Jack Macco, a Long Island rock musician Wilson meets handing out liquor samples, and of Macco's late fiancée Dorothy Chin Brandt, the first female Asian American judge in New York State, who died on January 27, 2025, at 78. On the film around that turn, the file agrees in its parts: IndieWire's David Ehrlich calls it consistently hilarious and sneakily profound, sustained by a tension between order and entropy—concrete as the material men scratch their names into as a bid for immortality, and use to build the burial vaults that hold them after. The New York Times' Alissa Wilkinson, in the praise carried with the film, records being left wondering how a movie about concrete could leave a lump in the throat; the Guardian's Adrian Horton calls it consistently laugh-out-loud funny, compelling and surprising; the New Yorker's Richard Brody finds it rich in an authentic sense of discovery, and pointedly, urgently political. RogerEbert.com's account files the reservation the favorable reviews concede in passing: the film is in substance a long episode of the HBO series, and the history of its title takes up only a small portion of its running time—and a college review filed November 13 files the cooler edge, a narration and a comic persona that reviewer found an acquired taste. The film took the Best Narration prize at the Critics Choice Documentary Awards, in the November 14 account filed below. The peg on this paper is plain: Friday's Special Report, Concrete: What the Record Shows About the Most Consumed Material on Earth (November 13), covers the material; this item covers the film.

Chicago Review of Books — a haunting first love, loss and life in exile: Stations

Eileen Zampa reviews Stations on its American publication: since its European publication in September the novel has received critical acclaim in Ireland and England, and fans of Trespasses will not be disappointed. Róisín and Redmond are two outsiders navigating family trauma, addiction and exile in Ireland and London from the 1980s to the early 2000s; Kennedy creates a tension between a modern reader's sensibilities and Róisín's character evolution, refuses to wrap her life in a neat bow, and leaves her future unresolved in the final pages, which some readers may find frustrating. The review calls the novel sweeping in thematic scope, with deft prose and unforgettable characters, in the tradition of Irish literature from Edna O'Brien to Claire Keegan.

Ron Charles, The Tuesday Review — the demands of love: Stations

Ron Charles reviews Stations as a story of devotion, addiction and the limits of friendship: Róisín McCaffrey and Red Camelin meet as teenagers, and neither recovers from the moment of happiness. Kennedy constructs the novel as a series of dated moments over a quarter century, with silent gaps of days and one of 18 years; her prose mixes narration and internal reflection, and her dialogue, without quotation marks, captures the erratic structure of real conversation. Charles records that Kennedy worked as a chef for decades before publishing, that Trespasses, published in 2022 when she was 55, was shortlisted for the Women's Prize for Fiction, and that with Stations she has confirmed her status as a writer whose stories compete with the reader's own life. The book is listed at 288 pages and $28 from Riverhead Books.

Publishers Weekly — Louise Kennedy has found her home

Publishers Weekly profiles Kennedy ahead of Stations: as a young woman she followed the same migratory path as her characters, moving to London in the 1980s after accepting an offer of four weeks of unemployment benefits to anyone who agreed to leave Ireland for five years. The novel is shot through with the political and social issues of Thatcher's England, including the Troubles and the AIDS crisis, and is focused primarily on the characters: Róisín McCaffrey and Red Camelin, on the dole in a decrepit London squat. The profile records her melanoma diagnosis in 2019, the breakneck approach she took to the first chapters, and that Trespasses has sold 40,000 print copies in the United States to date by BookScan's count.

Decider — stream it or skip it: Dead-End Job on Netflix

Joel Keller reviews the Korean series Dead-End Job: Byeon Hyeok-jun, played by Lee Jae-wook, owes a violent loan shark over 140 million won after an unwise investment by his mother, and takes night work from the Spider Staffing Agency at 50 times minimum wage. MyDramaList — a viewer review of Dead-End Job

A long viewer review on MyDramaList gives the cooler account: the series is an imitation of The Twilight Zone and Tales from the Crypt without the psychological and societal depth of the first or the dark humour of the second, enjoyable for a popcorn evening and easily forgettable, and not the horror series of the year. Rotten Tomatoes Editorial — Godzilla Minus Zero, first reviews

Rotten Tomatoes collects the first reviews of Godzilla Minus Zero after its New York Film Festival premiere, ahead of its November 6 opening: a sequel to Godzilla Minus One set two years later, following the Shikishima family as the monster returns to face another enormous creature. The collected verdicts agree the film is bigger and more surprising, with visual effects that top the predecessor's Oscar-winning work; they disagree on whether it is better than the previous film, one critic calling it even better in every way and another recording that with great expectations come considerable letdowns, that it lacks the clarity of purpose of Minus One, and that the human elements are not as cohesive.
