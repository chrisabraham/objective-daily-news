The Midterm Elections — The Last Entry of the Day 
 
 The Rally Is Gone and the Count Is Not Finished

The last entry of the day at 0000: Thursday’s closes are fixed and lower, the futures fall again in the evening, Tokyo opens its Friday in a slide that carries the Nikkei into a bear market, Brent trades back toward a hundred dollars on an expanded tanker file, and the count—two Senate seats, two governorships, and the last House races, Colorado’s among them—goes to Friday unfinished.

What Thursday Settled

Thursday’s closes are fixed, and they are the day’s largest settled fact. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed down 1,833.06 points, or 3.44 percent, at 51,523.06, against a gain of 1,569.40 points on Wednesday. The S&P 500 closed down 4.36 percent at 7,353.73, its worst day since April 2025. The Nasdaq Composite closed down 5.52 percent at 24,315.00, in correction territory, 11.9 percent below its October 6 record close. The Nasdaq 100 closed down 6.36 percent at 26,629.81, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.25 percent to 9,011.14, and the Russell 2000, in the evening futures account filed below, lost 4.5 percent. The volatility index closed at 28.09, up 69.1 percent, and the ten-year Treasury yield slipped about six basis points to roughly 4.84 percent. All three principal indexes closed below their levels of Tuesday, election day. The figures stand as the Evening Watch printed them, from the closing account; midnight adds nothing to them except the hours after them, which are filed below.

The day’s economic figures are also fixed, and they run the other way. Initial claims for unemployment benefits stand at 189,000 for the week ended October 31, down 6,000, the lowest reading since 1969, per Reuters’ report of the Labor Department’s releases; third-quarter productivity rose at a 3.6 percent annualized rate and unit labor costs fell 0.7 percent. In London, Bank Rate stands at 4 percent, raised from 3.75 percent by 8–1, per Reuters’ Bank of England file. Control of Congress and of the governorships stands settled as it stood on Wednesday: the Senate at 54 seats for the Democrats and 44 for the Republicans, the governorships at 28 for the Democrats, and the Democrats to organize the House in January. What Thursday added to the settled column was calls. The Associated Press called Colorado’s 5th District for the Democrat, Jessica Killin, in the afternoon. In the evening the wire called New York’s 17th District for the Democrat, Cait Conley, at 140,165 votes to the incumbent Republican Mike Lawler’s 128,292, per the district file—two days after Lawler called Conley himself to concede on election night, per the account of the networks’ confirmation filed below. The running live file recorded the evening’s other calls in sequence: Democrats flip Arizona’s 6th, Colorado’s 5th, Montana’s 1st, and North Carolina’s 9th Districts at 5:55 p.m. ET, and New York’s 17th at 6:44 p.m.

The Count at Midnight

No Senate race was called on Thursday, for the second full day, and the two open races end the day where the evening left them. The Associated Press Senate file in its latest state lists Alaska and Mississippi as too early to call, Mississippi at 99.83 percent of precincts reporting. The newest figures under the two races are Decision Desk HQ’s. In Mississippi, in the file’s update of 4:12 p.m. EST carried in the live results file filed below, the Democrat, Scott Colom, stands at 466,763 votes and the incumbent Republican, Cindy Hyde-Smith, at 464,939—a lead of 1,824 votes, at the top of the range this paper printed during the day, with more than 95 percent of the estimated vote counted. In Alaska the same file carries the Democrat, Mary Peltola, at 102,962 first-choice votes and the incumbent Republican, Dan Sullivan, at 102,325—a lead of 637. The dates that govern both races stand as filed: Mississippi’s affidavit identification is due November 10 and its counties certify by November 13, and the candidate with the most votes wins; Alaska takes domestic mail ballots until November 13 and overseas ballots until November 18, and its ranked-choice tabulation is scheduled for November 18.

The House count crept again in the evening, and the call desks end the day farther apart in arithmetic and in agreement on everything else. The Associated Press tracker’s latest state, as the Evening Watch printed it, shows 248 seats won by the Democrats and 13 more led, against 154 won and 20 led for the Republicans. The live results file’s check at 22:45 CET runs the called figure higher: 251 House seats called for the Democrats and 155 for the Republicans, with 29 still uncalled. Newsmax’s Thursday account runs it higher still, on a projection: the Associated Press projects the Democrats will win 252 seats, with 19 races still uncalled, 38 seats flipped, and the party’s highest House total since 2009; the Republicans, in the same account, flipped only one seat in a district unaffected by mid-decade redistricting, Maine’s 2nd. The three files were taken at different hours by different methods—seats won, seats called, seats projected—and the desk prints them as three bearings, not one number. The governorships stand at 28 for the Democrats with Alaska and Kansas open. Kansas stands where it stood: the Democrat, Cindy Holscher, ahead by 7,045 votes with all precincts reporting, the close-race audits ordered, the race uncalled.

Colorado, Corrected in the Open

Colorado’s 3rd District has changed hands since this paper last printed it whole, and the correction is the midnight file’s first duty. The Midnight Watch of November 5 and the editions that followed carried the Democrat, Dwayne Romero, leading the incumbent Republican, Jeff Hurd, by more than 2,200 votes, on the Colorado live file as it signed off on election night, when Romero did lead. The lead changed hands overnight, as the district’s remaining vote came in, and on Thursday it widened in the incumbent’s favor. The state results file, carrying the secretary of state’s figures and updated at 5:25 p.m. MST, puts Hurd at 162,669 votes, 49.31 percent, and Romero at 156,472, 47.43 percent—a margin of 6,197 votes. Decision Desk HQ’s file at 9:07 p.m. EST runs the same order slightly wider: Hurd 163,655, Romero 157,523, a margin of 6,132 in a count of 332,044 votes. The Associated Press district file carries the state’s figures and labels the race too early to call, at 96.73 percent of precincts reporting. The margin stands outside the half-percent threshold that would require a recount. The leader this paper printed on Wednesday was the leader of Wednesday’s count; the leader of Thursday’s count is Hurd, and the log records the change where it happened, in the open.

Colorado’s 4th District narrowed all day and ends it uncalled, with the incumbent’s strongest county finished counting. The state’s figures at 5:25 p.m. MST put the Democrat, Eileen Laubacher, at 184,024 votes and the incumbent Republican, Lauren Boebert, at 180,758—a margin of 3,266. Decision Desk HQ’s file at 9:56 p.m. EST puts the same pair at 184,624 and 181,555—a margin of 3,069, in a count of 378,685 votes, more than 95 percent of the expected vote. A later account of the state results, filed below, puts the lead at 2,376 votes and records the fact the remaining arithmetic turns on: Weld County, which gives Boebert her largest county lead, 20,074 votes to 13,572, has finished counting except for ballots with signature problems and ballots forwarded from other counties, and the ballots still out are concentrated in Douglas, Larimer, and Arapahoe counties, which Laubacher is carrying. The district’s own paper counts at least about 7,000 ballots remaining in the four most populous counties as of 5:30 p.m. and 474 ballots in Weld awaiting signature cures. Signature fixes and military and overseas ballots are due by 11:59 p.m. on November 12, and the counties finish tabulating on November 13. The Associated Press has not called the race.

After the Close: The Futures, Then Tokyo

The evening trade continued the day’s direction, at a slower speed, in two readings that do not match. The early-evening account, carrying CNBC’s figures, records S&P 500 futures and Nasdaq-100 futures each down 0.4 percent and Dow futures down 201 points, or 0.4 percent. The later account, in the same wire’s figures at a later hour, records S&P 500 futures down 0.8 percent, Nasdaq-100 futures down 1.3 percent, and Dow futures down 264 points, or 0.5 percent. Both accounts name the same subjects in front of the market: the October payrolls report, due Friday at 8:30 a.m. ET, and the concern, stated in the later account, that the Democratic victory could slow the pace of the data-center and artificial-intelligence buildout. No floor was reported in either account. The futures are a reading, not a close, and the desk files them as a reading.

By the hour Washington reached midnight, Tokyo was already in its Friday afternoon, and Friday there opened with the same selling, in larger size. The Nikkei 225, which had closed Thursday at 61,077.9 in the figures carried in the Tokyo–Seoul account filed below, gapped down at the open to 60,374.35 and fell 6.2 percent to 57,288.91. A second account of the Tokyo morning carries the fall deeper: an intraday low of 56,745.81, down 7 percent, and the index 5.7 percent lower at 57,579 at 11:18 a.m. in Tokyo. The index closed above 72,000 for the first time in late June; a fall of more than 20 percent from that record puts it in a bear market, and both accounts record it there. The names are the supply chain of the same buildout: Advantest down 12 percent, SoftBank Group down 11 percent, Kioxia down 11 percent, and Tokyo Electron down nearly 9 percent, in CNBC’s figures carried in the accounts. In Seoul the Kospi fell 8.55 percent to 4,923.47, below 5,000, and a Level 1 circuit breaker halted trading for twenty minutes; Samsung Electronics fell 10.07 percent to 181,200 won and SK Hynix fell 12.26 percent to 1,109,000 won. Taiwan’s Taiex fell more than 8 percent in its session before recovering part of the fall, and the Shanghai Composite stood down 1.1 percent and the Hang Seng down 0.60 percent in the Tokyo-morning snapshot. No new American fact arrived in the night to explain the Asian session. The explanation the accounts give is the one Thursday gave in New York: the election, read as a check on the buildout, priced a second time—this time on the other side of the Pacific, on the next day’s date.

Oil at Midnight: Brent Toward a Hundred Again

The settlement is fixed, and the evening trade carried Brent past it. Brent crude futures settled on Thursday up $3.67, or 3.88 percent, at $98.29 a barrel, and West Texas Intermediate closed up $2.40 at $90.52, per Reuters’ New York file as the Evening Watch printed it. In the hours after the settlement, Reuters’ early-Friday file records Brent futures for January up $1.57, or 1.6 percent, at $99.86 a barrel as of 0222 GMT, and West Texas Intermediate up $1.10 at $91.62; the Tokyo-morning account carries an evening-session high of $99.62. The same Reuters file holds the week’s other bearing in the same paragraph: Brent remains on track to fall about 12 percent for the week, after the December contract’s expiry on October 30, and the rebound of Wednesday and Thursday is recorded as a rebound on the surge in tanker attacks in the Gulf and the strait. Flows through the Strait of Hormuz, in that file’s figure, run under 60 percent of capacity.

The tanker file grew on Thursday, in claims, and the claims are filed as claims. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards, in their Operational Statement Number 21, said two tankers and a tugboat caught fire after using unauthorized routes through the strait—one tanker on a naval mine in the southern passage and a supertanker on fire in the eastern strait. Later on Thursday Iran’s regular navy reported three additional tanker incidents, vessels said to have struck naval mines in restricted waters east of the strait, per the account of the expanded claims filed below; the Iranian claims for Thursday thus stand at five tankers and a tugboat. Reuters’ Dubai file records the measured file beside them: at least five vessels have reported incidents since Sunday, according to the British maritime security agency UKMTO—about the pace of October, when the agency counted 35 vessels hit, the most of any month of the war—and a vessel was struck by two unidentified projectiles late on Wednesday, placed in the expanded account at about 50 nautical miles north of Madinat ash-Shamal in Qatar. No independent confirmation of the three additional Thursday incidents appears in any file the desk took in the evening. South Korea’s Defence Ministry said it will deploy maritime patrol aircraft and an explosive ordnance disposal team to waters near the strait, per the account of the strait’s week filed below, which records that South Korea sourced 70 percent of its oil from the Middle East in 2025 and that Iran has warned South Korea against the deployment. No agreement was announced on Thursday. The mediation stands where the midday and evening editions left it—Qatar’s efforts intensified, both governments reviewing proposals, Tehran’s conditions stated, per the mediation account—and Eurasia Group’s odds, carried at evening, stand unrevised at midnight: a deal at 10 percent, renewed American strikes at 60 percent, a continued standoff at 30 percent.

Washington at Midnight

The positions filed on Thursday stand unchanged at midnight, and the evening’s new paper is arithmetic. The president’s two halves—he will get along with the Democrats unless they want to be destructive, and he holds the power of the veto—stand as the Evening Watch printed them, with his answer on impeachment, “not even a little bit.” The incoming majority’s program stands as filed: affordability first, the Medicaid changes reversed, the lapsed health-insurance credits restored, and the funding bills used to limit the administration and the war. The evening account of what happens next, filed below, works the arithmetic both sides are working from: most legislation needs 60 votes to clear the Senate and then the president’s signature; overriding a veto takes two-thirds of both chambers, 67 votes in the Senate, which the majority does not hold; the House can impeach by a simple majority and the Senate can remove only by the same 67; and the power that changes hands whole on January 3 is the Senate’s power to confirm or refuse the president’s nominees. Senator Kirsten Gillibrand, on NPR’s Morning Edition on Thursday, stated the majority’s order in her own words, per the account of the interview filed below: lowering costs, restoring health care access, and ending the war in Iran; on the Medicaid restoration, “I dare him to veto that”; on impeachment, no commitment; and, on doing accountability and legislation together, “We can do both.”

The calendar the lame duck will run on is settled, and its first deadline is today. House Republicans’ candidates for their leadership elections must declare by today, Friday, November 6; the elections themselves are set for Tuesday, November 10, in both parties’ House conferences and in the Senate Republican conference, as the Evening Watch filed. Congress returns on Monday, November 9, for the lame-duck session. The government is funded through Friday, December 11, under the stopgap the president signed on September 2, Public Law 119-103, per the funding account filed below; twelve fiscal 2027 appropriations bills stand unfinished, the House having passed three and the Senate none in the accounting carried in the funding files, and the largest sticking point recorded is defense spending, against a request of about $1.5 trillion. Money for the war with Iran is recorded in the same files as the largest item on the outgoing majority’s wish list for the session. None of that business was transacted on Thursday. It is the work the log hands to the week that begins on Monday.
