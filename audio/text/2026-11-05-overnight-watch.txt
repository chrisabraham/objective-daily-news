The Night File — The Desk’s Report 
 
 The Night the Count Stood Still

At 0400 no new race had been called in the night. What the night produced instead was the calendar: the dates on which Mississippi and Alaska will finish, stated plainly; a narrower margin in Colorado’s 4th; a second Asian selling of the chip trade; oil testing $93; a draft agreement circulating in Doha with no signature on it; and the vice president’s full accounting of the defeat, printed where his party could read it.

The Night in Brief

No call moved in the night, in either chamber, in any state. The Senate stands where the midnight edition left it: 54 seats for the Democrats and 44 for the Republicans, with Alaska and Mississippi uncalled. The Associated Press House tracker in its latest state is also unchanged: 235 seats won by Democrats and 26 led, against 146 won and 28 led for Republicans, with 26 seats still too close to call. The governorships stand at 28 for the Democrats with Alaska and Kansas open. Control of no chamber and no majority is in question in any count on the shelf. The night’s work was not the result; it was the schedule on which the remainder of the result will arrive, and the readings that Asia, Tehran, and the Republican Party’s own vice president put on the result while Washington slept.

Two facts organize the night: the remaining counting is now dated—Mississippi certifies by November 13, Alaska tabulates on November 18—and the war is still unsettled, with a draft circulating in Doha, unverified claims of fresh tanker incidents in the Strait of Hormuz, and oil priced as if the war were ending anyway.

Mississippi: The Calendar, Stated Plainly

The overnight explainer, carrying the Associated Press’s account, corrects the procedure this paper printed on Wednesday, and the correction is filed first. Mississippi does not require a majority in a regular federal general election. The candidate with the most votes wins; there is no runoff. The December 1 runoff date carried in Editions Number 2, 4, and 5 of this paper does not apply to this race, and the desk withdraws it here, in the next edition in the log, which is where a log corrects itself. The count itself stands as the midnight edition left it in its latest figure: the Democrat, Scott Colom, at 464,926 votes, 49 percent, and the incumbent Republican, Cindy Hyde-Smith, at 463,788, 48.9 percent—a margin of 1,138 votes, with an estimated 96 percent of votes counted, per the account of why the two Senate races have not been called filed below. Al Jazeera’s account of the same delay carries the same margin and the same leader.

What remains, and by when: approximately 3,600 absentee-by-mail ballots still to be received within five business days, and an unknown number of affidavit ballots, per the November 4 statement of Michael Watson, the secretary of state, quoted in both accounts. A voter who cast an affidavit ballot for lack of acceptable identification has until November 10 to present it. Counties have until November 13 to submit certified results. Mississippi holds no automatic recounts; a candidate seeking one would have to obtain a court order, per the Associated Press. The wire calls a race when the trailing candidate has no path left to close the gap; here the path runs through several thousand ballots against a margin of 1,138, and it stays open until they are counted.

Alaska: Eleven Days of Mail, Then the Tabulation

Alaska’s Senate count did not move in the night; its procedure is also now fully stated. The Associated Press Senate file still shows 75.43 percent of precincts reporting, and the Democrat, Mary Peltola, still leads the incumbent Republican, Dan Sullivan—by 626 votes in the Associated Press figures carried in the overnight explainer, and by 637 in the same wire’s evening dashboard entry carried in the midnight edition. The desk prints both numbers; they are two states of one unfinished count. A second candidate named Daniel Sullivan holds 3,583 votes, more than either margin. Alaska accepts domestic mail ballots for up to 10 days after Election Day and overseas ballots for up to 15 days, provided they were mailed by November 3. If no candidate clears 50 percent of first choices—both leaders are below it—the ranked-choice tabulation is scheduled for November 18.

The legal file around the count, stated without weight on either side: the Justice Department sued to block certification if the incumbent loses; Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed the suit on November 1, ruling that the government had offered no workable plan to investigate ballots cast for the second Sullivan while preserving ballot secrecy; the department has said it will appeal. The governor, Mike Dunleavy, has ordered a state investigation and has suggested he may not certify a Peltola victory, per the Associated Press account filed below. Peltola has said her campaign had no involvement in the second Sullivan’s candidacy, and he has said he had no contact with her campaign about running. Each statement stands as its maker made it. In the governorship, the Democrat, Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, holds 49.2 percent of first choices with about 74 percent counted, the Republican Bernadette Wilson second at about 29 percent—the same November 18 schedule if no majority emerges.

Colorado and Kansas: The Counts That Moved

Colorado’s 4th District narrowed in the night, toward the incumbent. The Associated Press count in its latest state shows the Democrat, Eileen Laubacher, at 176,162 votes and the incumbent Republican, Lauren Boebert, at 173,649—a margin of 2,513 votes, with 92.64 percent of precincts reporting. The midnight edition carried the same file at 174,963 to 171,711, a margin of 3,252. The race remains too early to call. The account of the incumbent’s path filed below puts about 30,000 ballots outstanding, concentrated in Douglas and Larimer counties, and records her own figure from Wednesday evening: “We’re about a 5,000-vote deficit right now, a little bit less than that.” Colorado’s deadline for military and overseas ballots to arrive, and for voters to cure a missing or mismatched signature, is November 12.

Colorado’s 3rd District is reported two ways, and the desk prints both. The midnight edition, following the Colorado live file as it signed off for the night, carried the Democrat, Dwayne Romero, leading the Republican incumbent, Jeff Hurd, by more than 2,200 votes with 88 percent counted. The Rocky Mountain Voice file, in an update timed 8:22 a.m. Wednesday, reports Hurd ahead by 1,211 votes. The two files cannot both describe the same state of the count; they were taken at different hours from different tallies, and the race is uncalled in both. Which man leads the 3rd District at 0400 is a question the next counts will answer, and it is filed here as open.

Kansas’s governorship did not move in the night, and its audit is now general. The Associated Press Kansas file stands at 549,974 votes for the Democrat, Cindy Holscher, and 542,929 for the Republican, Ty Masterson—a margin of 7,045, with all precincts reporting, the race still too early to call. The secretary of state, Scott Schwab, has ordered close-race audits in six races that finished within one percent—governor, secretary of state, attorney general, and three state House districts—each a hand count of ballots in 10 percent of precincts, per the account of the audits filed below. Mail ballots postmarked by Election Day and received by Friday will be added. Holscher has declared victory; Masterson has not conceded; the governor, Laura Kelly, has congratulated Holscher as governor-elect. All three statements stand, and the count proceeds underneath them.

Asia: The Second Selling of the AI Trade

Asia’s Thursday session repeated Wednesday’s pattern in sharper form: the chip trade sold, and nearly everything else rose. Reuters’ Singapore file reports oil, chip stocks, and the dollar under pressure and broad indexes higher, on the same reasoning the American session used: a clear electoral outcome, and the expectation—an expectation, not an agreement—that the war with Iran will end sooner under a Democratic Congress. Brent crude futures fell nearly 9 percent overnight, below $100 a barrel, and stand about 20 percent lower on the week; if the fall holds through Friday’s close it is the largest weekly drop since April 2020, when American crude briefly traded below zero. In the Asia morning Brent was testing its lows at $93.50; a Dow Jones account carried front-month Brent at $93.85, down 0.8 percent on the day in Asia, and West Texas Intermediate at $86.93, down 1.4 percent. The dollar fell to a one-month low of $1.1273 per euro. The ten-year Treasury yield fell 6 basis points overnight and a further point in early Tokyo trade, to 4.87 percent.

The selling sat in the same names, and the percentages differ by the hour each file was taken. Reuters’ morning figures: the KOSPI down 4 percent and near a seven-month low, Taiwan down 1.8 percent, the Nikkei down 0.5 percent. The Asia-Pacific market file filed below, taken later in the session, carries the KOSPI down about 4.8 percent after a sidecar—a five-minute halt to program trading—was triggered, the Nikkei down about 1.5 percent while the broader Topix rose about 1.4 percent, Shanghai up about 3.6 percent, and the Hang Seng up about 3 percent. SoftBank fell about 5.8 percent, on the slide in its chip designer Arm and a Financial Times report that Chinese regulators will not allow its $5.4 billion acquisition of ABB’s robotics division. Solar shares rose the most in the Reuters account; data-centre companies and their suppliers fell across Asia. In India, the Nifty 50 rose more than 2 percent to 24,442.20 and the Sensex more than 2 percent to 79,001.19, on the fall in crude. Gold extended Wednesday’s rise of about 3 percent to about $4,135 an ounce. A Melbourne strategist quoted by Reuters states the markets’ position in one sentence: “The magnitude of the victory is what has caught markets off guard.”

The Strait and the Draft

No agreement was announced in the night, and the two public accounts of the talks do not describe the same distance. On one bearing, a draft is close: reports carried in the Indian market coverage state that the United States has approved a draft understanding passed to Iran through Qatari mediators, and the account of the Qatar-presented draft filed below reports that Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates were briefed and approved it, that concessions cover Iran’s nuclear program and its frozen assets, and that the timing of simultaneous announcements is under discussion. On the other bearing, the distance stands: a senior Iranian official told Reuters the two sides remain far from a deal, that Tehran’s conditions must be met if the United States wants the Strait of Hormuz reopened, and that feedback on the mediators’ latest suggestion was expected from both capitals within 36 hours; the Qatari team has returned to Doha and the talks continue through the mediators, per the Reuters account of the diplomatic push filed below. Iran’s stated basis remains its seven-step plan of September: an end to the American naval blockade, a waiver on oil sales and cancellation of new sanctions, reopening of the strait—which Tehran says it can now do within two days of an agreement—and talks on the remaining issues, including the nuclear program, to follow. The secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, Mohsen Rezaei, has said the American reply was not convincing and all seven conditions must be answered clearly. From Washington there is no agreement and one sentence of posture: the president has said the United States has no immediate plans for military action.

Two further facts stand beside the talks, each attributed. First, Tehran is on high alert: two senior Iranian sources told Reuters that while Iranian leaders welcomed the Democrats’ victory, they were concerned the president could seek to compensate for it with a military strike, per Reuters’ account of the Iranian response filed below. Second, the strait itself produced claims in the night that the desk cannot verify and prints as claims: Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said two tankers and a tugboat had caught fire in the strait and that a tanker using an unapproved route had struck a mine, per the Asia-Pacific market file; a separate report carried the Guards’ navy saying three oil tankers hit mines on Tuesday. No second source on the shelf confirms either incident. Oil fell through both claims, which is a fact about the market’s reading, not about the strait.

The Vice President’s Accounting

The fullest Republican document of the night is the vice president’s, and it was published where his own party reads. Le Monde’s overnight analysis carries the post in substance. JD Vance wrote: “The reason we lost is the war. The president himself said so last night. If you’d like to argue it was worth it, that losing the Senate for the next 6 years was worth it, then do so.” And: “To the people who are pissed off and want to say, ‘I told you so’: you’re right, you did tell us so.” The post’s analysis of the vote is that the loss was not a turnout failure: many people who voted for the Trump–Vance ticket in 2024 voted for Democrats in 2026. Its four lessons are ending the war in Iran, focusing on the cost of living, adhering to the America First doctrine, and reassessing the relationship with Israel. On immigration enforcement, the vice president wrote that he is open to changing the approach, for instance by focusing more on businesses that hire illegal labor.

The rest of the party’s argument ran in the same hours, in the open. The president, asked at the White House ballroom construction site about the vice president’s assessment, answered: “Well, I think it’s largely true because it drove the gasoline up a little bit, short term, and probably that’s true, but at the same time, if I had it to do again, I’d have to do it again because, long term, it’s the only answer. You can’t let Iran have a nuclear weapon.” Florida’s governor, Ron DeSantis, who leaves office in January, described the midterms as “close to an extinction-level event for Republicans.” The commentator Rod Dreher, long close to the vice president, wrote that the president should resign and let the vice president run things for the next two years, and called him a spent force; the commentator Matt Walsh called him, on Tuesday evening, “yesterday’s news.” The Speaker, Mike Johnson, announced Wednesday morning he will not seek the leadership of the Republican minority in January. Former Ambassador Nikki Haley stated the contrary lesson: “When we stay close to our policies, we win: economic freedom, lower taxes, smaller government, making sure we get families back to work, national security.” Nothing in the argument was settled overnight; the 2028 succession is now an open question inside a party that still holds the White House, the veto, and the command of the armed forces.

The World’s Morning Papers

Europe’s second-day readings divide between welcome, caution, and the judgment that nothing structural has changed. The roundup of the world leaders’ reactions filed below carries them in sequence. Germany’s defense minister, Boris Pistorius, congratulated the Democrats on a historic victory and stated that Europe must continue assuming greater responsibility for its own security. France’s economy and finance minister, Roland Lescure, called the gains undeniable and cautioned that the president retains significant authority over American foreign policy. Italy’s deputy prime minister and foreign minister, Antonio Tajani, said the results would not alter relations between Rome and Washington. Poland’s prime minister, Donald Tusk, welcomed the victory as a positive development for American democracy; the Czech prime minister, Andrej Babi&scaron;, called it expected but exceptionally large. The co-leader of Germany’s AfD, Alice Weidel, stated the reading from the other side of her country’s politics: the president had promised his voters no interventionist foreign policy, and the broken promise had come back to haunt the Republicans. Britain’s prime minister, Andy Burnham, stated the cost-of-living reading in general form: “The world now has an opportunity to unite around an issue that affects us all, and that’s the cost of living.” Belgium’s defense minister, Theo Franken, stated the governing reading: with so many internal divisions, it will be nearly impossible to govern in the United States over the next few years.

The other capitals held the positions the earlier watches recorded. China’s foreign ministry spokeswoman, Mao Ning, described the election as a domestic matter that will not change Beijing’s position. The Kremlin spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, expressed hope that the president would continue pursuing diplomatic solutions; the deputy foreign minister, Sergei Ryabkov, counseled waiting for the final results. In Tehran, the newspaper Hamshahri printed the anniversary of the 1979 embassy seizure beside the election under the headline “Iran’s shadow over US elections,” with an image of the president blindfolded and his head bowed. In London, the Guardian’s readers divided between enthusiasm and the repeated caution that Washington may return to business as usual. Each capital’s reading is printed as that capital made it; the disagreement is itself the record of the night abroad.

The Cocked Hat

Three bearings never give a point; they give a small triangle, and this paper prints the triangle. First, Colorado’s 3rd District: one Colorado file carries the Democrat ahead by more than 2,200 votes as its night closed, another carries the Republican ahead by 1,211 in a Wednesday-morning update—the leader itself in dispute until the next counts. Second, the Asian percentages: the KOSPI down 4 percent in the morning file and about 4.8 percent in the later one, the Nikkei down 0.5 and about 1.5, Brent at $93.50, $93.85, and about $97 in three files taken at different hours of one falling market—a disagreement of clocks, printed because a reader checking any one file against this page will find the other numbers. Third, the talks in Doha: one set of reports has a draft approved in Washington and welcomed in two Gulf capitals, and the named Iranian officials on the record say the American reply is unconvincing and the two sides far apart. Both bearings are printed above; an agreement exists when both capitals announce one, and neither has. Against the triangle, the load-bearing facts of the night are single-valued: no race was called; Mississippi’s calendar runs to November 10 and 13 with no runoff in the procedure; Alaska’s runs to November 18; Colorado’s 4th narrowed to 2,513 votes; oil fell below $100 and tested $93; and the vice president’s sentence—“The reason we lost is the war”—stands unretracted, with the president’s answer beside it. The log proceeds on the clerks’ schedules now, not on Washington’s. The next count belongs to the states.
