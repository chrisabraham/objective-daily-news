 The Day Whole: The Count Holds, the Markets Close Higher, and Every Side Has Spoken

The day’s developments whole at 1800: the count stands where the morning left it, the Dow closes up 1,569 points, oil falls near nine percent, the president spends his afternoon at a construction site, the vice president names the war as the reason, and the incoming majority states its program and its limit.

The Count at Evening

The top of the count did not move all day, and no source on the shelf disputes it. Democrats hold the Senate and the House. The Senate stands at 54 seats for the Democrats and 44 for the Republicans, with two races uncalled, in Alaska and Mississippi. The seven flipped Senate seats stand: Texas, North Carolina, South Carolina, Ohio, Iowa, Kansas, and Maine. In the House, the Associated Press tracker in its evening state shows 232 seats called for the Democrats and 29 more led, against 138 called and 36 led for the Republicans. The noon edition carried the same tracker at 232 called and 30 led for the Democrats: one lead changed hands during the afternoon, and the called totals did not move. The final size of the House majority is the remaining arithmetic question; the projections in circulation run from 251 to 268 Democratic seats—a disagreement about uncalled western districts, not about control.

The governors’ map finished its movement. Nevada’s race, uncalled at noon, has been called for the Democrat, Aaron Ford, the fifth governorship to change parties, after Georgia, Iowa, Ohio, and Texas. The Associated Press governors’ file in its evening state lists two races still open, Alaska and Kansas. The standing tally is 28 governorships for the Democrats and 20 for the Republicans, a Democratic majority of the offices for the first time in 16 years, per the account of the governors’ races filed below. In Kansas, the Democrat, Cindy Holscher, leads the Republican, Ty Masterson, by about 5,500 votes, 50.3 percent to 49.7, in the NBC count; she has declared victory, and the margin inside one percent requires an automatic audit of ten percent of precincts under Kansas law. In Alaska, the Democrat, Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, led the first-choice count Wednesday morning; the ranked-choice tabulation is scheduled for November 18.

The Open Races at Evening

Alaska’s Senate race is where the noon edition left it. The Associated Press count still shows the Democrat, Mary Peltola, at 102,701 votes and the incumbent Republican, Dan Sullivan, at 102,075, a lead of 626 votes, with 74.19 percent of precincts reporting. A second candidate named Daniel Sullivan holds 3,583 votes. Newsmax’s account records the procedure ahead: absentee ballots arrive for up to 15 days, the ranked-choice tabulation follows, and the race could remain unresolved until near Thanksgiving. The Justice Department has appealed a lawsuit seeking an investigation of the result if the incumbent loses narrowly enough that the namesake candidate made the difference, per USA Today’s live file.

Mississippi’s Senate margin is still counted three ways. The Associated Press count carried at noon—462,857 votes for the Democrat, Scott Colom, against 462,792 for the incumbent Republican, Cindy Hyde-Smith, a margin of 65 votes, with 99.83 percent of precincts reporting—stands in the wire’s evening table. Other counts put the margin at 43 votes, and The Hill, citing Decision Desk HQ, reported 2,169. Neither candidate has declared victory. Mail and provisional ballots are still being processed, and if neither candidate clears 50 percent the seat is decided in a runoff on December 1.

Colorado’s 4th District moved a little, toward the challenger. The Associated Press count in its evening state shows the Democrat, Eileen Laubacher, at 169,066 votes and the incumbent Republican, Lauren Boebert, at 163,993, a margin of 5,073 votes, with 83.14 percent of precincts reporting. At noon the same file read 168,729 to 163,835. The race remains too early to call. The new Congress convenes on January 3, 2027; until then the counting continues on the clerks’ schedules, not on Washington’s.

The Markets at the Close

The stock market closed higher, and by a record margin for the day after a midterm. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed up 1,569.40 points, 3.03 percent, at 53,356.12. The S&P 500 closed up 1.58 percent at 7,688.62, its best day in three months. The Nasdaq Composite closed up 1.44 percent at 25,736.51. The figures agree across the closing accounts filed below, including the closing report that records Amazon up 6 percent and the memory shares sold off, and the account that states the comparison the day will be filed under: the Dow’s strongest post-midterm performance in 44 years. The reasons traders gave did not change during the day: the election ended the uncertainty, the president accepted the results, and divided government limits the range of policy either side can enact. The exception named at noon remained the exception at the close: shares tied to data-center construction weakened.

Oil fell all afternoon, and the day’s figures do not reconcile into one price, because they were taken at different hours of a falling market. The morning file had Brent up 0.7 percent at $104.36 a barrel. The midday report carried Brent down 3.09 percent at $100.40. By early afternoon, OilPrice’s report, timed at 1306 ET, had Brent down $8.94, 8.6 percent, at $94.66, its lowest since September 4, and West Texas Intermediate down $8.01, 8.3 percent, at $88.08. The Associated Press figures carried by TT News put Brent down 9.2 percent at $94.11. The desk prints the range: Brent fell from above $104 in the morning to the neighborhood of $94 in the afternoon, about nine percent on the day, on the same reasoning in every account—that the election, and the administration’s own statements tying the loss to the war, raised the expectation of an end to the conflict in Iran. No agreement has been announced. The war continues. The price records an expectation, stated here as one.

The President in the Afternoon

The president’s first public appearance of the day was at a construction site. He visited the White House ballroom site to mark the installation of its final beam, spoke to the construction workers, and signed the beam with a marker, adding the word “Love!” USA Today’s account records his words on the project: “To me, it’s relaxing.” The New York Post’s account records that he spoke for 25 minutes about his construction projects before taking questions about the election.

His answers are the administration’s considered position at evening. Asked whether he regretted his campaign strategy, he said he did not: “No, look, if I were on the ballot we would have won.” Of Vice President Vance’s statement that the war was the reason for the loss, he said, “I think it’s largely true because it drove the gasoline up a little bit short term,” and then stated his own position in full: “But at the same time, if I had it to do again, I’d have to do it again because long term, it’s the only answer. You can’t let Iran have a nuclear weapon.” Of the Congress that convenes on January 3, he predicted, “I’ll get along probably with the Democrats unless they want to be destructive.” He denied speculation that he will remove the Secretary of War, Pete Hegseth. The exit polls printed with the Post’s account found 71 percent of voters saying Congress must do more to check the president and 62 percent blaming him personally for higher prices. Both records stand: the numbers, and the president’s answers to them.

The Vice President’s Accounting, and the Party’s

The fullest Republican account of the defeat came from the vice president, in a long post on X. Its central sentence is short: “The reason we lost is the war. The president himself said so last night.” The post states that the problem was not turnout—“Our voters showed up”—but conversion: many who voted for the Republican ticket in 2024 voted for Democrats in 2026. It lists the four answers those voters gave: end the war; focus on the cost of living and inflation; put America First; change the country’s relationship to Israel. To the war’s defenders: “If you’d like to argue it was worth it, that losing the Senate for the next 6 years was worth it, then do so.” To those who warned against the conflict: “You were right, you did tell us so.” The Daily Caller’s account and the wire account carried by the Rapid City Post agree on the wording. The difference inside the administration’s own record is the day’s clearest disagreement between allies: the president called the war a piece of the result; the vice president called it the reason.

The party’s wider argument ran along the same lines, in public, all day. Fox News’ roundup collects the responses: a columnist calling the night “a catastrophic night for Republicans”; a radio host writing, “Sometimes you get your a— kicked, and deserve it”; a commentator writing that the president “launched an unpopular and pointless war. Gas prices are too high. People can’t afford groceries”; and a second host listing causes beyond the war—corruption, tariffs, deportations, the redistricting push, the ballroom. The Washington Examiner’s account records the other side: a White House official said candidate quality was the failure, and a former deputy press secretary answered that voters took the president at his word when he told them to vote as if he were on the ballot. In Texas, a Republican lobbyist told the BBC, in the account filed below: “It’s on him. It’s a total, complete rejection of President Trump.” One institutional fact belongs here: the Speaker, Mike Johnson, told his staff Wednesday morning that he will not run for minority leader, per the USA Today live file.

The Incoming Majority at Evening

The incoming majority spent the day stating its program and its order of business. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries, in line to become the first Black Speaker, stated the program in one sentence: “We ran on affordability, we won decisively on affordability, and now we have a responsibility to govern on affordability.” Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer pledged both halves of the mandate the party claims—lower prices, and investigations where the party believes the administration has violated the law. The Wall Street Journal’s accounting lists the items: reversal of parts of the president’s tax-and-spending law, including the Medicaid work requirements and the reductions in food assistance; revival of the expanded health-insurance subsidies; action on tariffs and on the war, in Schumer’s words on television, a focus on “gasoline and diesel by ending this damn war, a useless, stupid war, as quickly as possible”; oversight with subpoena power; and hearings on artificial intelligence. Newsmax’s account frames the difficulty the majority itself names: delivering on the cost of living while pursuing the investigations its voters also elected it to pursue.

On impeachment, the day produced two statements that do not agree, and the desk prints both. Jeffries told reporters that impeaching the president is not a priority: “I haven’t had a single member of the House Democratic Caucus talk to me about impeachment,” and, “We’ve been relentlessly focused on affordability and accountability.” The first named official in the accountability file is not the president but the Secretary of War: the incoming chairman of the House Armed Services Committee, Adam Smith, has pledged to investigate Hegseth, the conduct of the Iran war, and the removal of senior military officers, per the USA Today live file, which also records a Republican senator, Thom Tillis, saying, “Pete Hegseth has a reckoning.” Senator Ted Cruz, speaking on an election-night program, predicted the opposite of Jeffries’ statement: impeachments of the president and of Cabinet members, beginning with a push against Hegseth in the lame duck, and “all-out partisan war.” The account of Cruz’s remarks is filed below. The president denied that he will remove Hegseth.

The World at Evening

By evening the foreign responses had sorted themselves into positions. Reuters’ report on the European parties that had stood closest to the president records them moving away from him during the day, naming the same cause the vice president named: the war in Iran and the cost of living. Germany’s Alice Weidel said the president had promised his voters not to pursue an interventionist foreign policy and that “this broken promise has now come back to haunt the Republicans.” Italy’s Matteo Salvini attributed the result above all to wars, international tensions, and the increase in the cost of living. Reuters’ survey of the result’s effects abroad records the Gulf governments’ reading, from two sources familiar with their thinking: the president now has more reason to seek a peace agreement, and another full-scale assault on Iran is less likely. Together the statements establish the day’s last point: the same two causes—the war and the price of living—are named in Washington by the vice president, in Paris and Berlin by the president’s former allies, and in Tehran by the parliament speaker who read the vote as a no to war. On that much, at 1800, every side agrees, and agrees on nothing else.
