 The Count at Noon: Alaska Turns, Mississippi Narrows, Markets Rise

The midday state of the 2026 midterms: Alaska’s Senate lead changes hands, Mississippi’s margin is counted three ways, the House count firms up, markets rise, and the official statements arrive.

The Noon Count

Control of both chambers is unchanged, and no source disputes it. Democrats hold the House and the Senate. The Senate tally stands where the morning left it: 54 seats for the Democrats and 44 for the Republicans in the counts carried by NBC News and The Guardian, with two races still uncalled, in Alaska and Mississippi. The morning’s seven flipped Senate seats stand: Texas, North Carolina, Ohio, Iowa, South Carolina, Maine, and Kansas. Ballotpedia’s running file added two routine holds during the morning, Alabama’s Senate seat for the Republicans at 1121 and Colorado’s for the Democrats at 1125. Neither changed the balance.

The House count firmed up, and the trackers do not agree on the firm number. The Associated Press balance-of-power tracker at midday showed 232 House seats called for the Democrats and 30 more led, against 138 called and 35 led for the Republicans. The morning edition carried the same tracker at 222 called and 41 led for the Democrats: ten leads have become calls, and the Republican called figure has not moved. Other trackers, updated at other hours, run lower: The Guardian’s count showed 228 Democratic seats and 139 Republican, and Ballotpedia’s file showed a smaller called total with 68 House races still uncalled. The desk prints the spread rather than a single figure: the called Democratic total at noon stands between 228 and 232 depending on the tracker, the uncalled total between 65 and 68, and every tracker’s called-and-leading arithmetic ends in a Democratic majority in the mid-250s, where NBC News placed its projection on Wednesday morning, at no fewer than 258 seats. The disagreement is over the pace of the calls, not over the outcome.

The county evidence widened. Reuters’ seven-numbers accounting, compiled with USA Today, reported that Republicans lost ground in 99 percent of the roughly 2,100 counties where most votes had been counted by Wednesday morning, their share of the vote down nine points against the president’s 2024 share, and down 14 points in the 64 majority-Hispanic counties with Senate contests. Of the 15 Democratic-held seats the Republican redistricting effort targeted, none has changed hands: Democrats have won nine and six remain undecided.

Alaska and Mississippi: The Cocked Hat

Alaska’s lead has changed hands since the morning edition, and the morning figure is corrected here. Edition Number 1 reported the incumbent Republican, Dan Sullivan, ahead of the Democrat, Mary Peltola, by roughly 2,400 votes in the count CBS News was reporting. The Associated Press count at midday runs the other way: Peltola 102,701 votes, Sullivan 102,075, a lead of 626 votes for the Democrat, with 74.19 percent of precincts reporting. CNN and NBC, in the account carried by the Agence France-Presse file, put the same race at 48.2 percent to 47.9 percent, Peltola ahead, at 74 percent counted. An earlier Fox News count had Sullivan ahead, 48.5 to 47.7, at about 72 percent counted. The three bearings were taken at three different hours of a count still in motion; they do not reconcile into one figure, and the desk does not force them. Together they establish a narrow point: the race is uncalled, the lead is measured in hundreds of votes, and it has already changed hands once today. A second candidate named Daniel Sullivan holds 3,583 votes in the AP count, more than five times the margin. Alaska counts absentee ballots for days and begins its ranked-choice tabulation on November 18; a final result may be weeks away.

Mississippi’s margin is counted three ways, and the desk publishes all three. The Associated Press count at midday showed the Democrat, Scott Colom, at 462,857 votes and the incumbent Republican, Cindy Hyde-Smith, at 462,792: a margin of 65 votes, with 99.83 percent of precincts reporting and an estimated 95.59 percent of the vote in. The morning edition carried a margin of 43 votes from the same wire at an earlier hour, and other counts in circulation put the figure at 43 as well; The Hill, citing Decision Desk HQ, reported Colom ahead by 2,169 votes, 461,370 to 459,201. The totals underneath the three margins differ: the counts are different snapshots of the same unfinished tally. No candidate has claimed victory. Mississippi requires an outright majority; if neither candidate clears 50 percent, the seat is decided in a runoff on December 1. Thousands of eligibility-check ballots remain uncounted, per local reporting in the AFP file. The honest statement at noon is the triangle itself: Colom leads in every count the desk has seen, by somewhere between 43 and 2,169 votes, and the race is too early to call.

Colorado’s 4th District did not move. The AP count still shows the Democrat, Eileen Laubacher, at 168,729 votes and the incumbent Republican, Lauren Boebert, at 163,835, 49.2 percent to 47.7, with 82.98 percent of precincts reporting. The race remains too early to call. In the governors’ races, Fox News’ state-of-play file records Nevada still uncalled and the Alaska and Kansas governorships still counting. The new Congress convenes on January 3, 2027; until then the present Congress sits, and the counting continues.

The Markets’ Reading

The markets read the result as certainty, and rose. Stock futures fell first: in the overnight hours, Nasdaq-100 futures dropped as much as 2.4 percent and S&P 500 futures 1.4 percent, on concern, as Reuters reported, that a Democratic Congress would bring closer scrutiny of artificial intelligence spending and data-center expansion. The futures reversed after the president accepted the election results, and by the opening bell the averages were climbing. Reports of the morning session differ by the hour they were filed: the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose more than 1,100 points at the open, about 2.3 percent, per Reuters figures carried by northjersey.com; USA Today’s account put the Dow up 2.1 percent at the open, the S&P 500 up 1.8 percent, and the Nasdaq Composite up 2.5 percent. CNN’s market file reported the Dow extending its gain toward 1,500 points, or 2.9 percent, in the course of the morning, a pace CNN, citing CFRA Research, described as the index’s largest single-day gain after a midterm election since 1982, and reported the VIX, the market’s measure of expected volatility, down almost 20 percent. Bond yields fell as prices rose. The explanation traders gave was consistent across the accounts: the election ended uncertainty, a divided government limits the range of policy either side can enact, and the president’s acceptance of the results lowered the odds of a prolonged contest over them. Morningstar’s strategist Dan Lefkovitz, quoted by USA Today: the results “have put an end to uncertainty, which is generally welcomed by markets.” Chip stocks were the exception: Nvidia and Micron fell, and data-center shares weakened, while Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Apple, and Microsoft rose.

Oil fell on the same reading, applied to the war. Reuters’ oil report had Brent futures down $3.20 a barrel, 3.09 percent, at $100.40 in volatile trading, with West Texas Intermediate down $3.01 at $93.08, as traders weighed what a Democratic Congress means for the war with Iran. The president told the Financial Times on Tuesday that the United States had no immediate plans to strike Iran, and he acknowledged that the war played a role in his party’s defeat. Vice President JD Vance stated the administration’s own accounting on X, in the words CNN’s market file quotes: “The reason we lost is the war. The president himself said so last night.” A senior Iranian official told Reuters on Tuesday that Iran and the United States remain far from a deal to end the war, and Qatar has stepped up its mediation. The price move records an expectation, not an event: no agreement has been announced, and the war continues.

The Official Statements

The White House answered with a reminder and a photograph. On Wednesday morning the White House account on X posted a photograph of the president with five words in large type: “PSA: TRUMP IS STILL YOUR PRESIDENT,” captioned, “Thank you for your attention to this matter.” The communications director, Steven Cheung, posted the same point in sentence form: “Donald J. Trump is the President of the United States. Still your President!” The Wall Street Journal’s live file records the instrument behind the reminder: on election night the president posted a photograph of a red cap reading “VETO,” and the Journal notes that overriding a veto takes two-thirds of both chambers, a threshold neither party’s numbers approach. In a late-night call with CBS News, the president congratulated the Democrats and said he looked forward to working with them, and he repeated the veto point. A White House official told Fox News the mood inside was defiant, and the administration’s position, as reported, is that the losses belong to the candidates who ran.

The incoming majority answered with its program and its limit. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer, at a Washington news conference on Wednesday, called the result “a generational remake of the Senate map that is going to change America,” and said, “Americans voted for change, and now Democrats must deliver.” Newsmax’s account of the election-night remarks records Schumer’s shorter version: Democrats had won the Senate “against all odds,” and “Relief is on the way.” House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries, who is positioned to become the first Black Speaker of the House, posted “Victory for America!” and stated the constitutional point as he sees it: “You will have a Congress that recognizes we don’t work for the president.” The Associated Press report records what the new majority says it will do with the gavels in January: investigations of administration officials and of the president’s family business dealings, and legislation on the cost of living. The president holds the veto and says so; the incoming majority holds the committees and says so; and the counting in Alaska and Mississippi goes on underneath both.

Abroad at Noon

Foreign governments read the result through their own files, and said so in their own words. Reuters’ roundup of the reactions records Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez describing the Democratic victory as part of the unravelling of the global far right, and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Stoere calling it “a powerful political signal about a change of course among American voters.” South African President Cyril Ramaphosa told reporters the result would “introduce a new era in world relations.” Russia’s deputy foreign minister, Sergey Ryabkov, advised waiting for the final results and noted that the Democratic leadership’s approach to Moscow is sharply negative; Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Moscow hoped the results would not affect the president’s peacemaking efforts. From Tehran, the Al Jazeera account records two statements that do not agree: Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said the voters had rejected conflict, while Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei said a change of party in Washington would not alter American hostility toward Iran. European Parliament Vice President Christel Schaldemose said she was cautiously optimistic for Europe; France’s finance minister, Roland Lescure, said Paris would wait to see whether the president, who retains control of foreign policy, adjusts his approach. Le Monde’s visual accounting reaches the structural point the foreign statements circle around: the Democrats did not win the two-thirds in either chamber that overriding a veto requires, and the president remains president. That is the whole of the midday record: a count still moving at its edges, a market that has placed its reading, two governments-in-waiting describing the same January from opposite ends, and two Senate races that will be settled by clerks, not statements.
